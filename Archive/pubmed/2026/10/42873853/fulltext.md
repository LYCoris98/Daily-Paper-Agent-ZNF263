# A transcriptional cofactor GRIP1 drives PPARγ DNA binding and p300-dependent enhancer activity during homeostatic macrophage polarization


## Abstract
  Homeostatic macrophages maintain tissue health and resolve inflammation. Upon sustained exposure to type II cytokine IL4, transcription factors STAT6, KLF4, EGR2 and a ligand-regulated nuclear receptor PPARγ orchestrate the M2IL4 ‘polarization’, marked by enhancer RNA (eRNA) production near multiple M2 genes. The mechanisms of enhancer activation by unliganded PPARγ are poorly understood. We interrogated cofactor requirements for PPARγ-driven gene expression in M2IL4, including those lacking PPARγ or a ligand-dependent nuclear receptor coregulator NCoA2/GRIP1 vs. macrophages treated with the PPARγ agonist rosiglitazone. As expected, rosiglitazone activated conventional PPARγ targets; GRIP1 deletion blunted their induction and depleted GRIP1-interacting histone acetyltransferase p300 from PPARγ-bound sites. Surprisingly, GRIP1 colocalized with unliganded PPARγ in M0 and M2IL4, and GRIP1 ablation dramatically reduced genome-wide DNA binding of PPARγ itself while promoting broad deposition of CTCF insulator. Functionally, GRIP1 deletion mirrored PPARγ deficiency, establishing GRIP1 as a central regulator of unliganded PPARγ DNA binding, required for both eRNA and mRNA transcription of PPARγ targets in homeostatic macrophages.


## INTRODUCTION
  Macrophages are plastic cells of the innate immune system, responsive to the myriad stimuli in their environment, which program them to occupy distinct states along a spectrum of phenotypes. At the opposite poles of the spectrum are the inflammatory (‘M1’-like) state induced by pro-inflammatory stimuli, such as LPS and IFN-γ, and the homeostatic (‘M2’-like) state enacted by long-term exposure to the Th2 cell-derived IL4 cytokine, or by glucocorticoids (GCs) secreted by the adrenal cortex (1–6). M2 macrophages are involved in maintaining tissue health, engulfing apoptotic cells, and play a role during the pro-resolving phase of acute inflammation (7, 8). In the context of wound healing, homeostatic macrophages promote tissue repair and remodeling during the resolving phase (9, 10). M2-like macrophages associated with tumors (tumor-associated macrophages, TAMs) stimulate angiogenesis, metastasis and suppress anti-tumor immune response, thus contributing to tumorigenesis (11).

  Several transcription factors have been implicated in promoting the M2-like homeostatic state downstream of different stimuli and at different steps of the polarization process. In response to IL4, the IL4 receptor signals to STAT6 leading to sweeping activation of STAT6 targets, e.g., Arg1, Egr2 and Mrc1 (12–14). Recent in vitro study proposed STAT6 binding to be transient, implicating another transcription factor, EGR2, at later stages of M2 polarization (15). EGR1 is another marker of both the M1-like and the M2-like states (16). KLF4 – a transcriptional target of STAT6 – is similarly upregulated upon IL4 signaling and has been described as essential for establishing the M2-like macrophage state in vitro and in vivo, and upregulating M2 genes such as Arg1, Retnla, Chil3, Pparg (17, 18).

  Endogenous and synthetic GCs, a distinct stimulus that biases macrophages toward the homeostatic phenotype, signal through the glucocorticoid receptor (GR)–a member of the nuclear receptor (NR) superfamily, which both suppresses the M1-like programming by antagonizing actions of pro-inflammatory transcription factors, e.g., NFkB, and directly activates many of the key M2 genes including CD206, CD163, Mertk, Klf9 and Klf4, among others (19, 20). We have previously described a significant transcriptomic and epigenomic overlap between macrophages polarized with IL4 vs. GCs, with KLF4 and GR colocalizing at many sites associated with M2 genes genome-wide and utilizing a shared transcriptional cofactor – NcoA2/GRIP1 (20) originally described as an NR coactivator of the p160 family (21, 22). Indeed, upregulation of many classic M2 ‘marker’ genes including Chil3, Chil4, Retnla, and Mrc1 during IL4- and/or GC-driven polarization was attenuated in GRIP1-deficient macrophages (20). Cooperativity between the two pathways has also been proposed to occur at the level of GR and STAT6 in vitro, whereby GR recruitment promotes STAT6 binding and synergistic gene activation by IL4 and GCs near select M2 genes (23).

  Another NR–peroxisomal proliferator activated receptor-gamma (PPARγ)–has been linked to the homeostatic macrophage phenotype. As a ‘type II NR’, PPARγ is activated by ligands (such as natural fatty acids or synthetic compounds, e.g., thiazolidinediones) that promote the dismissal of corepressors NCoR/SMRT from PPARγ/RXR heterodimers and recruitment of p160 coactivators, thus facilitating loading of histone acetyltransferases (HATs) CBP/p300 to target PPARγ-bound enhancers (24–27). PPARγ is a master regulator of lipid metabolism which facilitates adipogenesis and adipocyte differentiation and also exerts systemic insulin sensitizing effects (28–30). In macrophages, PPARγ, like GR, is known to counteract inflammatory pathways (31–33). In addition, PPARγ is upregulated by IL4 and facilitates M2 polarization in vitro and in vivo upon ligand activation (34). More recently, PPARγ has been reported to function downstream of EGR2 to enact the delayed long-lasting epigenomic changes necessary for establishing the M2 state in response to IL4, notably, even in the absence of added ligand (12, 15). A subset of such new IL4-dependent RXR/PPARγ-bound sites were marked by recruitment of p300 and RAD21, cofactors involved in chromatin reorganization.

  Upon binding to regulatory regions associated with specific genes, transcription factor complexes induce the expression of enhancer RNA (eRNA), often viewed as a proxy of the associated protein-coding gene activity. In some cases, eRNAs have been postulated to play a regulatory role by, for example, providing the interface for protein complex assembly, although this notion continues to be debated (35–39). eRNA has also been proposed to facilitate RNA polymerase II pause-release enabling productive elongation (40, 41) or, at low levels, assist transcriptional condensate formation in a sequence-independent manner (42). eRNA transcription, in turn, was recently proposed to be controlled by a shared checkpoint mechanism that releases the KAP1 repressor from the P-TEFb kinase–a gate-keeper of RNA polymerase II pause-release (43), potentially revealing another link between eRNA and mRNA transcription. In the M2IL4 macrophages, eRNA expression is robustly induced at multiple sites surrounding the Egr2 gene, all within a region of 200 Kb comprising a single topologically associating domain (TAD) (15, 44). Similarly, strongly inducible enhancers–that bore the H3K4me1 ‘poised enhancer’ mark at baseline and gained H3K27ac activation mark upon polarization–were identified as drivers of Igf1 and Arg1 (44), illustrating that the M2IL4 state is characterized by a robust enhancer network. Many other M2 genes are marked by active enhancer expression nearby, often surrounded by several sites producing bidirectional transcripts (12).

  Unlike PPARγ-dependent gene activation in response to the ligand, the transcription complexes and mechanisms driving the activity of PPARγ-bound enhancers during IL4-mediated macrophage polarization are only beginning to emerge. In response to IL4, PPARγ, p300 and the architectural protein RAD21 assemble on DNA (12). However, what drives PPARγ recruitment, the identity and roles of additional cofactors in PPARγ-driven complex formation, enhancer expression and cofactor-driven 3D genome rewiring are still unknown. Here, we interrogate genome-wide actions of the p160 coregulator GRIP1, typically viewed as a partner for ligand-activated NRs and the HAT p300, in the context of PPARγ-dependent gene expression in macrophages polarized with IL4 vs. treated with the PPARγ thiazolidinedione agonist rosiglitazone (RSG). We describe unexpected and broad engagement of GRIP1 in transcription complexes with unliganded PPARγ in M2IL4 and the mechanistic basis for its contribution to M2-specific, PPARγ-driven enhancer activation beyond p300 recruitment.


## RESULTS

### GRIP1 and PPARγ co-regulate a set of overlapping IL4- and RSG-activated targets
  To begin to evaluate cofactor requirements for IL4-induced PPARγ-dependent polarization, we used wild-type (WT) bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM), and those produced from mice conditionally lacking GRIP1 (GRIP1-cKO) or PPARγ (PPARγ-cKO) in myeloid cells (Fig. 1A). In WT macrophages as well as the GRIP1-cKO, a 24-hour polarization with IL4 led to a marked increase in PPARγ protein levels. As expected, no full-length PPARγ protein was detectable in any macrophage populations in the PPARγ-cKO.

  We next compared the expression of hallmark M2 targets in IL4-polarized WT BMDM with those lacking PPARγ or GRIP1 using RT-qPCR. Strikingly, upregulation of numerous M2 genes including Ak2, Abcg1, Arg1, Fabp4, Il6st, Ier3, Klf9 and Cd36 was similarly attenuated in both PPARγ-cKO and GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 (Fig. 1B). Thus, GRIP1 and PPARγ both regulate the expression of signature PPARγ-dependent M2 genes and share a functionally relevant set of M2 targets, raising the possibility that GRIP1 serves as a cofactor for PPARγ during polarization with IL4.

  As a positive control, we also assessed the effect of PPARγ and GRIP1 loss on RSG-activated genes. First, in an RNA-seq experiment, out of >200 genes upregulated after a 3-hour RSG treatment in WT macrophages (fig. S1A, left; canonical RSG targets highlighted in dark blue), the vast majority were no longer differentially expressed in either PPARγ-cKO (fig. S1A, middle) or GRIP1-cKO (fig. S1A, right). To determine when gene induction by RSG was most pronounced, we performed a time course experiment and observed that the 24-hour RSG treatment yields the highest induction, without significantly upregulating PPARγ protein levels (fig. S1B and Fig. 1A: MRSG). We then performed RT-qPCR in WT, PPARγ-cKO and GRIP1-cKO macrophages treated with RSG for 24 h. Most genes, including the canonical RSG targets Angptl4, Plin2, Plet1 and Serpine1, were no longer responsive to the ligand in PPARγ-cKO and showed attenuated induction in GRIP1-cKO (Fig. 1C), with Axl, Vdr and Cpt1a not showing dependence on GRIP1. Given such a clear effect of GRIP1 on ligand-dependent gene regulation by PPARγ, we proceeded to include RSG in the subsequent experiments.


### GRIP1 is required for genome-wide PPARγ binding to DNA in M2IL4, and for p300 recruitment to PPARγ-bound sites in both M2IL4 and MRSG macrophages
  Given the effect of GRIP1 loss on PPARγ target gene expression in M2IL4, we next interrogated genome-wide occupancy of PPARγ by CUT&RUN and compared it to the GRIP1 cistrome we recently reported (20). As shown in fig. S2A, over 55% of PPARγ binding sites in M2IL4 overlapped with those of GRIP1. We then assessed the genome-wide effect of GRIP1 on transcription factor and cofactor distribution at PPARγ binding sites. We first evaluated PPARγ occupancy in M0 and M2IL4 macrophages in both WT and GRIP1-cKO. As depicted with the summary plots, PPARγ binding increased dramatically in WT M2IL4 relative to M0 (Fig. 2A). Surprisingly, this increase of PPARγ binding was no longer detectable in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 (Fig. 2A). Out of the total 4,731 PPARγ-bound sites detected in at least 2 replicates after peak calling, 685 were significantly upregulated in M2IL4 relative to M0, and none were downregulated (Fig. 2B, left). GRIP1 deletion resulted in downregulation of 3,880 PPARγ peaks, amounting to >80% of all detected PPARγ peaks (Fig. 2B, right) with almost no statistically significant peaks gained in GRIP1-cKO during M0-to-M2IL4 polarization (fig. S2B, left). Sites close to signature M2 genes such as Arg1 –4.3 Kb, Ak2 –9.4 Kb, Il6st –25 Kb, Abcg1 –17.5 Kb, Klf4 –34.7 Kb and Ier3 –11.9 Kb showed both increased PPARγ recruitment in WT M2IL4 compared to WT M0 and reduced PPARγ binding in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 compared to WT M2IL4. Notably, most of these target genes were dependent on both GRIP1 and PPARγ for their full induction in M2IL4 (Fig. 1B), revealing a close agreement between the binding and transcription data.

  Given that a canonical role of GRIP1 is to recruit HATs such as p300 (45), we assessed genome-wide p300 occupancy in WT and GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 and M0 macrophages. The binding of p300 increased modestly in WT M2IL4 over M0, but not in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 compared to M0 (Fig. 2C). Indeed, out of 1846 p300 peaks identified within this dataset, 500 were upregulated in M2IL4 relative to M0 (Fig. 2D, left). Of those, 44 peaks were still upregulated in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 relative to matching GRIP1-cKO M0 (fig. S2C), and in a direct comparison between WT and GRIP1-cKO M2IL4, the downregulation of 210 peaks (such as Itgax +7.6 Kb) reached statistical significance (Fig. 2D, right). Overall, GRIP1 had an unexpectedly greater contribution to PPARγ occupancy on DNA in M2IL4 than it did on p300 recruitment. Of note, although the effect of GRIP1 deletion on PPARγ and p300 occupancy at individual sites at M0 baseline did not reach significance (fig. S2, B and C, right panels), the global normalized binding of PPARγ and p300 was reduced in both M2IL4 and M0 GRIP1-cKO macrophages, as evidenced by empirical cumulative distribution function (ECDF) plot with a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for statistically significant differences (Fig. 2, E and F). Together, these data support a fundamental role of GRIP1 in the formation of IL4-induced PPARγ enhancers, stabilizing the entire protein:DNA complex.

  To compare these data with PPARγ complexes in the context of more conventional activation by ligand, we assessed the effect of 24-hour RSG treatment on the binding of PPARγ and p300 in WT and GRIP1-deficient macrophages. RSG treatment produced a relatively small number of new PPARγ peaks compared to those seen in M0 (fig. S2D, left), consistent with unliganded PPARγ being already prebound at its canonical genomic sites. In addition, GRIP1 ablation did not result in any loss of PPARγ peaks (fig. S2D, right). Unexpectedly, global p300 binding was not increased in WT MRSG macrophages over M0 and dropped markedly in GRIP1-cKO MRSG compared to M0 (fig. S2, E and F, left). Many p300 peaks seen in WT MRSG were downregulated in GRIP1-cKO MRSG including sites close to canonical RSG targets such as Prex1 –16.4 Kb and Gnat3 +37.8 Kb (fig. S2F, left). Moreover, these were the same sites that lost p300 occupancy in response to RSG compared to their relevant GRIP1-cKO M0 baseline (fig. S2F, right). Thus, GRIP1 is not required for DNA binding of liganded PPARγ, but it is necessary for stable p300 occupancy in the context of RSG-activated PPARγ.


### Colocalization of PPARγ and p300 at a subset of sites in M2IL4 produces context-dependent binding scenarios
  To better understand the p300 and PPARγ occupancy patterns, we integrated their genomic binding data. Out of the total 1,846 p300 sites detected in this dataset, 1129 overlapped with PPARγ, which represents >60% of all p300 sites (fig. S3A). We then stratified all p300 peaks into 500 that were induced by IL4 (>2-fold) and 1,346 non-induced sites and interrogated PPARγ binding at those regions (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, at induced p300 sites, we also observed an increase in PPARγ signals in WT M2IL4, and the induction of both was completely lost upon GRIP1 deletion (Fig. 3A). Moreover, p300 and PPARγ signals at non-induced sites were visibly reduced in both M0 and M2IL4 GRIP1-cKO populations (Fig. 3A). An analysis of a subset of 1,129 sites co-occupied by p300 and PPARγ revealed 269 IL4-induced and 860 non-induced p300 sites (fig. S3B). In the WT macrophages, IL4-induced p300 binding was fully mirrored by striking upregulation of PPARγ signals, and both were eliminated in GRIP1-cKO (fig. S3B). Furthermore, 860 non-induced sites displayed a considerable loss of both p300 and PPARγ signals in M0 and M2IL4 GRIP1-deficient macrophages (fig. S3B). Thus, the overlapping PPARγ and p300 peaks showed similar dependence on GRIP1.

  Given that we detected many more PPARγ binding sites than those of p300, we stratified all PPARγ peaks, irrespective of p300 occupancy, by the response to IL4 and assessed the impact of GRIP1 deletion on each subset of sites. In a pool of 4731 sites, 685 PPARγ sites were found to be significantly induced, whereas 4046 were not (Fig. 3B). Importantly, both induced and pre-bound sites revealed an enrichment for PPARγ binding motifs as well as motifs for RXR, a PPARγ heterodimeric partner (Fig. 3C; see data files S2 and S3). Similar to a subset of PPARγ sites overlapping with p300 peaks in fig. S3B, both clusters of sites showed a broad dependence on GRIP1: for IL4-induced sites, GRIP1 deletion led to loss of increased PPARγ binding in M2IL4, whereas pre-bound PPARγ peaks were greatly attenuated in both M0 and M2IL4 GRIP1-cKO macrophages (Fig. 3B).

  Our analysis of PPARγ and p300 co-occupied enhancers near candidate PPARγ targets revealed multiple binding scenarios. For example, in the gene body of Cd36, we noted three different occupancy patterns (Fig. 3D, left; also see summary below the tracks). In scenario 1, PPARγ and p300 are both constitutively co-bound in WT M0/M2IL4. In GRIP1-cKO, PPARγ binding is blunted in both M0 and M2IL4 (red arrow), but p300 binding persists in both populations, suggesting that a factor other than PPARγ might be responsible for its retention. In scenario 2, the binding of both PPARγ and p300 is similarly present in M0 and M2IL4, but deletion of GRIP1 ablates both (red arrows). This scenario seems to be most typical among the PPARγ and p300 co-bound sites. Finally, in scenario 3, the binding of both PPARγ and p300 is strongly induced in WT M2IL4 (orange arrows) and blunted in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 (red arrows), reflecting what appears to be a dominant pattern for IL4-induced sites. Thus, even in the context of a single PPARγ target gene, we observe distinct occupancy patterns of p300 and PPARγ and their dependence on GRIP1, corroborating the genome-wide data (Figs. 2, A to D and 3, A and B and fig. S3B).

  To compare these findings with liganded PPARγ complexes, we analyzed Plin2 as a representative RSG-inducible gene of our CUT&RUN dataset in WT and GRIP1-cKO M0 and MRSG macrophages (Fig. 3D, right; also summarized below the tracks). In scenario 1, PPARγ and p300 are bound similarly in M0 and MRSG. PPARγ binding is GRIP1-independent in both macrophage populations. Interestingly, p300 binding is blunted in the GRIP1-cKO MRSG population below that observed in M0 (red arrow). In scenario 2, PPARγ and p300 peaks are both augmented in MRSG (brown arrows) and, mirroring scenario 1, PPARγ binding is unaffected by the loss of GRIP1 in either population, whereas p300 binding is once again GRIP1-dependent specifically in MRSG (red arrow). Both scenarios align well with genome-wide analysis (fig. S2, D to F) and affirm that PPARγ DNA binding at these sites is GRIP1-independent, whereas p300 recruitment to the liganded PPARγ complexes relies on GRIP1.


### The recruitment of p300 to PPARγ-bound enhancers and their eRNA transcription in M2IL4 are both GRIP1-dependent
  Analysis of available datasets generated by us and others (12, 20) reveals that enhancers associated with many of PPARγ- and GRIP1-dependent M2 genes are bound by both proteins. Hence, we investigated eRNA transcription as a function of PPARγ and GRIP1 binding. The amplification sites were selected from the published GRO-seq dataset (12), which also informed the direction of eRNA transcription. As expected, all the profiled eRNA sites required PPARγ for their induction in M2IL4, as their upregulation was abolished in PPARγ-cKO macrophages (Fig. 4A). These included two sites each for Il6st (–23 Kb sense and –25 Kb anti-sense), Abcg1 (–18.5 Kb anti-sense and –17.5 Kb bidirectional) and Ak2 (–13 Kb anti-sense and +40 Kb anti-sense), and a single site for Eif5a (–10.5 Kb sense) and Ier3 (–7.5 Kb sense) (Fig. 4A). Notably, except for the Abcg1 –17.5 Kb and Ak2 +40 Kb sites, the rest were also GRIP1-dependent with the induction blunted in GRIP1-cKO (Fig. 4B). These results illustrate that GRIP1 co-regulates the activity of PPARγ-bound enhancers near shared gene targets in a site-specific manner. Given that GRIP1 was differentially required for the IL4-induced transcription of eRNAs even associated with the same protein-coding gene (i.e., GRIP1 selectively activates the Abcg1 –18.5 Kb, but not the –17.5 Kb enhancer, and the Ak2 –13 Kb, but not the +40 Kb enhancer), we questioned whether the selectivity can be explained by differences in GRIP1-driven p300 or PPARγ occupancy near these sites.

  We examined the binding of PPARγ and p300 in WT and GRIP1-cKO macrophages in the M0 and M2IL4 populations near the sites of eRNA expression for the Abcg1 and Ak2 genes (Fig. 4C; eRNA locations marked by vertical black lines). Upstream of the GRIP1-dependent Abcg1 –18.5 Kb site, PPARγ and p300 are recruited in WT M2IL4, and an increase in their binding is blunted in the GRIP1-cKO. As expected, GRIP1 occupancy and H3K27ac enhancer mark from our prior study (20) are both evident and increasing at this site in WT M2IL4 over M0. Conversely, at the GRIP1-independent Abcg1 –17.5 Kb site, PPARγ is recruited in M2IL4 in a GRIP1-dependent manner, and GRIP1 is bound in WT M2IL4, however there is no appreciable p300 occupancy in either M0 or M2IL4. A similar dichotomy of the PPARγ vs. p300 binding patterns is seen near the two Ak2 enhancers: downstream of the GRIP1-dependent Ak2 –13 Kb site, both PPARγ and p300 are recruited in the WT (but not in GRIP1-cKO) M2IL4, along with GRIP1. In contrast, only PPARγ, and not p300, is recruited to the GRIP1-independent Ak2 +40 Kb site. We inspected the rest of the GRIP1-dependent eRNA transcripts and found that upon IL4 polarization, p300 is recruited to all PPARγ-bound enhancers (along with GRIP1 and H3K27 acetylation) in the WT but not in GRIP1-cKO (see fig. S4 for Il6st –23 Kb and –25 Kb enhancers and Eif5a –10 Kb enhancer). Thus, it appears that GRIP1-driven p300 recruitment to PPARγ in M2IL4 predicts whether the induction of associated eRNAs is GRIP1-dependent.


### GRIP1 deletion promotes the deposition of CTCF insulator near PPARγ target genes in both M2IL4 and MRSG
  Given that GRIP1 regulates the expression of eRNAs near M2 genes, we investigated its genome-wide effect on enhancer activation. Apart from H3K27ac, a hallmark of poised enhancers which enables rapid gene activation is monomethylation of histone H3 lysine 4 (H3K4me1) (46, 47). We therefore profiled H3K4me1 distribution in M0 and M2IL4 macrophages in WT and GRIP1-cKO. Polarization of macrophages to M2IL4 led to a widespread increase in H3K4me1 levels at distal intergenic regions, with 1,947 sites showing a significant increase (Fig. 5A, left). Compared to WT M2IL4, GRIP1 ablation resulted in significant depletion of the H3K4me1 mark at only 100 sites (∼5% of the total number of IL4-upregulated peaks), while 124 sites gained H3K4me1 in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4 (Fig. 5A, right). Out of the H3K4me1-down sites in GRIP1-cKO M2IL4, Vegfc +211.4 Kb and Crtc3 –11.2 Kb are examples of M2 genes that required GRIP1 for H3K4me1 deposition in both M0 and M2IL4 (Fig. 5B). Thus, GRIP1 is necessary for establishing the poised enhancer mark at a few M2 genes, but the overall genome-wide effect of GRIP1 on H3K4me1 patterns is modest.

  Macrophage polarization involves large-scale chromatin reorganization, shifting regions of activation and the boundaries across megabase-scale topologically associated domains (TADs). CTCF is an insulator protein which demarcates TAD boundary edges (48), with its gain or loss potentially indicative of the sliding of the boundaries. Hence, we assessed by CUT&RUN the effect of polarization on CTCF genomic distribution and the potential role of GRIP1 in this process. We first examined CTCF binding near candidate M2 PPARγ targets including Klf9, Arg1, Abcg1 and Ak2, and observed no consistent effect of IL4 on CTCF occupancy with signals ranging from modestly induced, to unaffected or slightly downregulated (Fig. 5C and fig. S5A). Surprisingly, irrespective of the effects of IL4, GRIP1 ablation uniformly increased CTCF occupancy at all the sites examined (Fig. 5C and fig. S5A). Notably, these were the same enhancer sites that were losing PPARγ and p300 binding in GRIP1-cKO along with additional intergenic and promoter regions of associated M2 genes (Fig. 5C and fig. S5A; also see diagram in fig. S5B). Interestingly, similar trends with enhanced CTCF loading in GRIP1-cKO were seen at conventional RSG-inducible targets of PPARγ, Cpt1a and Serpine1 (fig. S5C). We then assessed genome-wide CTCF binding anchored on PPARγ peaks in WT BMDM and observed a notable increase across all three GRIP1-cKO macrophage populations over their WT counterparts (Fig. 5D); no such increase was detectable when all genomic CTCF peaks were pooled. Moreover, global normalized CTCF signals assessed at such PPARγ-occupied sites were significantly increased in GRIP1-cKO (fig. S5D). Thus, GRIP1 not only facilitates DNA binding and assembly of PPARγ enhancer complexes, but contributes to global chromatin organization in homeostatic macrophages by precluding the loading of CTCF insulator at M2 gene enhancers, promoters and intergenic regions.


## DISCUSSION
  PPARγ is a type II NR, whose function has been well defined within the framework of the ‘cofactor exchange model’: unliganded DNA-bound PPARγ/RXR heterodimers release the corepressor/HDAC complexes and recruit coactivator/HATs upon agonist binding leading to target gene activation. The model is supported by structural studies using high-affinity synthetic thiazolidinediones, e.g., RSG (49), as well as lower affinity structurally diverse natural ligands such as oxidized fatty acids (50, 51). The cell type-specific patterns of PPARγ binding and activity have also been noted, especially when comparing adipocytes and macrophages – cells rich in PPARγ expression (12, 52). In the case of macrophages, IL4 polarization in the absence of added ligand was shown to trigger the ‘extension’ of the RXR cistrome in a PPARγ-dependent manner (53). Our study agrees with findings by the Nagy lab, as we observe genome-wide mobilization of PPARγ to numerous novel genomic sites in M2IL4 (and not MRSG) however, the mechanisms and cofactor complexes that mediate PPARγ actions in its unliganded state are not well understood.

  Based on their mode of action, cofactors have been divided into three major classes: 1) those that use enzymatic activities to modify histones and components of transcriptional machinery (e.g., CBP/p300); 2) ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complexes (e.g., SWI/SNF/BAF); 3) scaffolding proteins that serve as platforms for the assembly of secondary coregulators. The p160/NCoA1–3 family members including GRIP1 function predominantly as such ‘recruiters’, with designated domains for binding CBP/p300 and CARM1/PRMT1, among others. The hallmark of the p160 family is their agonist-dependent recruitment to NRs driven by ligand-induced repositioning of helix 12 of the NR ligand binding pocket. Our data on broad utilization of GRIP1 by unliganded PPARγ in macrophages suggests more versatility in PPARγ:GRIP1 interaction, perhaps specifically augmented by IL4 signaling. Notably, post-translational modifications are known to act as regulatory switches for orphan NRs such as LRH1, SF1 or members of the Nr4A family (54–56). PPARγ and GRIP1 are also both subject to a variety of post-translational modifications including phosphorylation, acetylation and sumoylation which alter their transcriptional activities (57–61). Whether such mechanisms operate in IL4-polarized macrophages to direct PPARγ to specific sites and/or promote its ligand-independent interaction with GRIP1 is a promising direction to pursue. Of note, no PPARγ peaks were downregulated/lost in M2IL4 relative to M0, indicating that if such IL4-driven posttranslational modifications occur, the receptor does not undergo genome-wide ‘redistribution’ or is being ‘evicted’ from the binding sites it occupies in resting macrophages.

  The other striking finding was the nature of the GRIP1 contribution to the PPARγ-driven gene and enhancer activation in M2IL4. The primary function attributed to GRIP1, e.g., recruitment of secondary coregulators, held true at RSG-regulated PPARγ genes: while we did not observe a net increase in p300 occupancy in WT MRSG over M0 baseline, p300 was dramatically depleted in GRIP1-cKO MRSG, whereas PPARγ occupancy was largely unaffected. In contrast, in M2IL4, GRIP1 ablation nearly extinguished PPARγ binding to DNA, without appreciably changing the PPARγ protein levels, with p300 occupancy relatively less affected. This finding suggests that the composition and structure of unliganded PPARγ transcription complexes at such IL4-inducible enhancers is distinct, with GRIP1 anchoring or stabilizing the DNA binding of the receptor itself. If so, the functional consequences of GRIP1 ablation in M2IL4 are expected to be more profound compared to that in MRSG. This hypothesis is supported by our gene expression data, whereby treatment with RSG yielded a clear subset of GRIP1-insensitive targets, such as Axl, Vdr and Cpt1a, whereas at PPARγ-dependent IL4-induced targets, GRIP1 loss all but phenocopied the loss of PPARγ.

  Given that GRIP1 itself is not a DNA-binding protein, we can envision several potential mechanisms through which GRIP1 may affect PPARγ DNA occupancy. It may, for example, promote the formation of soluble condensates in M2IL4 which contain active chromatin, thus enabling PPARγ binding, whereas in the GRIP1-cKO those chromatin sites would remain insoluble. We further speculate that GRIP1 binding specifically to the unliganded PPARγ results in a conformational shift of GRIP1 conducive to condensate formation in a manner that GRIP1 interacting with the RSG-bound PPARγ is not. Indeed, cofactors such as BRD4 and MED1 form nuclear puncta at super-enhancers with properties of liquid-like condensates; intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs) of these cofactors form phase-separated droplets that can compartmentalize transcriptional apparatus from nuclear extracts (62). Moreover, a cofactor closely related to GRIP1, a p160 NCoA3/SRC-3, was shown to promote via its IDR phase separation with GR (63), making such a scenario highly plausible for GRIP1. In addition, eRNA-mediated condensate formation and GRIP1 could act cooperatively, driving phase separation of chromatin into the active liquid phase.

  While future biochemical and structural studies are needed to discern the architecture of these enhancers, it is already apparent that the role of GRIP1 in M2 programming is quite nuanced, with distinct types of PPARγ transcription complexes assembling even at the enhancers associated with the same gene. Our motif enrichment analysis of both pre-bound and IL4-induced PPARγ peaks yielded RXR sites among the top enriched motifs, suggesting that much of PPARγ likely relies on its conventional heterodimeric partner. As such, it would be informative to assess the potential contribution of RXR to GRIP1 recruitment at each subset of PPARγ sites in differentially polarized macrophage populations. Moreover, some of the PPARγ enhancers are adjacent to binding sites for other transcription factors, such as KLF4 or EGR2, implicated in M2IL4 polarization (15, 17, 64) and known to utilize p300 as well as other HATs, notably CBP (65–67). Indeed, KLF motifs were among those highly enriched in pre-bound PPARγ peaks (data file S2). The proximity of such binding sites and the presence of potentially composite response elements of PPARγ along with other effectors of IL4 signaling, together with the combinatorial use of secondary chromatin modifiers, would also explain the partial insensitivity of some of the tested eRNAs to ablation of GRIP1 or even of PPARγ itself.

  One striking and unexpected observation that emerged in this study is that GRIP1 ablation promoted broad deposition of the CTCF insulator at the enhancer sites that lost p300 and/or PPARγ, as well as promoters of associated genes and at adjacent intergenic regions. This effect was specific to the PPARγ target genes, as unrelated chromatin regions were not marked by CTCF accumulation. It is tempting to speculate that by precluding excessive CTCF loading, GRIP1 ensures that enhancers activate nearby PPARγ targets, whereas CTCF accumulation in GRIP1-cKO would hinder those interactions. Another established function of CTCF is acting in concert with cohesin to establish TAD boundaries. While global mapping of TADs in differentially polarized macrophages was outside of the scope of this work, this result reveals a surprising facet of GRIP1 function in chromatin organization beyond secondary cofactor recruitment.

  Aside from IL4, GCs represent a physiologically relevant signal that elicits macrophage phenotype (M2GC) transcriptomically and functionally similar to that of M2IL4 (1, 4, 68–70). Our recent comprehensive analysis of transcriptomic and epigenomic programming downstream of each signal (20) revealed a significant overlap in the key target genes both up- and downregulated in the two populations. Given that GR recruitment to the genome is strictly ligand-dependent and approximately one-third of all M2GC differentially expressed genes required GRIP1 for their regulation (20), it would be informative to assess GRIP1 distribution in macrophages exposed to IL4 and GCs concurrently–a physiologically relevant scenario. For genes upregulated in both M2IL4 and M2GC, several outcomes are plausible: 1) binding of GRIP1 occurs both at the GR- and PPARγ-bound enhancers and transcriptional regulation of ‘shared’ targets is cooperative; 2) GRIP1 is lost from a subset of GR or PPARγ sites, given that GRIP1 protein is not abundant in macrophages. This may manifest in antagonistic effects between the two signals for a subset of genes, whose upregulation in M2GC or M2IL4 macrophages would be attenuated in the M2IL4 + GC state. Further studies are needed to establish the contribution of GRIP1 to the activity of distinct types of enhancers capable of combinatorial responses to different M2 signals as well as its impact on global chromatin organization.

  In summary, our analysis reveals that the dominant type of PPARγ transcription complexes in IL4-polarized macrophages are those recruiting a p160 coregulator GRIP1 in a ligand-independent manner and relying on GRIP1 both for DNA binding and for engagement of p300 to ultimately drive enhancer transcription and activate M2-specific genes. Further, by preventing excessive CTCF deposition, GRIP1 may enable cross-talk between PPARγ-bound enhancers and the associated target gene promoters. Future studies will establish whether similar mechanisms operate in other cell types in which PPARγ plays a central role, or whether IL4 signaling in macrophages represents a unique context that enables this type of cooperativity between PPARγ, GRIP1 and secondary coregulators.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Mice
  C57BL/6 mice (National Cancer Institute, Charles River Laboratories) and their transgenic derivatives were maintained in the Weill Cornell Animal Facility in compliance with the protocol approved by the Weill Cornell Medical College Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol # 2015–0050). All mice were housed in separate ventilated cages (maximum 5 mice/cage) and maintained on a 12/12-hours light/dark cycle, 22–26°C, 40–70% humidity with sterile pellet food and water ad libitum. Homozygous LysM-WT (LysM-Cre;GRIPwt/wt), PPARγ-cKO (LysM-Cre;PPARGfl/fl) and GRIP1-cKO (LysM-Cre;GRIPfl/fl) mice were generated previously (71, 72). For all tissue and cell collection, mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation. All mice used were 8- to-10-week-old males.


### Cell culture
  BMDM were prepared as previously described (73). Briefly, bone marrow was flushed from the femora and tibiae of mice and differentiated for 5 days in the DMEM (1 g/l glucose) supplemented with 20% bovine serum and 10% L-cell conditioned media. Adherent cells were scraped, seeded into 150 mm or 6-well plates and polarized for 24 hours with 10 ng/ml IL4 (R&D Systems) or 1 μM RSG (Sigma Aldrich) unless otherwise indicated.


### RNA isolation and RT-qPCR
  Total RNA from macrophages, treated as described in Figure legends, was isolated using the Qiagen RNAeasy Plus Mini kit. RNA samples were subjected to random-primed cDNA synthesis and gene expression was analyzed by qPCR with Power Sybr Green/ROX/2x master mix (Thermo Scientific) on the QuantStudio 6 Pro real time PCR system (Applied Biosystems) using standard protocols, and data was analyzed using δδCt method with b-actin transcript as a housekeeping control. For eRNA RT-qPCR, the Ppia transcript was used as a control. Primers are listed in table S1.


### Western blotting
  BMDMs were treated with IL4 or RSG for 24 hours, lysed, and total protein was extracted. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto separate 7.5% Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels (Cat. #4561033), and then two separate gels were run simultaneously: one probed for PPARγ and the other probed for α-tubulin as a loading control. Detection was performed via chemiluminescence using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Ref #32209) following incubation with primary antibodies (PPARγ: Cell Signaling #2443S and α-tubulin: Abclonal #AC049) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (PPARγ: Cell Signaling #7074P2 and α-tubulin: Promega #W4018). Band intensities relative to the membrane background were quantified using ImageJ. For each sample, PPARγ was normalized to the corresponding α-tubulin band, and all values were normalized to their respective no-treatment control group. Statistical significance (P < 0.05) was determined using 2-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test.


### RNAseq
  The Quality of total RNA was assessed using Agilent BioAnalyzer 2100 for RNA integrity. Samples with RIN > 8 were used for library preparations. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Library quality was evaluated using Agilent BioAnalyzer 2100, and the libraries were sequenced by a NovaSeq 6000 system (50 bp, paired-end) at the Genomics Resources Core Facility at Weill Cornell College of Medicine at 15–25 million reads per sample. RNAseq with RSG-treated macrophages was performed with n = 2–4.


### CUT&RUN
  CUT&RUN assay was performed using the EpiCypher CUTANA ChIC/CUT&RUN kit (v. 2.1–4.0) according to manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, 106 cells were harvested for each condition, washed with 1X PBS, and resuspended in wash buffer, followed by incubation at RT for 10 min with activated Concanavalin A (ConA) beads. Cells were then permeabilized and incubated overnight on a nutator at 4°C with 1 μg of monoclonal rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology #66362), PPARγ (Cell Signaling Technology, #2435), p300 (Abcam, ab259330), CTCF (EpiCypher SKU: 13–2014) and H3K4me1 (EpiCypher SKU: 13–0057) antibody. Cell-bead antibody complexes were then washed with cell permeabilization buffer and incubated with pAG-MNase (EpiCypher), followed by addition of 2 mM CaCl2 and incubation for 2 hours at 4°C. Stop buffer was added to the reaction mixture and DNA fragments were released and purified with a CUTANA DNA purification kit (EpiCypher). DNA library construction was performed using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit from NEB (E7645L). The libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (50 bp paired-end) at the Genomics Resources Core Facility at Weill Cornell Medicine.


### Data analysis

#### RNAseq
  Read quality filtering and adapter trimming were performed with fastp 0.23.2 (74). Filtered reads were mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) with the STAR aligner 2.7.0 (75) and exonic reads were counted against GENCODE release 27 annotation with featureCounts (76) using a customized pipeline available at https://gitlab.com/hssgenomics/pipelines/-/tree/master/RNAseq. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with edgeR 3.38.4 (77) using quasi-likelihood framework. Genes with low expression levels (<2 counts per million in at least one group) were filtered from all downstream analyses. P-values were adjusted to correct for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg FDR procedure. Genes with adjusted P values < 0.05 and log2 fold change > 0.6 were considered differentially expressed. Downstream analyses were performed in R using a Shiny-driven visualization platform (RNAseq DRaMA, 1.4.2, https://gitlab.com/hssgenomics/Shiny) developed at the HSS David Z. Rosensweig Genomics Research Center.


### CUT&RUN
  Sequencing QC is provided in data file S1.

  Paired-end reads were pre-processed using fastp, which performed adapter trimming, low-quality base trimming, and calculation of quality control metrics. Trimmed reads were aligned to the mm10 reference mouse genome using Bowtie2 in --very-sensitive-local mode. Alignments were filtered using samtools, and reads mapping to the mitochondrial chromosome (chrM) were excluded prior to downstream analysis. After coordinate sorting and duplicate marking, peak calling was performed for each sample against its respective control using MACS2 with a fragment size of 76 bp and a shift of −37 bp. High-confidence binding sites were identified by aggregating peak calls across replicates.

  For visualization, genomic coverage tracks were generated using BAMscale to produce normalized bigWig files, which were visualized in the UCSC Genome Browser. To identify differential binding sites, data were processed via an established analysis pipeline (https://gitlab.com/hssgenomics/workflow-xchip). A consensus peak set was generated by merging individual sample peaks using bedtools, and read counts per feature were quantified using featureCounts. Heatmaps were summarized using bamsignals and visualized with EnrichedHeatmap. The specific plotting regions are described in the Figure legends.

  To assess global changes in PPARγ, p300 and CTCF occupancy, ECDF plots were generated using normalized binding signals (Log2 counts per million, log2CPM) across all identified peaks. The ECDF represents the cumulative fraction of peaks at or below a given signal intensity. Statistical differences between the distributions were evaluated using the two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. The D statistic was calculated to determine the maximum vertical distance between the curves.

  We used HOMER (78) to identify overrepresented transcription factor motifs in peak assemblages. Full lists of over-represented motifs are provided in data files S2 and S3.
