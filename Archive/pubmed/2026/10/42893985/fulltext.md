# Three-dimensional chromatin architecture is rapidly restored after DNA replication


## Abstract
  Studies of post-replicative chromatin exclusively focus on the kinetics of protein binding after DNA replication to understand maintenance of transcriptional programs. Here we investigated both chromatin-bound factors and the three-dimensional architecture of the post-replicative genome in human cells. We found that transcription factors and chromatin looping proteins rapidly associate with nascent DNA. We discovered that the architectural factors, CTCF and YY1, bind to their target sequences immediately following DNA replication, and that chromatin loops from 39 to 425 kb re-form within 10 min of DNA replication. This suggests that the 3D structure of chromatin is at most briefly disrupted by DNA replication and quickly restores following replisome passage, providing insight into an unaddressed question of the structure of post-replicated chromatin. Our results suggest that all components of the transcriptional network and tertiary chromatin structure are restored shortly after replication, which is consistent with previously detected fast resumption of transcription on daughter DNA strands.


## Introduction
  During S phase of the cell cycle, the entire nuclear genome within a cell is duplicated. In addition to faithfully copying the DNA, cells must also transmit chromatin structure such that gene expression levels are maintained from G1 to G2 phase. Studies of the nature of post-replicative chromatin often focus on potential epigenetic bookmarks1,2, which can transmit the status of gene activation or repression. Up to this point, however, no study has sought to investigate the nature of the three-dimensional structure of chromatin in the nascent genome.

  Chromatin forms three-dimensional loops for both packaging the genome into the nucleus, as well as regulating gene expression3,4. Transcription of target genes requires the coordinated recruitment of RNA polymerase II (Pol II) by various lineage-specific transcription factors (TFs) which bind to DNA to elicit gene expression5. TF binding may occur either at the gene promoter or at distant cis-regulatory elements (CREs), called enhancers, which then induce three-dimensional looping to bring the enhancer close to the promoter of the target gene6,7. Chromatin loops can be subdivided into structural loops, which typically have CTCF bound to anchor points and are stabilized by the cohesin complex, and CRE loops, which contain other architectural factors, such as YY1, at one or both anchor points6,8,9. Structural loop anchors may occur near promoters and enhancers and can therefore also facilitate contacts between these regions10. Promoter-enhancer contact requires additional factors, including the mediator complex, to properly bridge these regulatory regions and induce Pol II activation at the target gene11.

  Given that the three-dimensional structure of chromatin is critical for both DNA packaging and gene regulation, we sought to understand the timing of binding of these various factors to nascent DNA. Moreover, we wanted to determine the timing of loop re-formation on newly synthesized DNA. Strikingly, we found that loops re-form within 10 mins of replication, with TFs, looping factors, and mediator binding to nascent DNA in shorter time periods. This suggests that DNA replication may function in a way that preserves and transmits the three-dimensional structure of chromatin.


## Results

### Transcription factors rapidly rebind DNA post-replication
  The binding of TFs to the promoter and enhancer regulatory regions is required to maintain lineage-specific gene activation. We decided to access the timing of recruitment of several TFs to DNA after replication to understand how and when TFs bind to replicated DNA. To do so, we utilized our previously developed Chromatin Assembly Assay (CAA)12–14. In CAA, nascent DNA is pulse-labeled with EdU followed by cell fixation. Then, biotin is conjugated to EdU using click chemistry, and a proximity ligation assay (PLA) is performed with antibodies to biotin and a protein of interest, to assess whether the protein is bound to nascent DNA within the labeling time. CAA can be done in a pulse-chase manner to assess protein recruitment to nascent DNA over time. Additionally, it is performed in an asynchronous cell population, so by quantifying the number of signals in many nuclei, we can assess the kinetics of protein recruitment across the genome.

  We performed CAA for six different TFs after 5 min EdU, 10 min EdU, or 10 min EdU with a 10 min chase. Importantly, we did not see any PLA signals in negative control reactions with antibodies to TFs and an isotype IgG control (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Of the six assessed TFs, we found that five (JUN, CREB, MYC, ATF2, and HNF3α) were all bound within 5 min of DNA replication and steadily accumulated after that (Fig. 1a–e). At 5 min EdU labeling, FKHRL1 showed few CAA signals, suggesting that at 5 min it is either not bound or bound at very low levels and only accumulates on nascent DNA at 10-20 min post-replication (Fig. 1f). This suggests that many TFs are capable of quickly reassociating with DNA after replication, but some are recruited with slower kinetics. To access this, we performed CAA following longer chase times for CREB and FKHRL1 (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b).   To understand whether some of the factors bound at 5 min are also on DNA at shorter times after replication, we decided to utilize our multiplex version of CAA (see scheme in Fig. 2a)14. In multiplex CAA, two PLA reactions, each of which detects proteins within 40 nm of nascent DNA, are performed in parallel within the same cell, each between a protein of interest and EdU-labeled DNA. For these experiments, one of the proteins assessed is PCNA, which functions during DNA replication as a processivity factor that travels with the DNA polymerases on both daughter stands of DNA15. We previously showed that by using STED super-resolution microscopy to assess overlaps in multiplex CAA we can determine the distance between two proteins on nascent DNA by measuring the distance between the centers of PLA foci. We determined that when PLA foci overlapped, they were on average 136 nm ± 53 nm (s.d.) apart14 which corresponds to approximately 400 bp on linear DNA given that 100 bp of linear DNA is 34 nm16 and linear DNA is expected at the replication fork17. ### Proteins involved in chromatin looping bind post-replicative DNA
  TFs regulate gene expression by binding at either the promoter or at distal regulatory enhancers. Enhancer binding of TFs elicits gene expression by looping to the promoter, a process that involves various architectural factors. CRE loops, such as those between promoters and enhancers often feature other factors, such as YY1, at one or both loop anchor points which help maintain loop contacts19–21. Structural loops, which contain CTCF and are stabilized by the cohesin complex, help form chromatin domains22, but may also facilitate promoter-enhancer contacts either if their anchor points occur near regulatory regions10,23 or by demarcating different compartments to ensure promoters and enhancers properly contact one another22,24. Additionally, the mediator complex is recruited to enhancer-bound TFs and can induce transcriptional initiation of Pol II at the promoter on the other end of the loop, resulting in activation of the target gene11.

  Given that we found TFs bound shortly after DNA replication, we decided to assess whether factors involved in looping are also bound to DNA shortly after DNA replication. We chose to investigate looping factors (CTCF and YY1), cohesion components (SMC1 and STAG-2), and mediator components (MED12 and cyclin C). We performed CAA with DNA labeled for 5 min or 10 min or 10 min followed by a 10 min chase. ### CTCF and YY1 rebind DNA within 100-200 bp of the replication fork
  Given that to this point there has been no study about the nature of chromatin looping on nascent DNA, we decided to focus on CTCF and YY1, given their known roles in DNA looping8,19,20,25, whereas cohesin plays roles in both chromatin looping and sister chromatid cohesion26,27. To investigate whether these proteins bind to their target DNA sites immediately following DNA replication, we performed sequential chromatin immunoprecipitation (re-ChIP) assays with PCNA as previously described13,14. In these experiments, chromatin is immunoprecipitated for a protein of interest, eluted, and then re-immunoprecipitated with an anti-PCNA antibody (see scheme in Fig. 5a). We previously showed that PCNA ChIP yields 100-200 bp fragments of only nascent DNA, because single-stranded DNA ahead of the replication fork is more susceptible to shearing by sonication13,14.   We first performed conventional ChIP and found CTCF-specific binding sites near the promoters of HOX5C, ARHGEF12, and GLI1; YY1-specific sites near the promoters of PEX14, WBP11, and ZC3H18; and shared binding sites near the promoters of MDM2 and TSC22D2 (Fig. 5b, c, top). We next split the CTCF and YY1 immunoprecipitated material and performed re-ChIP with either anti-PCNA or IgG. We found that both CTCF and YY1 bind to all their respective target regions after replication (Fig. 5b, c bottom), indicating that these proteins are associated with nascent DNA within 100-200 bp of the replication fork. Interestingly, relative YY1 levels on nascent chromatin show disparate levels compared to parental chromatin, suggesting that there may be locus-specific differences its nascent genome. This indicates that these proteins are only transiently displaced from DNA and can rebind to their target sites within seconds of passage of the replication fork.


### CTCF and YY1 are maintained at the replisome by PCNA
  We next wanted to determine whether CTCF and YY1 are maintained at the replication fork by direct interaction with PCNA, which is known to facilitate chromatin loading of various factors during replication14,15. To assess this, we performed co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays with an anti-PCNA antibody in nuclear lysates. We found that CTCF and YY1 co-immunoprecipitate with PCNA, suggesting that they physically interact with the replication machinery (Fig. 5d). We additionally found that the cohesin component STAG2 co-IPs with PCNA, which is in-line with previously shown interactions between replication proteins and cohesin components26,28 (Fig. 5d). To assess potential direct interaction between PCNA and CTCF and PCNA and YY1, we performed GST pulldowns with bacterially expressed and purified GST-PCNA. GST-PCNA pulled down both CTCF and YY1 (Fig. 5e), confirming our co-immunoprecipitation data and suggesting that these proteins can interact directly.

  To understand whether there is a mechanistic link between PCNA and the post-replication binding of CTCF and YY1, we performed CAA experiments following treatment with PCNA-I1, which affects PCNA trimerization29, and which we previously used to assess the mechanism of Pol II binding to nascent DNA14. Cells were treated with PCNA-I1 for various times, incubated with EdU in the presence of PCNA-I1, and then CAA was performed for PCNA, CTCF, and YY1. We found that increasing times of PCNA-I1 treatment caused loss of all three factors from nascent chromatin, but CTCF and YY1 were lost more quickly than PCNA (Fig. 5f). Next, we treated cells for 3 h with PCNA-I1 and then washed out PCNA-1I for 2, 3, or 4 h (Fig. 5g). Following washout, PCNA levels on nascent chromatin returned more quickly than for either CTCF or YY1. These results support a direct role for PCNA in facilitating the association of CTCF and YY1 with replicated DNA.


### Chromatin loops re-form within 10 min of replication
  Given that CTCF and YY1 associate with DNA very quickly after replication, we were curious about the timing of loop re-formation on nascent DNA. No current methods exist to study the nature of looping on post-replicative chromatin, so we adapted the existing chromatin conformation capture (3C) technique30,31 to develop our nascent 3C approach (see scheme in Fig. 6a). We labeled nascent DNA for 10 min or 40 min with EdU, crosslinked cells, and then digested chromatin with HindIII. DNA was then ligated together, protein crosslinks were reversed, and DNA was purified. Purified DNA was subjected to click chemistry either with or without biotin azide (as a negative control), captured on streptavidin-coated resin, and analyzed by qPCR. We assessed four different loops by our nascent 3 C approach (see genomic maps of target loops in Fig. 6b) using known loops in similar cells32–34. As shown in Fig. 6c, we identified loops from the promoters of four different genes to distant regulatory regions, ranging from 39 kb (POU2AF1) to 425 kb (ADAMTS8). We next assessed whether these loops were formed within 10 or 40 min of replication by our nascent 3C approach. Following EdU biotinylation and streptavidin capture, we detected similar levels of looping at both 10- and 40-min labeling at all four tested loops (Fig. 6d). Importantly, we detected little to no DNA in negative click reactions lacking biotin azide, confirming the specificity of our streptavidin pulldowns (Fig. 6d). ## Discussion
  Regulation of the 3D conformation of chromatin is complex and requires the coordinated effort of lineage-specific TFs35,36, in conjunction with other proteins, including looping factors, cohesin, and mediator. In our previous work studying general transcription and elongation factors, as well as Pol II, during DNA replication, we found that they rebind immediately following passage of the replisome14. However, uncertainty remained regarding the post-replicative fate of proteins involved in regulating transcription through long-distance interactions and chromatin architecture.

  Some previous studies of lineage-specific TFs have suggested that they might rebind DNA relatively shortly after replication. A recent proteomic study of factors on EdU-labeled DNA suggested that a majority of TFs may be able to bind to DNA within 15 min of replication37, while other proteomic approaches to assessing nascent chromatin have found more variable timings of binding of factors38–40. Several groups have combined the accessibility mapping approaches MNase-seq and ATAC-seq with EdU labeling to interrogate the chromatin structure of the nascent genome. In these approaches, DNA is EdU labeled and chromatin is either digested with MNase (for MINCE-seq41) or treated with transposase (for repli-ATAC-seq42), followed by biotinylation of EdU-labeled DNA, streptavidin capture, and sequencing. A more recent addition, ‘replication-aware single-molecule accessibility mapping’ (RASAM), combined BrdU-labeling with long read PacBio sequencing to access nucleosome structure on nascent DNA43. The positioning of nucleosomes on labeled DNA is then used to infer whether TFs may be bound to nascent DNA following replication. The results of these studies varied regarding the timing of binding of TFs after DNA replication, either due to differences between TFs or due to the indirect method being used to ascertain their binding. Most importantly, all these approaches typically utilize at least 10 min to 20 min and therefore are unable to assess events directly at the replication fork.

  We found that in a human cell line five of the six tested TFs bind to DNA within 5 min of replication (Fig. 1a-f). The one factor which either did not bind or may be bound at low levels, FKHRL1, is a FOXO family member which have been suggested to function as pioneer factors44. These types of factors may require a particular nucleosomal structure on nascent DNA in order to bind nascent chromatin. Interestingly, FKHRL1 accumulates with slower kinetics on nascent chromatin than the quickly loaded factors CREB and STAG2 (Supplementary Fig. 2). This suggests different kinetic of loading of different factors in the nascent genome. Additionally, by multiplex CAA, we found that JUN and CREB bind within several hundred bp of the replication fork (Fig. 2a,b). This suggests that these TFs can rebind DNA almost immediately after replication.

  Some studies have previously sought to understand the kinetics of recruitment of CTCF to replicated DNA. Using a replication-specific version of ChIP-seq, Xu and Corces detected CTCF on DNA labeled for 20 min with EdU45. Intriguingly, they also found that the cohesin component SMC1A is bound to nascent DNA at CTCF sites at this same time point. Given the role of cohesin in stabilizing loops at CTCF anchor sites, this suggests that CTCF-cohesin bound structural loops could potentially be re-formed within this timeframe. These data support our finding by CAA that CTCF binds to its sites in 5 min after passage of the replication fork. Similarly, by assessing nucleosome positioning on nascent DNA using EdU-labeling and micrococcal digestion, it was speculated that CTCF may bind to nascent DNA shortly after replication at a similar time to what we observed by CAA46.

  Further, by multiplex CAA, we found CTCF and YY1 at DNA within several hundred bp of the replication fork (Fig. 4a,b,d). While we obtained similar results with cohesin, interpretation of these results is more complicated, since they may reflect its role in sister chromatid cohesion and/or looping26,27. In addition, a recent study has shown that the cohesin ring is large enough to allow passage of the replisome through it47. This suggests a way in which cohesin is able to be retained on chromatin through replication that differs from other factors which bind specific DNA motifs.

  Our PCNA re-ChIP data show that CTCF and YY1 bind to their sites on 100-200 bp of the most nascent DNA (Figs. 5b,c, 7b,c). To understand the timing of loop re-formation, we developed a nascent 3C approach and found that loops ranging from 39 kb to 425 kb are re-formed within 10 min of DNA replication and their levels do not increase at 40 min (Fig. 6). The genome is divided into topologically associating domains (TADs), which are subdivided into active and repressed regions, termed A and B compartments, respectively3. Promoter-enhancer loops occur between regions confined within an A compartment. The boundaries of TADs overlap with replication domains (RDs), which are known to simultaneously replicate during S phase48–50. Thus, looped regions will be replicated synchronously during S phase. In support of this, origins are known to fire near CTCF sites bound by CTCF and cohesin51. Additionally, repli-seq analysis in various human GM lines52, similar to the cell line we utilized for our nascent 3C experiments, showed synchronous replication of the anchor and loop points for the four loops we assessed (Fig. 6c,d). Recent replication-specific adaptations of Hi-C and micro-C have been employed to understand the coupling of replication forks53,54; utilization of these approaches may prove useful in the future to further understand the formation of chromatin loops in the nascent genome that we observe.

  Taken as a whole, coordinated replication of distant loop points is consistent with our findings of fast restoration of distant DNA loops on replicated DNA, suggesting that this feature of TADs’ replication may be essential to regulate re-formation of regulatory DNA architecture during S phase. Together with our previous finding that Pol II rebinds DNA within gene bodies immediately following DNA replication14 and that chromatin-modifying complexes bind to DNA quickly after replication12,13,55,56, our results here suggest that chromatin structure is largely restored quickly after passage of the replisome. Given the complex nature of gene regulation, maintenance of TFs, as well as enhancer-promoter contacts, may be critical to maintaining gene expression levels through S phase.


## Methods

### Cell culture and DNA labeling for CAA experiments
  H1299 cells were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gemini) and 2 mM L-glutamine (Corning). GM22737 cells were grown in RPMI supplemented with 10% FBS and 2 mM L-glutamine. All shown CAA results are for H1299 cells. For CAA experiments, cells were trypsinized, plated on chamber slides, and grown for two days. Cells were then pulse-labeled with medium containing 10 µM 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU, Invitrogen) and subsequently chased with label-free medium. Cells were fixed for 12 min with 3.7% formaldehyde, washed with 1x phosphate buffered saline (PBS), permeabilized for 10 min with 0.25% Triton X-100, and washed with PBS.


### Click Reaction
  Prior to the click reaction, cells were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 10 min. Cells were then subjected to click reaction in 0.1x PBS containing (in the following order): copper (II) sulfate (final concentration 2 mM; Acros Organics), biotin azide (final concentration 5 µM; Invitrogen), and freshly prepared L-ascorbic acid sodium salt (final concentration 10 mM; Acros Organics) for 25 min at room temperature. Samples were washed with PBS and blocked for 1 h with 1x western blocking reagent (Roche) in PBS containing 1.5% donkey serum. Primary antibodies, against the protein of interest and against biotin, were diluted in 1x western blocking reagent and 0.01% Triton X-100 and incubated with samples overnight at 4 °C.


### Chromatin Assembly Assay (CAA) and Immunostaining
  CAA was performed as described14,55,56. Following overnight incubation with primary antibodies, cells were washed for 20 min with PBS. PLA was performed as described by the manufacturer (MilliporeSigma). The two PLA secondary antibodies, anti-mouse PLUS and anti-rabbit MINUS, were diluted 1:5 in 1x western blocking reagent and added to samples for 1 h at 37 °C. Samples were then washed for 10 min in Duolink II wash buffer A (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20). The ligation reaction was performed by diluting the ligation reaction buffer 1:5 in water, adding ligase enzyme 1:40, and incubating samples for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed for 10 min in wash buffer A, and then the amplification reaction was performed by diluting the amplification reaction buffer 1:5 in water and supplementing it with polymerase 1:80 and added to samples for 100 min at 37 °C. Samples were then washed in PBS.

  Following PLA, biotin was counterstained with either anti-mouse IgG or anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 and anti-biotin conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 to identify replicating nuclei. Samples were washed with PBS and mounted with Vectashield containing DAPI (Vector).

  The following antibodies were used for IF and PLA: rabbit monoclonal anti-JUN (Cell Signaling Technology, 9165), rabbit monoclonal anti-CREB (Cell Signaling Technology, 9198), rabbit monoclonal anti-ATF-2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 35031), rabbit monoclonal anti-cMYC (Cell Signaling Technology, 9402), mouse monoclonal anti-HNF3α (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-10158), mouse monoclonal anti-FKHRL1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-48348), rabbit monoclonal anti-CTCF (Cell Signaling Technology, 3418), mouse monoclonal anti-YY1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-7341), mouse monoclonal anti-STAG2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-81852), rabbit polyclonal anti-SMC1 (Bethyl Laboratories, A300-055A-T), rabbit monoclonal anti-MED12 (Cell Signaling Technology, 14360), rabbit monoclonal anti-Cyclin C (Cell Signaling Technology, 68179), rabbit polyclonal anti-biotin (Abcam, #ab1227), mouse monoclonal anti-biotin (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #200-002-211), donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 secondary (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #711-545-152), donkey anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 secondary (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #715-545-150), and mouse anti-biotin Alexa Fluor 488 secondary (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #200-542-211).


### Multiplex CAA
  Multiplex CAA was performed as described previously14. Generation of oligo-tagged secondary antibodies was carried out following manufacturer’s instructions (Sigma-Aldrich). Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 711-005-152) and donkey ant-goat IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 705-005-147) were conjugated to Oligo A and Oligo B, respectively, which generates a red PLA signal. Donkey anti-goat IgG and donkey anti-mouse IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 715-005-150) were conjugated to Oligo H and Oligo I, respectively which generates a far-red signal.

  For multiplex CAA cells were pulse labeled with 75 µM EdU. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized, conjugated with biotin, and blocked as described above for CAA. Primary antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies diluted 1:100 in 1x western blocking reagent were added to samples and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Samples were washed in Duolink II wash buffer A. The ligation reaction was then prepared by diluting the ligation reaction buffer 1:5 in water and adding ligase enzyme 1:20, and incubating samples for 30 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed for 10 min in wash buffer A, and then the amplification reaction was prepared by diluting the amplification reaction buffer 1:5 in water and supplementing it with polymerase 1:40 and added to samples for 100 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed for 10 min in wash buffer A, and then the detection reagent was prepared by diluting the dilution reaction buffer 1:5 in water. The detection solution was added to the samples, and they were incubated for 100 min at 37 °C. Samples were washed with PBS. Samples were then stained with anti-goat IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 and anti-biotin conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 to identify replicating nuclei. Slides were mounted in ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen) and covered with a #1.5 coverslip in preparation for STED imaging.

  Primary antibodies were the same as for regular CAA. Additional antibodies for multiplex CAA were goat polyclonal anti-biotin (Vector Laboratories, #SP-3000), mouse monoclonal anti-PCNA (Cell Signaling Technology, #2586), and anti-goat Alexa Fluor 488 secondary (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #200-542-211).


### Microscopy
  CAA images were acquired using an Olympus BX51 microscope equipped with a 100x objective. The microscope is mounted with an Olympus DP80 camera and connecting to a computer running cellSens software. STED images were acquired using a Leica TCS SP8 STED 3X confocal/super-resolution microscope equipped with a 100 x STED objective. Images were captured using HyD detectors. Far red PLA (excitation at 646 nm) and red PLA (excitation at 593 nm) were both depleted using the 775 nm STED depletion laser. Images were acquired using three times line averaging, an imaging speed of 400 Hz, and an image format of 2048 by 2048. Images were subsequently deconvoluted using Leica’s Lightning software.


### ChIP
  Approximately 5 million GM22737 cells were used per ChIP experiment. Cells were crosslinked in 1% formaldehyde (16% methanol-free stock, Fisher) for 10 min while rocking at room temperature. Following crosslinking, samples were quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 0.125 M and rocking for five minutes at room temperature. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and then washed three times with cold PBS. Nuclei were then isolated by resuspending in nuclear extraction buffer A (20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 10 mM EDTA, 0.25% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitors [Roche]), rocked for 10 min at 4 °C, and pelleted at 1300 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in nuclear extraction buffer B (50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and protease inhibitors), rocked for 10 min at 4 °C, and then nuclei were collected by centrifugation for 10 min at 300 x g at 4 °C. Nuclei were resuspended in sonication buffer (40 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, and protease inhibitors) and allowed to incubate on ice for 10 min. Samples were then transferred to sonication tubes and sonicated until the average fragment size was less than 500 bp. Samples were supplemented with NaCl (150 mM final concentration) and Triton X-100 (1% final concentration) and spun at max speed for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, 5% was stored as input material, and the rest was applied to Protein A/G magnetic beads (MedChemExpress) pre-bound to antibodies and allowed to incubate overnight at 4 °C with rocking.

  The following day the beads were washed with 1 mL of each of the following chilled buffers: Low Salt Immune Complex Wash Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.1), 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS), High Salt Immune Complex Wash Buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.1), 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS), LiCl Immune Complex Wash Buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.1), 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 1% deoxycholate), and twice with TE (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 1 mM EDTA). Following washes, material was eluted by rocking for 15 min at room temperature in 250 µl of elution buffer (0.1 M sodium bicarbonate, 1% SDS). This was repeated and the two eluates were combined for a final volume of 500 µl. Following addition of 10 µl 5 M NaCl, 10 µl 0.5 M EDTA, and 20 µl 1 M Tris-HCl [pH 6.8], crosslinking was reversed by incubating at 65 °C for 16 h in the presence of 2 µg proteinase K. DNA was then purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation and analyzed by real-time PCR (qPCR).


### Re-ChIP
  PCNA re-ChIP assays were performed as previously described14 using approximately 75 million GM22737 cells per experiment. Cells were crosslinked and immunoprecipitated as described above using anti-CTCF, anti-YY1, or anti-IgG as a negative control. Protein-DNA complexes were eluted in 35 µl TE supplemented with 50 mM DTT and 0.125% SDS at 37 °C for 25 min. The elution was repeated, and the two eluates were combined (total volume 70 µl) and diluted 15-fold in re-ChIP dilution buffer (15 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.1), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100), supplemented with 10 µg salmon sperm DNA and 0.2% BSA. Five percent of material was saved as ChIP material, and the rest was divided in half and incubated overnight at 4 °C with either biotinylated anti-PCNA or biotinylated-IgG. (Antibodies were biotinylated with Pierce Antibody Biotinylation Kit for IP according to manufacturer’s instructions.) The following day, complexes were captured by incubation with streptavidin magnetic beads (Pierce) for 2 h at room temperature. Complexes were washed, eluted, and reverse cross-linked as described above for ChIP. DNA was purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation and analyzed by qPCR.

  ChIP and re-ChIP experiments were performed using the following antibodies: rabbit monoclonal anti-CTCF (Cell Signaling Technology, 3418), rabbit polyclonal anti-YY1 (Active Motif, 61779), rabbit polyclonal IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #315-005-003), and rabbit polyclonal anti-PCNA14. Sequences of PCR primers used to analyze ChIP and re-ChIP can be found in Supplementary Table 1.


### Chromatin conformation capture (3C) and nascent 3C
  Nascent 3C protocol was developed from existing 3C protocols31,32. For each experiment, approximately 25 million GM22737 cells were labeled with 50 μM EdU for either 10 min or 40 min. Cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, quenched with 125 mM glycine, and pelleted by centrifugation for 5 min at 300 x g. Cells were washed twice in cold 1x PBS and then resuspended in cellular lysis buffer (10 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% IGEPAL CA-630, protease inhibitors) and lysed by dounce homogenization with pestle B for 15 passes. Nuclei were pelleted by centrifugation for 5 min at 300 x g, resuspended in 1 mL of 1.2 x NEBuffer r2.1 digestion buffer (New England Biolabs, B6002S) supplemented with 30 μL 10% SDS, and then incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour while rocking. Then, 100 μL 20% Triton X-100 was added to each sample, and samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour while rocking. Next, 50 μL HindIII (100,000 units/mL; New England Biolabs, R0104T) was added, and samples were incubated overnight at 37 °C while rocking.

  The following morning, 160 μL 10% SDS was added to each sample and each sample was incubated for 25 min at 65 °C. Each sample was then diluted with 8.3 mL 1.15X T4 ligation buffer (57.5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 11.5 mM MgCl2, 1.15 mM ATP, and 11.5 mM DTT) and supplemented with 400 μL 20% Triton X-100. Samples were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Then, 30 μL T4 ligase (400,000 units/mL; New England Biolabs, M0202L) was added and samples were incubated at 16 °C for 4 h followed by 30 min at room temperature. Samples were then reverse crosslinked overnight at 65 °C in the presence of 1 mg proteinase K. The following morning, RNA was degraded by incubation for 45 min at 37 °C in the presence of 50 μg RNase A. DNA was then purified by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation.

  DNA were resuspended in 1 mL of dH2O and 50 μL was saved as 3C material. The remaining material was split in half for the click chemistry reaction as described57. The following was added in this order: 805 μL dH2O, 15 μL 10X PBS, 30 μL 0.1 M copper (II) sulfate (final concentration 2 mM; Acros Organics), either 25 μL 1 mM biotin azide (final concentration 16.7 µM; Invitrogen) or 25 μL DMSO (as a negative control), and 150 μL freshly prepared L-ascorbic acid sodium salt (final concentration 10 mM; Acros Organics), and then incubated for 1 h at room temperature while rocking. Each sample was then precipitated by adding NaCl to a final concentration of 0.2 M and two volumes of ethanol.

  For each sample, 30 μL streptavidin resin (Pierce) was washed in 1x streptavidin binding and wash buffer (5 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 0.5 mM EDTA, 1 M NaCl). Samples were resuspended in 500 μL 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and diluted 1:2 in 2X streptavidin binding and wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM EDTA, 2 M NaCl). Samples were added to the streptavidin resin and incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature while rocking. Streptavidin resin was washed thrice with 1X streptavidin binding and wash buffer and then twice with 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5). Streptavidin resin bound with DNA was resuspended in 300 μL and 6 μL was used per qPCR. Primers for POU2AF1 and DDX39B were from32. Sequences of PCR primers used to analyze 3C and nascent 3C can be found in Supplementary Table 2.


### Co-immunoprecipitation
  GM22737 cells were collected by centrifugation at 300 x g for 5 min at 4 °C and then washed three times with cold PBS. The cell pellet was resuspended in nuclear extraction buffer A (20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 10 mM EDTA, 0.25% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 10 min. Cells were then lysed by Dounce homogenization using pestle B for 15 passes, and nuclei were collected by centrifuging the lysate at 1000 x g for 10 min at 4 °C. The nuclear pellet was resuspended in IP lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 300 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 5% glycerol, and protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 10 min followed by sonication to lyse nuclei. Following sonication, samples were centrifuged at max speed for 10 minutes 4 °C to pellet debris. The supernatant was diluted 1:1 in IP lysis buffer without NaCl, such that the final IP buffer concentration was 20 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 5% glycerol, and protease inhibitors. Input was saved from this material, and the rest was incubated overnight at 4 °C with rocking with either with anti-PCNA or IgG.

  The next day, samples were incubated with magnetic protein A/G beads (MedChemExpress) for 45 min at 4 °C. The immunoprecipitated material was washed with chilled buffers as follows: twice with IP buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% NP-40, 5% glycerol), once with high salt IP wash buffer (20 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.1% SDS, 5% glycerol), once with IP wash buffer, and finally once with PBS. Complexes were eluted by boiling in Laemmli buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting.


### GST pull-down
  GST and GST-PCNA were expressed in BL21 cells and purified as described by manufacturer (GenScript). Nuclear lysates were prepared as described for co-immunoprecipitation. Lysates precleared by incubation with glutathione resin and 5 μg GST at 4 °C for 2 h with rocking. Pre-cleared lysates were then incubated with 30 µg of either GST or GST-PCNA overnight at 4 °C with rocking. Glutathione resin was blocked overnight at 4 °C in 1X PBS containing 1% BSA, 1X western blocking reagent, and 1 mg/mL salmon sperm DNA. The resin was then incubated with GST or GST-PCNA containing lysates for 45 min at 4 °C to capture GST. The resin was washed as described for co-immunoprecipitation experiments. Complexes were eluted by boiling in Laemmli buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blotting.


### Western blotting
  Samples were electrophoresed on a 4–20% gradient gel (Bio-Rad) and then wet transferred onto PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membranes were blocked for 30 minutes with 10% milk in Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBS-T; 10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 0.2% Tween-20). The primary antibodies were diluted in 5% milk in TBS-T and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rocking. The following day the membranes were washed thrice with 1% milk in TBS-T and then incubated in secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature with rocking. The membranes were washed thrice in TBS-T and then detected using SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Scientific) and imaged on a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Uncropped blot images can be found in Supplementary Fig. 3.


### Statistical Analysis
  Prism software was used for graphing and statistical analysis. Significance throughout the paper is listed as: ns = not significant, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

  For CAA experiments, slides were first examined to make sure that PLA signals are detected only in the EdU labeled nuclei. Next, all EdU labeled nuclei were examined to make sure that they contain approximately the same number of PLA signals. Finally, PLA signals were counted and used for statistical analysis from 3 biological replicates. Statistical significance was assessed using a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc analysis or by Student’s two-way, unpaired t-test.

  Multiplex CAA experiments were quantified by counting the number of overlaps compared to the total number of PLA foci for the given transcription protein from at least three biological replicates. All data are presented as average ± s.d.

  Experiments analyzed by western blotting were performed in biological triplicate.
