# Genome-Wide Chromatin Structure Changes During Caste Differentiation in Honeybees


## Abstract
  Three-dimensional (3D) chromatin architecture is an important transcription regulator which is linked to gene expression regulation and correct developmental programs, yet little is known that the 3D chromatin architecture within honeybees and how it influences gene expression and methylation in caste differentiation. Here, we report that larvae at 96 h after hatching (L5) contain the highest number of known caste differentiation genes, and that constitutes the critical window determining the final phenotypic outcomes. We also show that DNA methylation pattern is conserved and gene body methylation is thought to contribute to phenotypic plasticity. Then, we determine the 3D organization of honey bee chromosomes at 5 kb resolution. Although almost 87% regions remained in the same compartments, TAD boundary insulation increased during larvae development, especially in worker larvae at 96 h after hatching (WL5). The insulator protein, CP190, CTCF-L and vtd/Rad21 appear to be indispensable in defining these topologically associating domain boundaries. Furthermore, Mrjp1, a member of the MRJP/yellow family, is also involved in regulating larval development. These results provide key insights into caste differentiation and establish a fundamental dataset for the future in-depth characterization of epigenetic features.


## 1. Introduction
  Many organisms have the capacity to produce contrasting organismal outcomes from one genotype using intricate developmental cues [1,2]. This captivating biological phenomenon known as phenotypic plasticity is considered one of the most interesting albeit poorly understood properties of biological systems. It is found in most striking representation in eusocial insects, such as ants and honey bees.

  Honey bees (Apis mellifera), as insect pollinators, play a crucial role in most ecosystems and strongly influence ecological relationships [3]. Honey bees are eusocial insects forming two phenotypically distinct castes: i.e., short-lived functionally sterile workers and long-lived fertile queens [4]. Despite their identical genomes, queens and workers exhibit tremendous differences in reproductive capacity, morphology, longevity, and behaviors. These differences largely depend on feeding nutrition during the early larval stage (within the first 96 h) [5]. During the initial 72 h post-hatching, both queen larvae (QL) and worker larvae (WL) were provided with a certain amount of nutritious jelly. Nonetheless, the worker jelly contains lower concentration of sugars and a few other ingredients than queen food known as royal jelly [6,7]. Larvae committed to the worker caste are then shifted to a diet comprised of nectar and pollen, whereas queen-destined larvae continue feeding exclusively on royal jelly [3]. After 96 h, brood cells are sealed, and larval feeding ceases entirely until pupation is complete. Therefore, the honey bee genome exemplifies environmentally induced phenotypic plasticity, where diet determines the emergence of discrete caste phenotypes from a single genome and represents one of the most striking examples of developmental plasticity in any phylum [3].

  Various mechanisms are proposed for this phenotypic plasticity in honey bees. RNA sequencing [8] or combined with genomics and epigenomics (e.g., DNA methylation [9,10,11], histone modification [3,12,13], RNA m6A modification [14]) were implemented to unravel the intricate genetic mechanism of phenotypic plasticity in honey bees. Tian et al. found that the compartmentalization and CTCF binding peaks generally weakened upon DNA methylation dioxygenase Tet triple knock-out (Tet-TKO), which resulted in a weakened topologically associating domain (TAD) structure and depletion of long-range chromatin loops. Previous studies showed that histone post-translational modifications (including methylation and acetylation) are critical to shaping the chromatin structure and regulating gene expression [15,16,17,18]. Overall, these studies reveal distinct epigenomic characteristics are not independent layers of regulation but act cooperatively, via direct and indirect pathways, to orchestrate the hierarchical folding of chromatin. Nevertheless, because these studies mainly focus on the linear or local epigenomic changes, it remains unclear how the three-dimensional (3D) organization of the genome contributes to the initiation and progression of caste-specific phenotypic plasticity in honeybees.

  Within eukaryotic cell nucleus, genomic DNA is hierarchically folded and spatially organized to form a complex 3D structure, including A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), and chromatin loops [19,20]. Chromatin conformation capture techniques, such as high-resolution chromosome conformation (Hi-C), have been developed to probe the 3D genome organization of genomes at high resolution and reveal gene regulatory mechanisms [21,22]. Nowadays, these approaches have been widely applied to multiple species, involving cancer development, spermatogenesis, folliculogenesis and silk secretion [23,24,25,26]. Although the higher-order genome folding of 3D genome architecture is conserved from insects to mammals, 3D genomics studies have uncovered that A/B compartment switching and TADs dissolution and re-establishment still occur during development [27]. Moreover, the 3D genomic organization and genome alterations can influence each other [24,28,29]. These findings implicated that 3D genome dynamic changes may represent an important but less-explored regulatory layer, particularly in honey bee caste differentiation.

  Based on linear DNA sequencing technology is still difficult to clear integrate the mechanism of phenotypic plasticity in honey bees. The phenotypic plasticity is commonly coupled with genomic alterations such as gene expression changes and methylated modification. It is important to characterize the spatial disorganization of the honey bee larvae genome and determine its functional consequences. In this study, we investigate the transcriptomic dynamics and DNA methylation of QL and WL at three key developmental stage (48 h, 72 h and 96 h after hatching) and generate higher resolution chromatin contact maps for QL and WL using in situ Hi-C sequencing. We also reported changes in gene expression associated with the disorganized 3D genome, with the aim of better understanding the molecular mechanism of phenotypic plasticity in honey bees.


## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Sampling
  All samples were obtained from Apis mellifera ligustica honeybee colonies. The queen was artificially inseminated with semen from a single drone, and then lays eggs normally. To obtain WL of known age, frames containing eggs were removed from healthy hives and placed as soon as possible in an incubator at 35 °C and ∼80% humidity, in a warm room (at least 30 °C), according to Wojciechowski et al.’s methods [3]. The locations of individual newly hatched larvae were marked on a sheet, and after that the frames were immediately returned to their original hives. The exact time was recorded. After hours, the frames were transferred back to a warm room and the larvae were collected at 48 h (L3), 72 h (L4) and 96 h (L5), respectively. To obtain QL of known age, standard queen raising techniques were used [3,30].


### 2.2. RNA-Seq Experiments and Analysis
  Total mRNA with polyA tail was extracted from three biological replicates at each of the three stages (48 h, 72 h, and 96 h after hatching) in both QL and WL, and reverse-transcribed to cDNA for sequencing. The RNA-seq was performed by the Smart-Seq2 method [31]. Qualified libraries were then loaded on Illumina Hiseq platform for PE150 sequencing. Sequencing libraries were created according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing data were aligned to the Apis mellifera reference genome (4.5) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) [32]. String Tied (v1.3.2.d) was then used to assemble transcripts from the mapped reads [33]. FPKM for each gene were calculated. Briefly, the DEGs were identified using DESeq2 (v1.20.0) [34](https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html, accessed on 4 April 2025; local dispersion fit) based on the raw count data, following the standard DESeq2 workflow. Prior to hypothesis testing, the “independent filtering” function within DESeq2 was employed to automatically filter out genes with low expression levels. Normalization was performed using size factors method within DESeq2 to correct for differences in sequencing depth between samples. Trended dispersion estimation was performed to improve the accuracy of differential testing. p-value used a negative binomial distribution calculation model. The effect sizes forpairwise comparisons across the six stages were reported with 95% CIs. The significant DEGs were screened with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and |log2fold change| ≥ 1 as cutoffs.


#### 2.2.1. Identifying Stage-Specific Expression Genes Set with JS Score
  To better identify stage-specific expression genes in each condition, we further calculated a tissue specificity score for each transcript using an entropy-based metric that relies on Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence according to Cabili’s methods [35]. This specificity metric (ranging from 0 to 1) quantifies the similarity between a transcript’s expression pattern across tissues and another predefined pattern that represents the extreme case in which a transcript is expressed only in one tissue. Thus, a perfect tissue-specific pattern will be scored as JS = 1.


#### 2.2.2. K-Means Clustering with DEGs
  Firstly, we calculated mean log2 FPKM value (add 0.001 to avoid zero) of each gene in each condition, respectively. Then, we used K-means R function to obtain 6 clusters with all DEGs between 15 comparable groups of pairs (iter.max = 10,000, scale by samples for each gene before run K-means). The heatmap plot was generated by pheatmap R package 1.0.13.


#### 2.2.3. GO and KEGG Functional Analysis
  The GO (Gene Ontology, http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, http://www.kegg.jp/) [36] enrichment of DEGs was implemented by the hyper-geometric test (Fisher’s exact test), in which p-value is calculated and adjusted as q-value, and data background is genes in the whole genome. Only GO and KEGG terms with q < 0.05 were considered significant.


### 2.3. WGBS Experiments and Analysis
  Genomic DNA was isolated from three biological replicates in each of the six groups with the Micro Elute Genomic DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcoss, GA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The genomic DNA library was constructed using the Swift Bioscience Accel-NGS (next generation sequencing) Methyl-Seq DNA Library Kit. Subsequently, library quality was assessed on the Agilent 2100, and 150 bp paired-end sequencing of each sample were performed on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform.

  The clean reads after quality control were aligned to the Apis mellifera reference genome (4.5) using Bismark (v0.16.3) [37]. The methylation information was extracted after filtering the duplicate reads using (bismark_methylation_extractor --no_overlap) and visualized in Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) [38]. Bisulfite conversion efficiency was estimated as the proportion of cytosine reads matching reference cytosine positions. The genome was partitioned into 10 kb bins for inter-sample methylation correlation analysis and 3.2 Mb bins for Circos plots. Methylated and unmethylated CpG, CHG, and CHH sites was counted for all samples. Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between QL and WL were calculated with the R package-MethylKit 3.23 (1000-bp sliding window, 500-bp overlap, fisher’s exact test and FDR < 0.05) [39]. DMRs were identified using DSS, requiring an absolute methylation difference (ΔmCG) ≥ 0.1, p-value < 0.00005, and at least 3 CpG sites per region [40]. Hypergeometric test was used to calculate the enrichment scores and significant p-values of these DMRs in different chromatin states.


### 2.4. In Nucleus Hi-C Experiments and Analysis
  The Hi-C experiment was performed from one individual at each of the three stages in QL and WL, following the in situ Hi-C protocol [41]. For each individual, two independent libraries were generated as technical replicates to assess library construction and sequencing stability. The Hi-C libraries were amplified by 8–10 cycles of PCR and sequenced in Illumina HiSeq instrument with 2 × 150 bp reads.


#### 2.4.1. Hi-C Data Processing
  Mapping, filtering, correction and generation of Hi-C matrices were performed using HiC-Pro (v2.7.8) [42] (https://github.com/nservant/HiC-Pro, accessed on 24 June 2025). Briefly, the high-quality read pairs were mapped independently to the Apis mellifera reference genome 4.5 using Bowtie2 [43] (end-to-end algorithm and ‘‘-very-sensitive’’ option) with HiC-Pro (v2.7.8) [42] default parameters. After filtering, valid pairs were used to generate raw and ICE normalized matrices [42,44] at 5 kb, 10 kb, and 100 kb bin resolution.


#### 2.4.2. A/B Compartments Analysis
  We identified A/B compartments at 20 kb resolution using the matrix2compartment module in cworld software (https://github.com/dekkerlab/cworld-dekker/, accessed on 15 June 2025) as previously described. The expected contact values were calculated by LOWESS smoothed averaging of intrachromosomal interactions. Log2-transformed observed/expected (O/E) ratios were generated, and Pearson correlations of bin interaction patterns were obtained for PCA. Chromatin was partitioned into two spatially distinct compartments according to the sign of the first principal component. The compartment with higher gene density was defined as compartment A, and the other as compartment B.


#### 2.4.3. TAD Analysis
  TADs were identified with 10 kb resolution ICE-normalized interaction matrices using a Perl script matrix2insulation.pl (https://github.com/dekkerlab/crane-nature-2015, accessed on 23 June 2025 ) [44]. Insulation Scores (IS) [45] were calculated for each chromosome bin and valleys of IS identified TAD boundaries with --is = 20, --ids = 10, --nt = 0.1. TADs smaller than 30 kb were filtered in our analysis. TAD structure closeness was measured by calculating TAD scores (internal contacts)/(external contacts).


#### 2.4.4. Loops Analysis
  The loops were called by Juicer HICCUPS [46] (https://github.com/aidenlab/juicer, accessed on 27 June 2025) using parameter setting “-r 5000 -f 0.01 --ignore_sparsity” and valid pairs. For Hi-C resolution calculation, the whole genome was divided into bins of the same size (1M, 500k, 200k, 100k, 40k, 20k, 10k, 5k, 1k), and then vaild pairs were used to stat the coverage of every bin. We sorted the bins in descending order according to the coverage of bins and stat the depth of 75th, 80th, 90th. At a specific resolution, resolution adequacy was determined when the 80th bin depth minimum reached 1000 reads. This criterion achieved 5 kb resolution, enabling loop analysis.


#### 2.4.5. Defining Chromatin States and Distinguish TAD Activities
  Bam files were obtained after bowtie2 [43] alignment; we used ChromHMM [47] (http://compbio.mit.edu/ChromHMM/, accessed on 3 July 2025) software to identify 15 states. Briefly speaking, first, we ran “BinarizeBam” mode with 200 bp to obtain binary files, and used “LearnModel” model to obtain segments of whole-genome regions.


#### 2.4.6. Motif Analysis
  De novo motif discovery: As TAD boundaries are relatively conservative among QL and WL, we used WL5 sample TAD borders (10 kb) as candidate sequences to identify motifs by using meme-suite [48] (http://meme-suite.org/, accessed on 8 August 2025); specifically, Meme and Dreme were used de novo to discover motif logos, and tomtom was used to annotate them. Finally, only two motif logos were obtained with default parameters named CTCF (MA0531.1) and vfl (MA1462.1), respectively. A total of 143 insect PFMs (non-redundant) motifs were download from JASPAR202060 [49] (http://jaspar.genereg.net/, accessed on 14 August 2025) as the database for annotation.

  Enrichment motif analysis: MotifScan [50] (http://bioinfo.sibs.ac.cn/shaolab/MAmotif/index.php, accessed on 20 August 2025) was used to analyze enriched motifs and sites for candidate promoter regions with 1 × 10−4 p-value cutoff, where all honeybee promoter regions were regarded as the control. “genomecompile” and “motifcompile” commands were run to obtain a reference index and background motif score cutoff under different p-values.


### 2.5. Candidate Genes Identification
  The Mrjp1 coding sequence (1299 bp, NM_001011579.1) was synthesized and cloned into pcDNA3.1 vector. To obtain the same age larvae, the queen was caged to restrict it from laying eggs on a specific comb for 12 h. On the fourth day after queen caging, the randomly selected larvae were grafted from the brood comb into a 48-well culture plate. This time was defined as 0 h. All obtained Apis mellifera WLs were reared in the laboratory following the method described by Schmehl et al. [51]. Feeding was conducted with 2 μL Mrjp1 overexpressed (1 μg/μL) or blank vector at 48th, 72nd and 96th larvae. Twenty-four hours after the third feeding, six larvae each group were collected, and six larvae of each group were left to continue growing normally until emergence. Morphometric characteristics (body weight, tergite 3 length, tergite 4 length and basitarsus length) were measured at 24 h according to Ruttner and Meixner (Table S1) [52,53]. Then, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare different groups.

  Total RNA was extracted from larvae samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and then reverse-transcribed to cDNA using M-MLV FIRST STRAND KIT (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) and oligo (dT)18 primer following the supplier’s instructions. Transcript quantification was performed using SYBR Green mix (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany) and Roche LightCylcer 480 (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Roche Applied Science, Mannheim, Germany) in a standard 96-well block (20 μL reaction; incubation at 95 °C for 10 min, 45 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for10 s, annealing/extension at 60 °C for 10 s). Mrjp1 Transcript specific primer pairs (F: 5′-CACAGCCCAAGATGGAATTT-3′; R: 5′-AAGAGGACGCCACTCTTTGA-3′) were designed with Oligo 6.0 software [54]. β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression was analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. ANOVA and t-test were conducted to analyze the RNA expression in different groups using software SPSS 22.0 [55].


## 3. Results

### 3.1. Dynamic Transcriptome Differences Between QL and WL
  In honey bees, female larvae are multipotent and can develop either into workers or queens with both organisms showing distinct phenotypic features. We first depicted developmental changes in the transcriptome of QL and WL at 48 h (L3), 72 h (L4) and 96 h (L5) after hatching (Figure 1a). We then generated a total of 122.89 Gb high-quality bulk RNA-seq data with three biological replicates for each group (~6.83 Gb sequences per sample) (Table S1). A total of 10,331 genes (73.32% of the annotated genes in the genome) had evident expression (fragments per kilobase million [FPKM] ≥ 0.5 at least at one instar time) during the QL and WL caste differentiation, which typically had a developmental stage-dependent pattern and is highly reproducible within biological replicates (Spearman’s r > 0.80) (Figure S1a,b and Table S2). Comparisons among developmental stages revealed that the L4 stage had the largest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with 1215 genes upregulated in QL and 760 genes upregulated in WL (Figure S1c). This is consistent with the result of Zhang et al., that more DEGs were up-regulated in QL than WL [56]. Next, a functional annotation analysis was performed for the DEGs between QL and WL (Tables S3 and S4). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis suggested that these DEGs are primarily involved in cell part, cellular process and binding (Figure S1d). Overrepresented GO-BP terms were related to “estrogen biosynthetic and metabolic process”, “lipid metabolic process” and “organic acid metabolic process” (Figure S1e). Overrepresented KEGG pathways were “Steroid hormone biosynthesis” “Apoptosis” and “Glycosphingolipid biosynthesis” (Figure S1e). These results highlight the differences in biological functions occurring in honey bee caste differentiation.

  Additionally, we investigated possible developmental scenarios for gene expression throughout caste differentiation in QL and WL and identified six main patterns involving 3403 genes using the maSigPro-GLM algorithm (Figure 1b and Table S5). We found that a total of 1538 genes (535, 386 and 617 genes in cluster 1, 4 and 6, respectively) were upregulated in WL. In contrast, a total of 1865 genes (294, 391 and 1180 genes in cluster 2, 3 and 5, respectively) were upregulated in QL. The genes mostly belong to cluster 5 and they show a reverse expression pattern at L4 stage, with high expression in QL4 and low expression in WL4. However, we did not find any genes known to be associated with caste differentiation in cluster 5, and most of these genes are of unknown function. Functional enrichment analysis suggested that these genes primarily involved nucleic acid metabolism and small-molecule compound metabolism, which could affect gene expression during growth and development of organisms (Figure 1c). The larvae had basically completed the differentiation of queen bee and worker bee at L5. We speculated that the L4 stage lays the foundation for L5 the final caste formation stage, and that the L4 differential genes should play an important role in caste differentiation and need to be explored more deeply in the future.

  Considering the limited tissue number and the large number of low-expression genes and zeros in RNA-seq, the commonly used metrics, such as Tau, were not suitable for honey bees [57]. To confirm that over-representative functions of stage-specific genes align with specific physiological changes during caste differentiation in honey bees, we further calculated a stage specificity score for each transcript using an entropy-based metric that relies on Jensen–Shannon (JS) divergence. Based on this measure, a comprehensive analysis of differentially expressed genes and stage-specific genes (JS score > 0.3) found that QL and WL exhibit similar gene expression pattern, most stage-specific genes being highly expressed in their respective developmental stages, with only one gene down-regulated in QL4 and QL5 (Figure S2a,b and Table S6). This result showed that using the JS score method to analyze specific genes expressed in the honeybee larvae stage is reliable. Further, we performed functional annotation analysis to find specific overrepresented functional terms for stage-specific genes (Figure S2c, Tables S7 and S8). Neural development is the most significant overrepresented term at all stages of WL and in the QL3 stage. The reproduction process and tissue development were highly overrepresented at QL4. At the QL5 stage, reproductive behavior and morphogenesis were highly overrepresented. In addition, we found the most genes related to caste differentiation at the L5 stage, for example Y-h, Mrjp2, Jhbp-1, Jhe, Mrjp7, Mrjp8 in WL5 and Br-c, E74, Y-e3, Y-y, Mrjp1, Mrjp3 in QL5 [58]. This aligns with the finding that L5 contains the highest number of known caste differentiation genes in K-means cluster, indicating that dynamical gene expression with a developmental stage-dependent manner is related to functional divergences during caste differentiation from L3 to L5.


### 3.2. DNA Methylation Profiles of QL and WL
  DNA methylation is one of the main epigenetic factors regulating gene expression in eukaryotes. To investigate the role of DNA methylation in caste differentiation in honey bees, QL and WL were isolated for whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS). After removing, bisulfite sequencing (BS-seq) yielded 336 Gb of clean data from 18 samples (three biological replicates for each of the six groups) (Table S1). To confirm the accuracy and reliability of the collected samples, we performed a correlation analysis for all samples. The results showed that the replicates from the same groups clustered relatively closely and the samples from the same stage correlated strongly (r2 ≥ 0.94), indicating the high repeatability of the BS-seq results (Figure S3a).

  Next, the global DNA methylation status of QL and WL was investigated. The results revealed that the global methylation levels were weakly in honey bees (4.31% in QL and 4.35% in WL, Figure S3b). The classification of mC contexts (mCG, mCHH, or mCHG) for each sample showed that the proportion of mCGs was the highest, and was mainly identified in gene body regions (Figure S3c–e). This indicated that mCG is the main type of mC, and this DNA methylation pattern is conserved during caste differentiation in honey bees.

  Differentially methylated regions (DMRs) are important markers of epigenetic changes, and are involved in the regulation of many biological processes. In this study, the number of DMRs (ΔmCG ≥ 0.1, FDR < 0.05) was 991, 1007 and 756, respectively. Compared to L3 and L4, a large number of DMRs were down-methylated in QL5 (Figure 2a,b). Then, we identified specific DMRs (only detected in one larval stage) in QL and WL to explore the transient epigenetic reprogramming events. We identified a total of 365 specific DMRs, including 295 hyper-DMRs in QL and 70 hyper-DMRs in WL (Figure 2c and Table S9). The DMRs were distributed on all chromosomes, mainly in Chr1 (Figure 2d) Among the 365 DMRs, 333 DMRs were annotated in the gene body region (Figure 2e). We also identified that the hyper-exon DMRs were associated with more highly expressed genes (Figure 2f). These findings suggested that the global methylation is low in honey bees, yet it may suggest the involvement of gene expression in honey bee caste differentiation.


### 3.3. Genome-Wide Chromatin Interaction Maps for QL and WL
  To study the genome-wide chromatin interactions of the phenotypic plasticity, we performed in situ Hi-C experiments of QL and WL with the restriction enzyme (MboI). The Hi-C data indicated an average depth of 5 kb. Sequencing the Hi-C library generated 5.86 billion raw reads, which yielded 5.66 billion high-quality read-pairs after all filtration steps (Figure S4a and Table S1). The stratum-adjusted correlation coefficient heatmap plot of Hi-C libraries were calculated by hicrep, and the results showed that the data sets were highly correlated (Figure 3a, Pearson’s correlation, r > 0.91). All samples showed a strong decrease interaction frequency with an increase in the genomic distance (Figure S4a). Like other species, the proportion of intra-chromosome interaction (cis-) identified in honey bees exceeds that of inter chromosome interaction (trans-), although the interaction patterns vary across different instars (Figure S4b). Additionally, we identified the resolution depth 1 kb to 1 Mb across all different stages and the intrachromosomal interaction ratio of bin resolution at 100 kb in all chromosome (Figure S4c,d). Then, we obtained chromatin interaction heatmaps at 100 kb resolution for each sample using combined data of replicates (Figure S5). After analyzing the Z-score difference in genome-wide chromosomal interaction at the same stage, we found that there are many inter-chromosomal interactions (i.e., non-diagonal regions, which occupied a larger proportion of the heatmap) in the L4 stage, whereas only Chr1 displayed pronounced differences in intra-chromosomal long-range interactions. In addition, we detected extensive differences in intra-chromosomal interactions at the L5 stage, suggesting remodeling of local chromatin folding, potentially involving TAD–internal interactions and enhancer–promoter loops (Figure 3b–d and Figure S6). In general, we propose that L5 is a key period in determining the final phenotypic outcome.


### 3.4. A/B Compartments Are Conserved During Larvae Development
  To elucidate the multiscale rewiring of chromatin architecture and its influence during caste differentiation, at the sub-chromosome level, we firstly characterized the compartment partitioning scenarios and examined associations between transcriptional features/methylation levels and chromatin compartment states. In honey bees, we used ICE-normalized interaction matrices at 20 kb resolution to detect chromatin compartment types and obtained 2205 chromosome bins. The genome consists of actively transcribed compartments A and inactive compartments B (Figure 4a). There are 16 chromosomes in genome of honey bees, at around 229.12 Mb [59]. Replicates of each stage shared similar A and B compartment patterns, and ~47.27% of the whole genome comprised Compartment A bins in QL and ~48.44% in WL (Figure 4b). Previous studies showed that the switching of compartments A and B between normal cells and cancer cells is associated with corresponding changes of gene expression [60]. We investigated whether this phenomenon is exhibited in honey bee caste differentiation. Finally, 909 A compartments were found associated with up-regulated gene expression and 1002 B compartments were found associated with down-regulated gene expression (Figure 4c). Most genomic regions (87%) remained in the same compartments from L3 to L5 during caste differentiation process in QL and WL. It indicated that the compartments A and B are highly conservative during the caste differentiation process. We identified a substantial level of compartmental switching in the caste differentiation process from L3 to L5. In these regions, most switching was unidirectional (74.15%): from A to B (“AAB” and “ABB”) or from B to A (“BAA” and “BBA”) (Figure 4c). We were also interested in the association between the reprogramming of these epigenetic modifications, gene expression and 3D chromatin structure. We analyzed the association between the methylated CpGs and gene expressions. We found that compartments A have higher gene expression and methylation than compartments B, for example in chromosome 6 (Figure 4d–f). These results indicated that A/B compartments are globally conserved, but local compartment switching may contribute to caste-specific gene expression and epigenetic regulation.


### 3.5. TAD Boundary Insulation Is Increased During Larvae Development, Especially in WL5
  At the sub-megabase scale, the local chromatin architecture can be characterized by TAD. To investigate the changes in TAD in caste differentiation, we used an insulation score (IS) approach to identify TAD boundaries at 10 kb resolution [45,61]. As a result, we found that the genome was shown to consist of small contiguous TADs in honey bees, a property also observed in Drosophila and mammals [61]. To identify all of the sub-TADs’ borders, a high-sensitivity threshold was used. A total of 2085 TADs were subsequently detected in QL and WL, and the TAD number was the highest at L5 (Figure S7a and Table S10). The identified TADs exhibited the smallest median size in WL5 (552 kb in WL5) (Figure S7b). Furthermore, we observed a stronger TAD boundary insulation at WL than that in QL (Figure 5a). Next, we intersected their TAD boundaries to test whether the TAD boundaries were gained or lost in QL and WL. We found that the precise location of our TAD boundaries exhibited a high degree of overlap (at least 91%) in QL and WL (Figure S7c). Only a small portion of TAD boundaries were unique to their period. Analyzing the DEGs at L5-specific TAD boundaries demonstrated that these genes could be candidate genes affecting caste differentiation (Table S11). In addition, we also found that the methylated CpGs were mostly located at TAD boundaries. However, the results showed that the methylated CpGs of genes at TAD boundaries did not show significant changes. Overall, most TADs identified in QL and WL were stable during caste differentiation.

  To assess the kinetics of intra-TAD interactions during the larval development, we calculated the domain score and signal (defined as the TAD compactness and reflect the self-interactive tendencies within a domain) of cTADs of each sample. Using the TAD interaction strength from the WL5 as a reference, we observed a reverse interaction trend in QL and WL, and the relative variance of TAD signal and TAD score were significantly enhanced and increased at WL5 (Figure 5b,c). For example, the TAD borders in Chr 16 (0–7,207,165) gradually became more and more clear, indicating increased spatial segregation of local chromatin when the caste differentiated (Figure 5d). This is consistent with our analysis of TAD characteristic, including the number, the length and the conservation. This further confirms that the strongest chromatin interaction occurs at this stage (Figure 3d).


### 3.6. Genes Expression and Methylation at TAD Boundary
  To further explore what precisely regulates the insulation strength of TAD boundaries in honey bees, we used Meme&Dreme to perform Motif de novo analysis of the TAD boundary sequences, and then motif name annotation on the identified motifs using tomtom. Then, we successfully detected the top two motif sequences with dreme: CTCF and vfl (top 1 and 2, respectively) (Figure 6a). By homology alignment, we found that CP190, CTCF-L, and vtd/Rad21 were highly homologous to the motif sequence, which may play an analogous role in honey bees as of in Drosophila. For the purpose of determining the intergenic interaction relationships between genes and identifying the genes with central roles, we performed co-expression analysis of TAD-associated key genes at absolute Spearman correlation coefficient value (abs_SCC) > 0.7. A total of 83 genes exhibited strong expression correlations with all of the three predicted motifs (Figure 6b). We also observed that there were 15 genes strongly linked with the motifs (abs_SCC > 0.9), which may play an important role in 3D genome organization by adjusting the strength of chromatin insulation at TAD boundaries (Figure 6c and Table S12). Function enrichment analysis indicated that the top 10 GO-BP of CP190 and CTCF-L are mostly involved in RNA processing and RNA metabolism process, while vtd/Rad21 is mainly involved in the regulation of biological and cellular processes (Figure 6d). These results indicated that CTCF and vlf sites may play an important role in the formation of TAD boundaries in honey bees.


### 3.7. Candidate Genes Identification
  Royal jelly (RJ) is the exclusive dietary requirement of honey bee queens and has been reported to play a critical role in caste differentiation [62,63]. The MRJP family represents the main characteristic proteins, in which MRJP1 is the most abundant and upregulated in QL [64]. The TAD results showed that the MRJP family is located in one TAD (Chr 11: 2,540,000–2,700,000 (Table S13). As mentioned above, Mrjp1 was also located in a L5-specific TAD boundary and upregulated in QL (Table S11). Here, we overexpressed Mrjp1 in worker bees to verify its effect on larvae development. The RT-qPCR confirmed that the overexpression of Mrjp1 effectively increased its expression in WL. Facilitated by a vector, the overexpression (OE) of Mrjp1 effectively increased its expression (294% compared with control group, p < 0.01) and led to a significant increase in emergence weight 17% and tergite 3 length 12% (p < 0.05) (Figure 7). Thus, these results provide evidence that the expression of Mrjp1 has a positive effect on larvae development.


## 4. Discussion
  Sequencing technology based on linear DNA sequence still does not clearly illustrate the mechanism of phenotypic plasticity in honey bees. Therefore, we assume that the genome 3D structure changes during honeybee caste differentiation may lead to the destruction of the original TAD and changes in gene interactions. This would further affect the regulation of genes and pathways associated with phenotypic plasticity. In this study, we comprehensively analyzed RNA-seq, WGBS and Hi-C with higher resolution across a key time course of honey bees in caste differentiation. Here, we confirmed that all these three omics were involved in caste differentiation. Our results showed that the L4 stage (76 h after hatching) served as the preparation period for caste differentiation, during which the chromatin started to undergo significant changes along with notable differences in gene expression and methylation. These up-regulated DEGs and stage-specific genes identified in QL4 were mainly involved in ncRNA metabolic process, providing the material and energy foundation for cell proliferation and tissue differentiation in queen larvae, especially the rapid development of their ovaries [65,66,67]. After that, we detected many genes know to be related to caste differentiation, perhaps suggesting that the caste-specific development at L5 stage is already irreversible [14,68,69,70].

  In addition, we found that the global methylation levels in honey bee genomes are sparse and mostly restricted to gene bodies, which commonly occurs in social insects [70,71,72]. A large number of DMRs were down-methylated at the QL5 stage, while these were up-methylated in WL5. In 2012, Foret et al. also observed greater methylation in 96 h-old worker larval heads [10]. Compared with a previously published meDIP-seq methylation pattern, our results showed a similar trend after the fourth day: DNA methylation of QL was decreased, while that of WL continued to increase [72]. This provides support again to the notion that larvae 96 h after hatching (L5) were at a critical juncture, and WLs older than 4 days were not able to switch to becoming queens.

  Interestingly, with the larval development, the TAD borders became gradually defined, sharpest at WL5. The 3D structure was extremely loose at the initial stage and lacked classical structural features, such as compartment A/B and TAD [73]. With embryonic development, the genome 3D structure and high-level structure of chromosomes gradually matured and became established [74]. This indicates that the 3D genome structure is gradually established and matured along with larvae development in honey bees. In humans, mice, fruit flies and other species, TADs exhibit higher intra-domain interaction frequency than inter-domain interactions, even in nearby areas [75,76]. We believe that TAD divides the chromosomes into independent small units to ensure spatial isolation, even if TADs are very close to each other on the linearized chromosomes. As universal structural features and fundamental units within the genome, TADs provide a physical platform for gene expression. The dynamic changes of TADs ensure precision of transcriptional dynamics and the reliability of complex patterning processes [24]. Workers are responsible for various and detailed tasks, including nurturing, guarding, gathering, etc. Indeed, we observed increased TAD signal, TAD score and insulation score in WL compared to that in QL, which is consistent with the worker bee biological characteristics. Boundaries are thought to act as insulators to preclude inappropriate enhancer–promoter interactions with neighboring genes or regulatory elements [77]. The compact TAD architecture and well-defined TAD boundaries broadly maintain regulatory specificity by preventing interference between neighboring TADs [24]. Our results suggested that combinatorial effects and insulating capacity of TAD boundaries could be an important mechanism shaping gene expression patterns and enabling the fine-scale partitioning of gene functions.

  TADs provide a suitable microenvironment for fine gene expression regulation [74]. This is helpful in identify candidate genes for caste differentiation in honey bees. The MRJP/yellow family is currently recognized as playing a key role in the differentiation of honey bees. Our results indicated that overexpression of Mrjp1 can increase body-weight- and body-size-related characteristics, whereas the interference effect of Mrjp1 is not satisfactory, which may be attributed to compensatory intake from its presence in the fed diet [64]. Our TAD results showed that the MRJP/yellow family is located in the same TAD (Chr11: 2,540,000–2,700,000). There are 12 genes located in this TAD. The TAD scores of the 12 genes are all the highest at L4, indicating that these genes interact more, and more closely, during the larvae development. Of them, nine genes are members of the MRJP/yellow family. The overexpression of Mrjp1 can promote larvae development and have a positive effect on workers’ body size. In this TAD, the other three genes are L-xylulose reductase (gene id: 413567) and two unknown genes. Notably, the expression level of L-xylulose reductase in honey bee larvae is significantly higher in WL than in QL, and hundreds of times higher than that of MRJPs, particularly in L4 and L5. L-xylulose reductase converts l-xylulose into xylitol, and reduces various α-dicarbonyl compounds, thus performing a particular role in carbohydrate metabolism [78,79]. Previous studies have shown that L-xylulose reductase can help maintain stable blood sugar levels and prevents cell damage caused by hyperglycemia [80,81]. We deduced that the expression difference of L-xylulose reductase between QL and WL may stem from nutrient-mediated regulatory mechanisms during critical late-stage larval development, which could play a regulatory role in caste differentiation.

  Juvenile hormone (JH), a class of structurally unique sesquiterpenoid hormones in honey bees, plays crucial roles in regulating growth and development [82], labor division [83,84], lifespan [85], and caste differentiation [68,86]. At the critical window of caste differentiation, the JH titer threshold determines the developmental trajectory of the larvae (development into a queen or worker) [68,86]. Juvenile hormone esterase (Jhe, gene id: 406066), located in TAD Chr 3: 8,520,000–8,720,000, is the primary JH-specific degradation enzyme regulating JH titers [87,88]. Genes within this TAD may interact with JH and JHE, and collectively affect the development of honey bee larvae. Another gene in this TAD, Titin (gene id: 409981), contributes to sarcomere elasticity and extensibility, especially responses to changes in muscle length [89]. Another study by the authors explored the regulatory relationship among JH, Jhe and Titin, as well as their functions in honeybee larval development [90]. The results revealed that inhibiting Jhe expression in workers’ larvae led to a significant accumulation of JH titer during the larval stage (p < 0.01) and a marked downregulation of Titin expression (p < 0.05). Direct inhibition of Titin expression led to an increase in multiple body size traits in adult workers, including fore wing, femur and tibia length (p < 0.01). The results indicate that JH, Jhe and Titin interact with each other and jointly regulate the development of worker larvae [90].

  At the honey bee TAD borders, we obtained the significant enrichment pairs of insulator proteins, CTCF-L and vfl. This pattern is analogous to the CTCF–cohesin enrichment at mammalian TAD boundaries and BEAF-32/CP190 or Chromator in D. melanogaster. Insulator proteins are known to colocalize with numerous TAD boundaries across species [61,91,92,93]. Nevertheless, there exists a notable mechanistic discrepancy: TAD borders in mammals are occupied by the CTCF/cohesin insulating complexes, whereas such complexes are absent from D. melanogaster TAD boundaries. This implies that the border-associated elements may not be conserved among metazoans. The insulator proteins located at TAD borders are known to be critical for TAD establishment [93,94,95,96]. In Drosophila, transcription has been hypothesized to serve as a primary driver of TAD architecture, though the involvement of genomic sequences bound by architectural proteins (APs) remains debated [61,97,98]. In 2020, Rodrigo et al. provided compelling evidence that discrete genomic regions bound by APs and RNA Polymerase II contribute to TAD partitioning and transcriptional regulation in Drosophila [98]. However, whether these proteins or their homologs play a similar function in honey bees remains uncharacterized and warrants further studies.


## 5. Conclusions
  In summary, our study presents an atlas of chromatin interactions in honey bee during larvae development at 5 kb resolution. We demonstrated that higher-order chromatin architectural changes, particularly the maturation of TAD boundaries and compartmentalization, facilitate the concomitant transcriptional activities and guarantee the precision of caste differentiation. Additionally, expression changes in key functional genes such as MRJP1 and Jhe-associated genes could influence larvae development. These results imply that the very different epigenomic profiles of the worker and queen bees are not pre-established and can diverge by taking progressively different developmental pathways. It will be an interesting challenge in the future to explore the functions of genes unknown to this study and how they influence 3D genome architectural dynamics in honey bees throughout caste development, as well as analyze various epigenetic data to better understand regulatory mechanisms in phenotypic plasticity.
