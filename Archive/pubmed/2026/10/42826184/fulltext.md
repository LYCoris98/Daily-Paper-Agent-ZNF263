# Aging-associated SATB1 deficiency remodels 3D genome architecture and transcriptional programs in naive CD4+ T cells


## Abstract
  Precise three-dimensional (3D) genome organization is crucial for regulating gene expression during development, yet its role in age-related transcriptional changes and physiological decline remains elusive. Here, we show that aging reshapes chromatin architecture and gene regulation in murine naive CD4+ T cells, a quiescent T cell population essential for adaptive immunity. Aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit intrinsic transcriptional reprogramming marked by increased expression of inflammatory and T cell activation–related genes, rendering them more prone to activation. Intriguingly, these changes are accompanied by pronounced alterations in 3D genome organization, including widespread weakening of topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries and extensive enhancer-promoter rewiring. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that these age-associated structural changes can be partly attributed to the diminished expression of chromatin organizer SATB1. SATB1 colocalizes with CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) in young naive T cells to spatially constrain CTCF-binding sites. Its decline with age extends the range of CTCF-mediated interactions without altering CTCF occupancy, leading to remodeling of chromatin architecture and up-regulation of proinflammatory and proactivation genes. Conditional SATB1 deletion in naive T cells recapitulates the 3D genome and transcriptional changes observed in aging. Our findings reveal a critical role for SATB1 in maintaining 3D genome integrity in naive T cells and suggest that the dysregulation of genome architecture contributes to immune and organismal aging.


## INTRODUCTION
  The eukaryotic genome is intricately organized within the three-dimensional (3D) nuclear space through the actions of key architectural proteins such as cohesin and the CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) (1), forming hierarchical structures including A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), and enhancer-promoter (E-P) interactions (2–5). Proper genome architecture is essential for precise spatiotemporal gene regulation during development, while its disruption is associated with developmental disorders and cancer (6, 7). However, the functional significance of 3D genome organization during aging is less well understood.

  Cellular senescence, the irreversible cell-cycle arrest caused by telomere attrition or environmental insults, reflects a terminal stage of cellular aging and has long been used as an in vitro model for aging studies (8, 9). Senescence is accompanied by extensive remodeling of 3D genome architecture, particularly through the altered interactions within and among heterochromatin domains (10–15). More recently, several studies have also characterized the 3D genome organization during normal organismal aging in different tissues and primary cell types, including hematopoietic stem cells, placenta, muscle stem cells, brain tissues, and pro-B cells (16–23). Intriguingly, all these studies revealed widespread age-associated changes in 3D genome architecture at various structural levels. However, whether these age-associated 3D genome changes reflect a global, stochastic decay of structural integrity or are caused by specific age-dependent regulatory pathways is not fully understood. Moreover, the contribution of these 3D genome changes to the tissue-specific physiological decline remains largely unexplored.

  Age-related dysregulation in the immune system increases the susceptibility to infection, the incidence of autoimmune diseases, and the risk of cancer, providing an important avenue for studying transcriptional and chromatin structure remodeling in aging (24–26). Naive T cells, which have self-renewal capacity and can differentiate into various effector subsets, are central to maintaining immune competence (27, 28). Upon antigen stimulation and activation, naive CD4+ T cells secrete cytokines and differentiate into various effector T cells, including T helper cell (TH1), TH2, and TH17 subsets, which facilitate the activation and proliferation of B cells, CD8+ T cells, and macrophages, thereby promoting antibody production and enhancing cell-mediated immune responses (29, 30). Aging induces a substantial reduction in the number of naive T cells, particularly for CD8+ T cells, partly due to thymus involution (31–34). In contrast, the naive CD4+ T cell population shrinks less with age because of enhanced proliferation and activation, as well as reduced apoptosis (35–37). Despite their sustained numbers, aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit pronounced functional changes, resulting in the accumulation of dysfunctional CD4+ T cell subsets (38–41). These observations suggest that age-associated alterations in naive CD4+ T cells are a central feature of immune aging, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying these changes remain poorly understood.

  Here, we demonstrate that aging reshapes chromatin architecture and gene regulation in murine naive CD4+ T cells. Aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit intrinsic transcriptional reprogramming marked by increased expression of inflammatory and T cell activation–related genes, rendering them more prone to activation. These changes are accompanied by pronounced 3D genome alterations, including widespread weakening of TAD boundaries and extensive E-P rewiring. These structural changes cannot be attributed to age-related changes in CTCF occupancy. Instead, we identify the chromatin organizer SATB1 as an age-dependent modulator of 3D genome integrity in naive T cells. SATB1 colocalizes with CTCF in young cells to spatially constrain the CTCF-binding sites. Age-dependent reduction in SATB1 expression increased the range of CTCF-mediated interactions without affecting CTCF binding, resulting in altered chromatin architecture and aberrant gene activation in naive T cells. Together, our findings uncover a critical role for SATB1 in preserving 3D genome architecture and transcriptional fidelity in naive T cells and establish dysregulation in genome organization as an important contributor to immune and organismal aging.


## RESULTS

### Aging-associated transcriptome changes in naive CD4+ T cells
  We characterized the CD4+ T cell compartment from young (6 to 8 weeks) and aged (∼18 to 22 months) mice using flow cytometry. Consistent with previous reports, aged CD4+ T cells exhibit a reduced proportion of naive T cells, an increased proportion of memory T cells, and aberrant expression of T cell activation markers (fig. S1). To further elucidate the intrinsic changes in naive CD4+ T cells during the aging process, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on naive CD4+ T cells isolated from young and aged mice (fig. S2). Aging resulted in significant changes in the expression of 2359 genes, with 1737 (73%) up-regulated and 622 (27%) down-regulated (Fig. 1A). Down-regulated genes were enriched for functions related to myeloid cell homeostasis (Fig. 1B), while up-regulated genes were predominantly involved in inflammatory responses and T cell activation (Fig. 1C). Notably, many critical inflammation-related genes such as Ccl, Il, and tumor necrosis factor (Tnf) gene families, and T cell activation or differentiation-related genes such as Tbx21, Sos2, Jak, and Icos are significantly up-regulated in aged naive CD4+ T cells (Fig. 1D).

  To further validate these findings in humans, we analyzed published single-cell RNA-seq datasets of CD4+ T cells from young and aged individuals (42, 43). Consistent with murine data, aged human naive CD4+ T cells also exhibited higher expression levels of T cell activation and inflammation-related genes (fig. S3). Consistent with these transcriptional changes, upon in vitro stimulation with anti-CD3/CD28 antibodies, aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibited increased CD44 expression and interleukin-2 (IL-2) production compared with young cells (fig. S4). Together, these results suggest that during the aging process, the relatively quiescent naive T cells undergo intrinsic transcriptional reprogramming that may predispose these cells to aberrant activation responses upon stimulation.

  To investigate the causes for the increased expression of activation and inflammation-related genes, we profiled chromatin accessibility and histone acetylation in naive CD4+ T cells isolated from young and aged mice. ATAC-seq revealed ∼79,000 open chromatin regions in young cells and ∼73,000 in aged cells (fig. S5). Among these accessible regions, 1780 were identified as differentially accessible regions (DARs), with 333 more accessible and 1447 less accessible in aged cells (Fig. 1E). Changes in chromatin accessibility exhibit a moderate correlation with gene expression changes (Fig. 1F). However, upon further analysis, we found that only 250 of the 2359 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were associated with DARs (Fig. 1G). Similarly, CUT&Tag profiling identified ∼19,000 and ∼12,000 H3K27ac peaks in young and aged cells, respectively (fig. S6). Among these sites, 787 exhibited significant changes in H3K27ac signals (Fig. 1H) but did not correlate with transcriptional changes (Fig. 1, I and J). Together, these findings suggest that alterations in chromatin accessibility and histone acetylation are unlikely to be the primary driver of the aging-associated transcriptional changes observed in naive T cells.


### Aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit moderate changes in chromatin compartmentalization
  Recent studies have demonstrated that 3D genome organization plays a pivotal role in T cell activation and differentiation (44, 45). The lack of apparent correlation between aging-associated changes in local chromatin features and gene expression prompted us to explore whether the changes in higher-order chromatin structure contribute to the transcriptional dysregulation observed in aged naive T cells. To characterize the aging-associated changes in 3D genome organization, we performed Hi-C in young and aged naive CD4+ T cells on two independent biological replicates (fig. S7A). The P(s) curves, which quantify the chromatin contact probability as a function of genomic separation (Fig. 2A and fig. S7B), are largely identical for young and aged cells, suggesting that the aging process does not affect the overall genome folding mode or the average chromatin loop sizes throughout the genome.

  We examined whether aging led to changes in higher-order chromatin organizational features at different scales. Transcriptionally active and inactive genomic regions in interphase cells preferentially associate with those of similar transcriptional status, leading to the formation of spatially segregated A and B compartments (46). We quantified the A/B compartment organization in young and aged naive CD4+ T cells by performing principal components analysis on Hi-C interactions. Among all the 10-kb genomic bins, ∼1 and ∼1.4% switch from A to B compartment or B to A compartment, respectively (Fig. 2B). Although these changes are moderate, the genomic regions that switch compartments are highly consistent between two replicates (fig. S7, C to F), suggesting that they are unlikely to be experimental artifacts.

  We further examined the correlation between the compartment switch and gene expression changes. The genomic regions switching from B to A compartment exhibited a moderate overall increase of gene expression compared to the regions remaining in the same compartment (Fig. 2C). Notably, genes located in the B-to-A–switched region are related to the TNF signaling pathway (Fig. 2D), including Naip family genes and Rora gene (Fig. 2, E and F). While this finding suggests that the A/B compartment switch may influence the expression of certain inflammatory genes in aging T cells, only a small fraction of significantly up-regulated genes upon aging (121 of 1616 genes) are associated with B-to-A compartment switching (fig. S7G). Thus, the changes in genome compartmentalization only partially contribute to aging-associated transcriptome changes.


### Aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit widespread attenuation of TAD boundaries and increased cross-boundary interactions
  At an intermediate scale, the genome is partitioned into TADs, which create insulated neighborhoods for coordinated gene regulation (47, 48). To determine how aging affects TAD organization, we applied the insulation index approach to identify TAD boundaries in naive CD4+ T cells (49). Lower insulation scores indicate stronger insulation, with local minima marking TAD boundaries. Using this method, we detected 2705 and 2655 boundaries in young and aged cells, respectively. Aging resulted in the loss of 445 and the emergence of 395 boundaries (Fig. 2G), a pattern highly consistent across biological replicates (fig. S8A). Although the numbers of gained and lost TAD boundaries were similar, the cumulative insulation profiles surrounding all the TAD boundaries exhibited an upward shift in aged cells (Fig. 2H and fig. S8B), indicating an overall attenuation of boundary strength with age.

  To better understand the nature of this phenomenon, we visualized the chromatin interaction patterns within TADs (intra-TADs) or across TAD boundaries (inter-TADs) using pile-up heatmaps. We found that the intra-TAD chromatin interaction frequencies were similar in young and aged naive CD4+ T cells (Fig. 2, I and J, and fig. S8C). In contrast, the inter-TAD chromatin interactions were markedly increased upon aging (Fig. 2, K and L, and fig. S8D), consistent with the compromised insulation ability of TAD boundaries. We further quantified the chromatin interaction frequency between the 500-kb genomic regions upstream and downstream of each boundary in young and aged naive T cells. Comparative analyses revealed substantially increased cross-boundary interactions at 791 boundaries, and decreased interactions at only 98 boundaries (Fig. 2M and fig. S8E), representing approximately 30 and 4% of total TAD boundaries, respectively. By comparison, changes in total intra-TAD interactions occurred at far fewer domains (Fig. 2N and fig. S8, F and G). Together, our analyses revealed that aging causes widespread weakening of TAD boundaries and increased cross-boundary chromatin interactions, thereby affecting the 3D genome architecture across a substantial fraction of the genome.


### Aging-associated rewiring of E-P interactions promotes proactivation and proinflammatory transcriptional programs
  At a finer scale, chromatin loops physically connect gene promoters with distal cis-regulatory elements, thereby directly regulating their transcriptional activity (50). We characterized chromatin looping landscapes in naive CD4+ T cells. At 10-kb resolution, we identified 5587 and 5633 chromatin loops in young and aged cells, respectively (fig. S9, A and B). Among these loops, 83% in young (4639) and 67.6% in aged cells (3810) overlapped at least one H3K27ac peak at loop anchors (fig. S9C), suggesting that they are potentially involved in transcriptional regulation. Comparative analysis revealed 3249 loops that either gained or strengthened with age, while 2271 loops disappeared or weakened (Fig. 3, A and B), representing 28.9 and 20.2% of the total identified loops, respectively. Notably, 23.8% (773 of 3249) of up-regulated chromatin loops occur across the TAD boundaries, compared to only 13.2% (514 of 3890) of unchanged loops and 14.1% (321 of 2271) of down-regulated loops (fig. S9D), consistent with the global weakening of TAD boundaries.

  We next assessed the functional significance of these altered chromatin loops. Genes associated with the enhanced chromatin loops upon aging exhibited an overall transcriptional up-regulation, whereas genes linked to the weakened loops were more likely to be down-regulated (Fig. 3C), implicating the altered chromatin loops in age-related transcriptional reprogramming. Strikingly, ∼33% (577 of 1737) of genes transcriptionally up-regulated in aged cells were associated with enhanced chromatin loops (Fig. 3D). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis showed that these genes are enriched in T cell activation, leukocyte differentiation, and inflammatory programs (Fig. 3E). Consistently, T cell activation– and inflammation-related genes linked to enhanced chromatin loops exhibited stronger transcriptional activation than those associated with unchanged or weakened loops (fig. S10).

  We examined the chromatin interaction patterns at key T cell activation– and inflammation-related gene loci such as Nfatc1 and Ccl (Fig. 3, F and G). In young naive T cells, promoters of these genes engaged in prominent long-range chromatin interactions with distal regulatory elements. Intriguingly, although these original interactions were preserved in aged cells, new interactions with more distal enhancers emerged (Fig. 3, F and G), coinciding with elevated gene expression.

  To assess the functional relevance of these age-associated chromatin interactions, we used the dCas9-KRAB system to silence the distal enhancers specifically engaged with the promoters of Nfatc1 and Ccl in aged T cells (51). We found that suppression of these rewired distal enhancers substantially reduced target gene expression (Fig. 3, H and I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that aging induces extensive remodeling of the chromatin looping architecture of naive CD4+ T cells. The rewiring of E-P interactions may promote proactivation and proinflammatory transcriptional programs, thereby priming aged naive T cells for heightened activation.


### Aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit an extended range of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions
  Having established their functional relevance, we next sought to understand the causes for the age-associated 3D genome alterations in naive CD4+ T cells. CTCF is the key chromatin architecture protein that demarcates TAD boundaries and mediates chromatin loop formation (52). Concordant with this notion, we found that CTCF is the most enriched motif not only in anchors of up-regulated and down-regulated loops but also in those of unchanged loops (fig. S11A). Subsequent analysis of CTCF CUT&Tag data from young naive CD4+ T cells further demonstrated that CTCF-binding peaks extensively colocalized with anchors across all loop categories (fig. S11B), indicating that the chromatin loops detected from our Hi-C data are predominantly CTCF-mediated structural loops.

  To assess the role of CTCF in age-associated genome organization changes, we examined the pairwise interactions among CTCF-binding sites using pileup heatmaps (site pileups). The pileup in aged cells exhibited a weaker signal at the center as well as stronger cross-shaped stripes extending from the center (Fig. 4, A and B), suggesting reduced interactions among CTCF sites themselves and increased contact probabilities between a CTCF-binding site and the flanking regions of another CTCF-binding site in aged cells. Consistent with this observation, the up-regulated chromatin loops in aged cells exhibit the highest fraction of loops only anchored by one CTCF-binding site (fig. S11C). We next generated pileup heatmaps for genomic regions surrounding CTCF-binding sites (local pileups). Notably, the local pileup heatmaps for the aged cells exhibited more pronounced stripe patterns, with the length of stripes extending up to 500 kb (Fig. 4, C and D), suggesting the CTCF-binding sites tend to interact with more distal genomic regions upon aging.

  To further elucidate the impact of the extended interaction range of CTCF-binding sites, we compared the chromatin interaction frequencies for nested chromatin loops anchored by a common CTCF-binding site. In aged cells, interaction frequency between the primary CTCF site (anchor 1) and its nearest looping partner (anchor 2) decreased slightly, while contacts with more distal anchors (anchor 3 and beyond) increased (Fig. 4, E and F). In line with this, the gained loops in aged cells are generally longer than the lost or unchanged loops (fig. S11D). Together, these analyses suggest that the CTCF-binding sites are more likely to skip the adjacent anchors to engage with more distal genomic regions in aged naive T cells, thereby reshaping the chromatin looping landscape and weakening TAD boundary insulation.

  We hypothesized that age-associated changes in CTCF occupancy may underlie the widespread changes in TAD boundaries and chromatin loops. However, to our surprise, the CTCF occupancy in aged naive T cells is largely unchanged compared to the young cells, with only 849 and 1420 of ∼25,000 CTCF-binding sites exhibiting a significant increase or decrease in CTCF occupancy (Fig. 4, G and H). At a more stringent threshold (|log2FC| > 1), only 91 and 135 CTCF-binding sites exhibited a significant increase or decrease in CTCF signals. However, these changes in CTCF occupancy are not correlated with alterations in TAD boundaries or chromatin loops (Fig. 4, I and J, and fig. S12). Notably, the extended interaction ranges also occurred at the ∼22,000 unchanged CTCF-binding sites (fig. S13), further suggesting that the broadly changed behaviors of CTCF-binding sites cannot be attributed to aging-associated changes in CTCF binding or distribution.


### Age-related decline in SATB1 expression extends the range of CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions in naive CD4+ T cells
  The lack of changes in CTCF occupancy in aged naive T cells prompted us to explore whether the extended interaction range of CTCF sites is driven by aging-associated changes in other chromatin architecture proteins, which may interact with CTCF to modulate its functions. To address this, we performed coimmunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (CoIP-MS) to compare the spectrum of CTCF-interacting proteins in young and aged naive CD4+ T cells. Among the CTCF-interacting nuclear proteins, 87 and 16 proteins exhibited significantly enhanced or reduced CTCF association, respectively (|log2FC| > 1, P value <0.05, Fig. 5A). Of these proteins, 12 and 5 also exhibited transcriptional up-regulation and down-regulation in aged T cells (RNA-seq |log2FC| > 0.5; fig. S14, A and B), suggesting that the changes in CTCF protein interactome can be partly attributed to aging-dependent gene expression changes.

  Functional annotation of the differential CTCF interactors revealed that most of the proteins showing increased CTCF binding in aged T cells are ribosomal components or involved in RNA processing (fig. S14C). In contrast, most of the proteins exhibiting reduced interaction with CTCF have been implicated in the regulation of chromatin structure, including nuclear matrix proteins SATB1 and SAFB2, cohesin subunit STAG1, epigenetic regulator HDAC2, DNA repair protein RAD18, and multiple nuclear lamina–associated proteins (fig. S14D). Intriguingly, SATB1 has been previously shown to modulate chromatin interactions surrounding CTCF-binding sites in T cells and regulate the transcriptional program associated with T cell activation (53, 54).

  We performed CUT&Tag experiments in naive T cells on RAD18, RBM25, and SATB1, the three proteins whose association with CTCF exhibits the most pronounced decreases in aged naive T cells. Notably, RAD18 and RBM25 exhibited substantially fewer genomic binding peaks (474 and 733, respectively) in CUT&Tag assays compared to SATB1 (8513) (Fig. 5B and fig. S15, A and B). We further demonstrated that SATB1 exhibits the most prominent colocalization with CTCF among the tested proteins, with 6679 of 8513 SATB1 peaks overlapping with CTCF peaks (Fig. 5, C and D, and fig. S15, C to F). Intriguingly, SATB1 also exhibits the most pronounced down-regulation in transcript level in aged T cells among all the CTCF-interacting proteins (log2FC = −2.91; fig. S14, A and B). We further confirm the age-dependent down-regulation of SATB1 at the protein level (Fig. 5, E and F). In line with this, CUT&Tag experiments revealed that the genome-wide occupancy of SATB1 markedly diminished in aged naive T cells (Fig. 5, B, G, and H). These findings strongly implicate SATB1 as an age-dependent regulator of CTCF function and genome architecture in T cells.

  To further investigate the influence of SATB1 on CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions, we classified CTCF- and SATB1-binding sites into three groups: CTCF-only, SATB1-only, and SATB1/CTCF–co-occupied sites. We then examined the chromatin topology surrounding each of these groups of sites by performing the Hi-C pileup analyses. The chromatin interactions surrounding the 6679 SATB1/CTCF–co-occupied sites exhibited more pronounced aging-associated changes to the 20,303 CTCF-only sites (Fig. 5, I to K). In young cells, pileup heatmaps centered on SATB1/CTCF–co-occupied sites lacked the prominent cross-shaped center stripes observed at CTCF-only sites, indicating that SATB1/CTCF sites are locally constrained and less likely to engage with distal genomic regions. Instead, these sites exhibited a distinctive fountain-like pattern, indicating preferential interactions between their upstream and downstream flanking regions. In aged cells, however, the cross-shaped signal stripes emerged at the SATB1/CTCF–co-occupied sites while the fountain-like pattern diminished, suggesting that these sites are in a more relaxed conformation and gain flexibility to interact with more distal genomic regions. Consistent with these changes, the chromatin loops connecting genomic regions proximal to SATB1/CTCF sites and regions distal from SATB1/CTCF sites were strengthened in aged cells (fig. S16A), whereas loops bridging the upstream and downstream flanking regions of SATB1/CTCF sites were weakened (fig. S16C). In contrast, these topological changes were less prominent at genomic regions surrounding CTCF-only sites (fig. S16, B to D). Collectively, these data suggest that the age-associated chromatin topology changes associated with the SATB1/CTCF sites rewire the chromatin loop landscape.

  Together, these findings support a model in which SATB1 localizes to a substantial subset of CTCF-binding sites, where it may contribute to stabilizing chromatin loops anchored at these sites while spatially constraining their interactions with distal genomic regions (Fig. 5L). Age-dependent reduction in SATB1 expression may relieve this spatial constraint, leading to increased contacts between these CTCF-binding sites and distal regions, while potentially reducing the stability of chromatin loops established in young T cells (Fig. 5M). We further propose that these SATB1-dependent changes in chromatin interaction patterns may collectively reshape 3D genome architecture and thereby contribute to aberrant activation and inflammatory gene expression in aged naive CD4+ T cells.


### Ectopic expression of SATB1 constrains CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions
  To further elucidate the function of SATB1 in genome organization, we ectopically overexpressed SATB1 in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells, which exhibit minimal endogenous SATB1 expression. In control cells, SATB1 binding was negligible, whereas overexpression induced thousands of SATB1 peaks genome-wide (Fig. 6, A and B). Consistent with the observations in T cells, SATB1 overexpression did not alter the CTCF occupancy or distribution in HEK293 cells (Fig. 6, A to C, and fig. S17). Hi-C analysis revealed that SATB1 overexpression markedly shortened the range of chromatin interactions at loci co-occupied by SATB1 and CTCF, as indicated by reduced stripe intensity and length in pileup heatmaps (Fig. 6, D and E). In contrast, chromatin interactions associated with CTCF-only sites were only modestly affected (Fig. 6, F and G). These results further demonstrate that SATB1 can spatially constrain CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions.


### SATB1 knockout recapitulates aging-associated 3D genome organization and transcriptome changes in naive CD4+ T cells
  Our findings predict that the loss of Satb1 in T cells would recapitulate key features of age-related T cell dysfunction. To directly test this, we generated CD4+ T cell–specific Satb1 conditional knockout (cKO) mice. Consistent with previous reports (55–58), we found that Satb1 cKO mice exhibited key hallmarks of T cell aging, including reduced naive CD4+ T cell populations, elevated memory T cell proportion, and enhanced T cell activation (fig. S18, A and B). Notably, naive CD4+ T cells from Satb1 cKO mice were hyperresponsive to activation stimuli, mirroring the phenotype of aged wild-type (WT) naive T cells (fig. S18, C and D). Transcriptomic profiling further revealed similarity between Satb1 cKO and aged WT naive CD4+ T cells (Fig. 7, A to C). Moreover, consistent with the observations in aged WT naive T cells, chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac changes in Satb1 cKO cells only weakly correlate with gene expression changes (fig. S19).

  Hi-C analysis of Satb1 cKO naive CD4+ T cells revealed substantial 3D genome organization changes that mirrored the aging-associated changes in WT cells. Satb1 loss induced A/B compartment switching in young naive CD4+ T cells that correlated with differential gene expression, with high reproducibility across biological replicates (Fig. 7D and fig. S20). Notably, the genomic regions exhibiting both A-to-B and B-to-A compartment switches in cKO mice substantially overlap with those observed in aged WT cells (Fig. 7, E and F). At the TAD level, Satb1 loss led to an overall reduction in boundary insulation strength (fig. S21, A and B), with 590 (26%) and 207 (9.2%) of 2236 TAD boundaries exhibiting increased or decreased cross-boundary interactions in cKO cells, respectively (Fig. 7G and fig. S21, C and D). In contrast, the frequency of intra-TAD chromatin interactions remained stable in Satb1 cKO cells (fig. S21, E and F). The weakened TAD boundaries in Satb1 cKO cells, which exhibited increased cross-boundary interactions, substantially overlapped with those weakened with age in WT cells (Fig. 7, H to J).

  Analysis of chromatin loops revealed extensive rewiring of 3D contacts, with 819 loops strengthened and 471 weakened, in Satb1 cKO naive CD4+ T cells (Fig. 7K). The genes adjacent to the strengthened chromatin loops showed an overall increase in transcript levels (Fig. 7L). GO analysis of these strengthened loop–adjacent genes revealed strong enrichment in T cell activation and inflammatory response pathways (Fig. 7M), highly consistent with the observations in aged WT cells.

  To further demonstrate the association between the SATB1-dependent genome organization features and CTCF, we performed CTCF CUT&Tag experiments in WT and Satb1 cKO naive CD4+ T cells (fig. S22). The loss of Satb1 resulted in altered chromatin interaction patterns among the CTCF-binding sites that resemble the extension of interaction range observed in aged WT cells (Fig. 7, N and O), with the most pronounced changes observed at the sites co-occupied by CTCF and SATB1 (fig. S23). At key loci such as Nfatc1 and Ccl gene cluster, the absence of Satb1 induced the formation of new E-P interactions that also emerged in aged WT cells (Fig. 7, P and Q).

  Given that SATB1 is known to play important roles in thymic T cell development, we sought to eliminate potential confounding effects arising from defective thymic development and further delineate the direct regulatory role of SATB1 in peripheral mature naive CD4+ T cells. To this end, we isolated mature naive CD4+ T cells from WT mice and performed in vitro knockdown of SATB1 via electroporation-mediated small interfering RNA (siRNA) delivery, followed by transcriptome profiling using RNA-seq (fig. S24, A and B).

  Transcriptomic analyses revealed that ∼50% SATB1 knockdown resulted in the significant down-regulation of 228 genes and up-regulation of 468 genes (fig. S24B). The relatively modest transcriptional changes compared to Satb1 knockout are likely attributable to the incomplete depletion of SATB1 by siRNA. Nevertheless, functional enrichment analyses showed that genes up-regulated upon SATB1 depletion were markedly enriched in pathways associated with inflammatory responses and T cell activation (fig. S24C). The inflammatory genes that were robustly up-regulated in naive CD4+ T cells from aged mice (identified in Fig. 1D) also exhibited concordantly elevated expression in both Satb1 cKO and in vitro SATB1-knockdown naive CD4+ T cells (fig. S24D). Together, these findings further support a direct regulatory role of SATB1 in mature naive T cells.

  We further examined the impact of acute SATB1 knockdown on chromatin topology by performing chromatin conformation capture coupled with quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (3C-qPCR) at several representative inflammatory loci, including Nfatc1, Ccl, and Il1b (fig. S25). These loci exhibited significantly strengthened E-P interactions and increased transcription in both aged and Satb1 cKO naive CD4+ T cells. Among the three loci examined, Ccl and Il1b also exhibited significantly elevated transcript levels following SATB1 siRNA knockdown, whereas Nfatc1 expression remained unchanged, suggesting that Nfatc1 is less sensitive to the acute and partial depletion of SATB1 achieved by siRNA-mediated knockdown. Consistent with these transcriptional changes, 3C-qPCR revealed significantly strengthened E-P interactions at both the Ccl (fig. S25, A to D) and Il1b (fig. S25, E to H) loci, but not at Nfatc1 (fig. S25, I to L), following SATB1 knockdown. Thus, these findings further support a direct role for SATB1 in constraining long-range E-P interactions in mature naive T cells.

  Together, our results suggest that SATB1 plays an important role in maintaining chromatin organization and transcriptional homeostasis in mature naive CD4+ T cells and suggest that age-associated SATB1 loss contributes, at least in part, to the chromatin architectural and transcriptional alterations observed during T cell aging.


## DISCUSSION
  The aging process manifests as a progressive loss of cellular organization across multiple levels, including widespread alterations in the transcriptome and epigenetic landscape (59, 60). The 3D organization of the genome within the nucleus provides an essential layer of regulation for gene expression and genome stability. Consistent with this notion, 3D genome architecture undergoes marked changes with age in diverse tissues (16–23). Although these architectural changes are frequently correlated with altered gene expression, the causal relationships between them remain incompletely defined. Here, we demonstrate that naive CD4+ T cells undergo site-specific, age-associated architectural remodeling. In particular, E-P rewiring occurs at key genomic loci involved in inflammation and T cell activation, thereby enhancing their expression and predisposing aged naive CD4+ T cells to heightened and potentially dysregulated activation responses. These findings establish genome architecture changes as one of the molecular drivers of immune and organismal aging.

  Cohesin and CTCF are central to establishing 3D genome architecture, particularly TADs and chromatin loops, during development (61–63). Prior work has implicated their redistribution in age-related genome architectural changes in several tissues. For example, aged pro-B cells exhibit elevated expression of WAPL, the universal cohesin release factor, leading to reduced cohesin occupancy and global weakening of TAD boundaries (19). Similarly, microglia in the aged hippocampus exhibit reduced chromatin accessibility at CTCF-binding sites, suggesting that diminished CTCF binding may lead to age-associated decay in TAD organization (64). In contrast to these mechanisms, we find that aged naive CD4+ T cells exhibit no appreciable changes in CTCF binding. Instead, aging is associated with a pronounced decline in SATB1, a chromatin organizer extensively colocalizing with CTCF (53, 54, 65) and is proposed to constrain CTCF-mediated chromatin interactions. Age-associated SATB1 down-regulation may relax the spatial constraints imposed on CTCF-bound loci, enabling these sites to engage more frequently in long-range interactions with distal genomic regions. Such changes could in turn contribute to the remodeling of higher-order chromatin architecture during aging. These results highlight the diversity of mechanisms underlying age-associated 3D genome remodeling and suggest that additional CTCF regulators beyond SATB1 may contribute to aging in other cell types.

  SATB1 plays critical roles in the development of T cells by promoting the transition of T cells from double-positive to single-positive stages and regulating the differentiation of Foxp3+ regulatory T cells and T follicular helper cells (55, 58, 66–68). Recent studies have also shown that the expression of SATB1 in precursors of exhausted T cells limits effector differentiation while preserving functionality of CD8+ T cells (69). Here, we show that SATB1 expression in lineage-specified naive CD4+ T cells is strongly age dependent, with its decline associated with dysregulated genome architecture and transcriptional programs. Thus, SATB1 not only orchestrates early T cell development but also preserves the “youthful” state of naive T cells by stabilizing genome organization. Notably, recent transcriptomic profiling of a human cohort identified SATB1 as one of the most prominent age-associated markers of immune aging (70), suggesting that its role in maintaining immune youthfulness may be evolutionarily conserved across mammals.

  Consistent with our model proposing SATB1 as a key regulator that maintains youthful chromatin organization in naive CD4+ T cells, several independent studies provide complementary evidence across T cell subsets and aging contexts. A previous study demonstrated that impaired SATB1 function remodels genome architecture and promotes a differentiated transcriptional state in naive CD8+ T cells (71), highlighting a conserved role of SATB1 in regulating T cell chromatin architecture. In line with this finding, SATB1 has also been shown to maintain transcriptional homeostasis and suppress inappropriate effector-like activation in naive CD8+ T cells (72), further underscoring its importance in preserving naive T cell identity. Moreover, it has been shown that aging induces widespread phenotypic remodeling of CD4+ T cells, promoting differentiation toward proinflammatory and regulatory states (73). This observation is consistent with our finding of enhanced inflammatory gene expression in aged naive CD4+ T cells. Our study further extends these observations by identifying a putative SATB1- and CTCF-dependent chromatin regulatory mechanism that may underlie this age-associated transcriptional dysregulation. Collectively, these findings support a model in which age-associated SATB1 decline disrupts chromatin organization and transcriptional homeostasis, thereby contributing to the functional deterioration of naive CD4+ T cells during aging.

  Mechanistically, SATB1 extensively colocalizes with CTCF across the genome in young naive T cells and may constrain the chromatin topology surrounding CTCF-binding sites through several possible mechanisms. A previous study showed that SATB1 preferentially binds to sequences embedded in the nucleosomal core, which are flanking the CTCF-binding sites, and can stabilize the DNA it binds from unwinding (74). Such a function may stabilize a special configuration at the CTCF-bound loop anchors. Loss of SATB1 may therefore increase “slippage” at loop anchors, resulting in longer chromatin loops. Alternatively, SATB1 has traditionally been referred to as a component of the nuclear matrix (75–77), which represents an interchromosomal protein compartment with both fluidity and structural rigidity to some extent (78–80). Thus, SATB1 may tether CTCF-bound loci to the nuclear matrix, thereby constraining their movement and chromatin topology. Dissecting these mechanisms will not only illuminate the molecular functions of SATB1 but also advance fundamental understanding of how 3D genome architecture is formed and modulated.

  Last, our findings raise the possibility that restoring SATB1 expression could rejuvenate genome architecture and function in aged naive CD4+ T cells. Notably, tumor-derived transforming growth factor–β has been shown to suppress Satb1 expression in mature CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, suggesting that age-associated changes in the tissue microenvironment may contribute to the decline of SATB1 expression in aging (67, 81). Future work to identify the extrinsic signals and intracellular pathways that down-regulate SATB1 in aged T cells may yield effective strategies to restore its expression.

  One limitation of the present study is that our mechanistic analyses were performed exclusively in murine cells. Although our analyses of published human single-cell RNA-seq datasets revealed transcriptional changes consistent with those observed in mice, the direct functional contribution of SATB1 to human immune aging remains to be experimentally established. Future studies will be required to determine whether SATB1 plays a similar role in maintaining genome architecture and cellular homeostasis in human naive CD4+ T cells and whether restoring SATB1 expression represents a potential strategy for mitigating age-associated immune dysfunction.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Generation of Cd4+ T cell conditional Satb1 knockout mice
  Satb1fl/fl mice (GemPharmatech, #T023038) were bred with Cd4-Cre+ mice (Saiye Biotechnology, #I001002) to generate T cell–specific cKO mice (Satb1fl/flCd4-Cre+). Genotyping was performed using tail-derived DNA with three primer sets: 5′-loxP (F1:5′-TTACCATATTTAACTGCAGCAGA-3′, R1:5′-CCTGCATACACACTTCTGTTAA-3′), 3′-loxP (F2:5′-TCTGAGGCGGAAAGAACCAG-3′, R2:5′-CAAGAAGAAAAGAATAGGAGTCCAT-3′), and Cd4-Cre (F:5′-AGGGTCGGAGACAATAACGG-3′, R:5′-TTGCGAACCTCATCACTCGT-3′). Male littermates aged 6 to 8 weeks were divided into experimental groups: Satb1+/+ or Cd4-Cre+ (named as WT) and Satb1fl/flCd4-Cre+ (named as Satb1 cKO). All mice were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions with controlled temperature (22° ± 1°C) and humidity (55 ± 5%), with experimental procedures approved by the Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine Animal Welfare Committee (approval number: SH9H-2025-A606-SB).


### Analysis of spleen T cell subsets
  Young (6 to 8 weeks) and aged (18 to 22 months) C57BL/6 male mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation and immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 min. The spleens were then excised using ophthalmic scissors, mechanically dissociated, and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Red blood cells were removed by lysis, and the remaining splenocytes were stained with the following antibodies for 30 min at room temperature: CD4-ABflo-594 (Abclonal, catalog no. A24722), CD25–phycoerythrin (PE)–Cy7 (Invitrogen, catalog no. 25-0251-82), CD62L–fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC, eBioscience, catalog no. 11-0621-81), CD44-ABflo-647 (Abclonal, catalog no. A24637), and CD69-ABflo-488 (Abclonal, catalog no. A24938). Flow cytometric analysis was performed on a BD LSRFortessa cytometer to determine the proportions of CD62L+, CD44+, and CD69+ cells within the splenic T cell population.


### In vitro activation of T cells
  Naive CD4+ T cells were isolated from male mice using the EasySep Mouse Naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, catalog no. 19765) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin, and 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol. T cell activation was induced by adding CD3/CD28 activation magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 11456D) to the cultures. After 24 hours of stimulation, the cells were harvested and stained with a CD44-ABflo-647 antibody (Abclonal, catalog no. A24637) at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. The cells were then washed, resuspended in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa) to assess T cell activation.


### Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
  Purified naive CD4+ T cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin-streptomycin, and 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol. T cell receptor activation was induced by incubating the cells with anti-CD3/CD28 activation beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 11456D). After 16 hours of stimulation, culture supernatants were collected, and IL-2 concentrations were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Multi Sciences, catalog no. EK202) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A standard curve was generated for each assay, and IL-2 concentrations were calculated from the corresponding optical density values.


### Naive CD4+ T cell isolation for RNA-seq
  To obtain naive T cells at high purity for RNA-seq, naive CD4+ T cells (CD4+ CD44low CD62Lhigh) were first isolated from male mice using EasySepTM Mouse Naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 19765) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The enriched naive T cells were subsequently stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies (CD4-ABflo-594, Abclonal, catalog no. A24722; CD25-PE-Cy7, Invitrogen, catalog no. 25-0251-82; CD62L-FITC, eBioscience, catalog no. 11-0621-81), and the CD4+ CD25− CD62L+ naive T cells were sorted using flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa), as described previously (53, 82). The same procedure was applied to isolate naive CD4+ T cells from 6- to 8-week-old male Satb1fl/flCd4-Cre+ (cKO) and Satb1+/+Cd4-Cre+ or Satb1fl/fl littermate controls.


### RNA-seq and data analysis
  Library preparation and sequencing services were provided by Anoroad Genomics (Beijing, China). Briefly, polyadenylated mRNA was enriched from 1- to 3-μg high-quality total RNA using poly-T magnetic beads, followed by fragmentation into 150– to 200–base pair (bp) segments. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand generation using DNA polymerase I. After end repair and adenylation, adaptor-ligated libraries were constructed using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, NR604 series) and sequenced in paired-end 150-bp mode on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw reads were aligned to the murine genome (GRCm38/mm10) using STAR (v2.7.3a) with default parameters. Gene-level quantification was achieved through HTSeq-count (v0.12.4), followed by variance stabilization and differential expression analysis using DESeq2 (v1.30.1). Statistical significance was assessed using DESeq2’s Wald test with multiple hypothesis correction to calculate adjusted P values. DEGs were defined on the basis of the magnitude of log2 fold changes (log2FC) and the threshold of adjusted P values specified in the corresponding figure legends. To minimize noise from lowly expressed genes, only genes with a BaseMean greater than 80 were considered. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using GO terms implemented in ClusterProfiler (v4.0.5). All RNA-seq libraries from the young/aged and WT/cKO cohorts were prepared and sequenced in a single batch, thereby minimizing potential technical batch effects.


### ATAC-seq library preparation
  The ATAC-seq experiments were conducted following the protocol of the Hyperactive ATAC-Seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, TD711). Briefly, naive CD4+ T cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 500g for 5 min, and the supernatant was carefully removed. Next, cell lysis buffer [10 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 10 mM NaCl, 30 mM MgCl2, and 0.1% CA630] was added to lyse the cells for 5 min, followed by another centrifugation step at 500g for 5 min to collect the nuclei after discarding the supernatant. Subsequently, 5× TTBL and TTE Mix were added to the nuclei suspension, and enzymatic digestion was carried out at 37°C for 30 min with thorough mixing. Last, DNA clean beads (Vazyme, N411) were added to purify the DNA. The purified DNA was then subjected to PCR amplification for library preparation, which was subsequently sequenced on the Illumina PE150 platform. All ATAC-seq libraries across young/aged and WT/cKO cohorts were prepared and sequenced in a single batch to minimize technical batch effects.


### CUT&Tag library preparation
  The CUT&Tag procedure was conducted following established protocols with minor modifications, using the Hyperactive In-Situ ChIP Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme TD901-01). Naive CD4+ T cells purified using EasySepTM Mouse Naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 19765) were attached to polylysine-coated coverslips as an alternative to ConA bead–based immobilization. Fixation was performed using 1% (v/v) formaldehyde in PBS, followed by quenching with 2.5 M glycine solution. Permeabilization was performed using 0.5% Triton X-100 (v/v) for 10 min at room temperature. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies against SATB1 (Abcam, ab109122), H3K27ac (Abcam, ab177178), CTCF (Abcam, ab128873), RBM25 (Abclonal, A15496), and RAD18 (HUABIO, SN66-01) followed by incubation with secondary antibodies. pG-Tn5 transposase (37°C, 1 hour) was used for DNA fragmentation and adapter insertion. Genomic DNA (gDNA) was purified through phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol extraction and amplified via PCR. Final library sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using paired-end 150-bp sequencing configuration.


### CUT&Tag data analysis
  For CUT&Tag data analysis, raw sequenced FASTQ files were processed using TrimGalore (version 0.6.6) with default parameters to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences. The trimmed reads were subsequently aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10) or hg38 using bowtie2 (version 2.4.1) with the parameters “--very-sensitive -X 2000.” PCR duplicates of mapped reads were removed using sambamba (version 0.8.1). Before peak calling, the mapped reads were shifted using the “alignmentSieve --ATACshift” function from the deepTools package (version 3.3.1). For each CUT&Tag library, bigwig files were generated at a 10-bp bin size using the “bamCoverage” function from deepTools, normalized by reads per kilobase per million. Peak calling for CUT&Tag data was performed using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) with default parameters. To ensure the reliability of differential peak identification, a batch effect correction step was introduced in the subsequent differential analysis: First, the R package RUV (Remove Unwanted Variation, v1.42.0) was used to uniformly correct CUT&Tag signals across different samples, effectively eliminating the interference of experimental batch differences on peak signals. Following correction, the DiffBind package (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DiffBind.html, v3.18.0) was used to calculate fold changes and statistical significance of peak intensities between samples. A threshold of false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 was applied to define significantly differential peaks, with the threshold of log2FoldChange specified in the corresponding figure legends. Volcano plots were generated using R-based plotting packages (ggplot2, v4.0.0) for visualization of differential peaks.


### Hi-C library preparation
  Hi-C library preparation was performed according to a previously published in situ Hi-C protocol with minor modifications (83). Specifically, ∼1 × 106 naive CD4+ T cells purified using flow cytometry were fixed with 1 ml of 1% (v/v) formaldehyde in 1× Hanks’ balanced salt solution for 10 min at room temperature. Following fixation, 55 μl of 2.5 M glycine was added to each sample to quench the cross-linking reaction. Cells were incubated for 15 min at room temperature. Fixed naive CD4+ T cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 800g for 5 min and resuspended in 200 μl of ice-cold Hi-C lysis buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% Igepal CA-630]. The cells were incubated on ice for 30 min. After lysis, the cells were centrifuged at 2500g for 5 min and resuspended in 360 μl of ice-cold 1× NEBuffer 3.1. To open chromatin, 38 μl of 10% SDS was added to each sample. The samples were incubated at 65°C for 10 min. Subsequently, 43 μl of 10% Triton X-100 was added to neutralize the SDS, followed by incubation at 37°C for 15 min. Next, 12 μl of 10× NEBuffer 3.1 was added to each sample, and digestion was performed overnight at 37°C using DpnII (NEB, 40 μl of 10,000 U/ml) in a ThermoMixer. After digestion, the samples were heated at 65°C for 20 min to inactivate the DpnII. A biotin Fill-in Mix (60 μl per sample, containing 2 μl of ddH2O, 6 μl of 10× NEBuffer 3.1, 1.5 μl of 10 mM deoxycytidine triphosphate, 1.5 μl of 10 mM deoxyguanosine triphosphate, 1.5 μl of 10 mM deoxythymidine triphosphate, 37.5 μl of 0.4 mM biotin-14–deoxyadenosine triphosphate, and 10 μl of DNA polymerase I (5 U/μl; Klenow) was prepared and added to each sample. The samples were incubated at 23°C for 4 hours in a ThermoMixer with intermittent shaking (900 rpm, 10 s every 5 min). A ligation mix [665 μl per sample, containing 242 μl of ddH2O, 240 μl of 5× ligation buffer (Invitrogen), 120 μl of 10% Triton X-100, 12 μl of bovine serum albumin (10 mg/ml), and 50 μl T4 DNA ligase (Invitrogen)] was then added. The samples were incubated at 16°C for 4 hours in a ThermoMixer with intermittent shaking. After ligation, 50 μl of proteinase K (10 mg/ml) was added to each sample. The samples were incubated at 55°C for an hour. DNA was extracted using phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol, ethanol-precipitated, and fragmented by sonication using Covaris M220 (peak incident power, 50 W; duty cycle, 20%; cycles per burst, 200; treatment time, 240 s) into fragments ranging from 300 to 500 bp. The sheared DNA was subsequently end-repaired, A-tailed, and adaptor-ligated using the Hieff NGS MaxUp II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen, 12200ES08). All Hi-C libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in PE150 mode. All Hi-C libraries across young/aged and WT/cKO cohorts were prepared and sequenced in a single batch to minimize technical batch effects.


### Hi-C data analysis

#### Mapping and heatmap generation
  Reads from raw FASTQ files were first quality-trimmed using TrimGalore (version 0.6.6) with default settings. Subsequently, the processed reads were aligned to the mouse (mm10) or human (hg38) genome using the 4D Nucleome Hi-C data processing pipeline to generate .cool files for downstream analysis.


#### A/B compartment analysis
  Eigenvalue decomposition was performed on Hi-C contact matrices binned at 10-kb resolution using the call-compartments function in HiCExplorer (version 3.6). A/B compartments were corrected on the basis of ATAC-seq signal intensity and assigned according to the sign of the first principal component (PC1). For compartment switch analysis, genomic bins with PC1 values shifting from negative to positive were defined as undergoing a B-to-A switch, whereas shifts from positive to negative were classified as A-to-B switches. To minimize the impact of minor variations, bins with |ΔPC1| <0.2 were excluded from further analysis. For visualization of the A/B compartment switch, scatter plots of PC1 values in different samples were generated in R using the ggplot2 package.


#### TAD boundary identification
  Insulation analysis and TAD calling were conducted on cooler matrices binned at a 10-kb resolution using the diamond-insulation utility from cooltools. TAD boundaries with a boundary strength exceeding 0.2 were classified as high-confidence boundaries and used for subsequent analyses. To compare the locations of TAD boundaries between different groups, each TAD boundary was extended by 20-kb upstream and downstream, resulting in a 50-kb boundary zone centered on the original 10-kb bin. Partially overlapping TAD boundary zones were defined as common TAD boundaries shared between different groups. Pileup heatmaps of the observed/expected (O/E) Hi-C interaction frequencies for either 1-Mb genomic regions centered at TAD boundaries or rescaled TADs were generated using the coolpuppy tool (https://github.com/open2c/coolpuppy).


#### Cross-TAD boundary interaction analysis
  Cross-TAD boundary interactions were quantified by calculating the average O/E chromatin interaction frequencies between genomic regions located 500 kb upstream and downstream of each TAD boundary. Cross-boundary interaction scores were then compared at corresponding TAD boundaries between aged and young WT T cells or between Satb1 cKO and WT T cells. A log2 fold change greater than 0.2 was used as the threshold to define significantly altered cross-boundary interactions.


#### Loop calling and differential loop analysis
  Chromatin loop detection was performed on Hi-C contact matrices binned at 10-kb resolution using Mustache (v1.1.7; https://github.com/ay-lab/mustache). An FDR cutoff of <0.05 was applied for young/aged WT samples, while a relaxed cutoff of <0.1 was used for Satb1 WT/cKO samples. For differential loop analysis, loops detected in each sample pair were merged using the pairtopair function in BEDTools (v2.30.0). A loop was considered stable if the positions of both anchors differed by no more than 10 kb between groups. To quantitatively assess changes in chromatin loop strength, O/E interaction values were extracted from the corresponding .cool files for each loop. For the young-versus-aged comparison, loops with log2FC > 0.1 or log2FC < −0.1 were classified as strengthened or weakened, respectively. For the WT versus Satb1 cKO comparison, differential loops were defined using thresholds of log2FC > 0.5 (strengthened) or log2FC < −0.5 (weakened).


#### Pileup analysis of CTCF- and SATB1-binding sites
  To characterize chromatin interaction patterns associated with distinct CTCF- and SATB1-binding sites, CUT&Tag datasets for CTCF and SATB1 were used to identify their genomic binding regions. Overlapping and unique peaks were visualized using Venn plots, and the binding sites were categorized into three groups: CTCF-only, SATB1-only, and CTCF-SATB1–co-occupied (CTCF&SATB1) sites. Pileup Hi-C interaction heatmaps centered on each group of sites, as well as pairwise interactions among these groups, were generated using the coolpuppy tool. The values along the central stripes of the pileup heatmaps were subsequently plotted as line profiles.


### Reverse transcription qPCR
  Reverse transcription qPCR (RT-qPCR) experiments were conducted using the EZ-press Cell to cDNA Kit PLUS (EZbioscience, B0003) following the manufacturer’s instructions described previously. Specifically, primary naive CD4+ T cells isolated from young and aged mice, with or without lentiviral transduction, were harvested by centrifugation (300g, 5 min). A total of 400 μl of cell lysis buffer was added to each sample. Following a 10-min incubation at room temperature, 4 μl of lysates were transferred to new tubes, and 0.8 μl of gDNA remover was added for an additional 5-min incubation. Subsequently, 5 μl of 4× RT Master Mix and 10.2 μl of ribonuclease-free ddH2O were added to the samples, followed by incubation at 42°C for 15 min. Afterward, 4 μl of the resulting cDNA was mixed with 1 μl of 10 mM primers and 2× qPCR buffer. The qPCR reactions were carried out on a LightCycler 480 II (Roche).


### dCas9-KRAB–mediated enhancer suppression
  For dCas9-KRAB–mediated enhancer suppression, single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting distal enhancers linked to Nfatc1 (sgRNA sequence: 5′-TGTAGTGTCGGGTGCTTCGT

  -3′) and Ccl locus (sgRNA sequence: 5′-GGCCCGTCTACGCCATCGTA-3′), as indicated in Fig. 3 (H and I), were selected using the web-based tool CRISPOR (https://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/). A nontargeting sgRNA (sequence: 5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGTA-3′) with no homology to sequences in the mouse genome was used as a negative control. The sgRNAs were cloned into a lentiviral vector pLV-hU6-sgRNA-hUbc-dCas9-KRAB-T2a–green fluorescent protein (GFP) plasmid (MIAOLING PLASMID, #P1120). The resulting constructs were transfected into 293T cells for lentivirus production. Naive CD4+ T cells were purified from male C57BL/6 mice using the EasySep Mouse naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 19765) and activated with CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11453D) for 24 hours in the presence of IL-2 (50 U/ml). Following activation, the cells were infected with the lentivirus at a multiplicity of infection of 100 and cultured for 72 hours. GFP-positive cells were sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS, BD Influx), and gene expression was analyzed by RT-qPCR. The primer sequences used in this study are listed as follows:

  Nfatc1: forward 5′-GGTGCCTTTTGCGAGCAGTATC-3′ and reverse 5′-CGTATGGACCAGAATGTGACGG-3′;

  Ccl5: forward 5′-CCTGCTGCTTTGCCTACCTCTC-3′ and reverse 5′-ACACACTTGGCGGTTCCTTCGA-3′;

  β-Actin: forward 5′-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′ and reverse 5′-TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG-3′.


### SATB1 overexpression in HEK293 cells
  To investigate the regulatory role of SATB1 in chromatin architecture, a human SATB1 overexpression plasmid and the corresponding empty vector control were constructed for transient transfection. The full-length canonical coding sequence (CDS) of human SATB1 was obtained according to the UniProt reference canonical sequence (Consensus CDS accession: CCDS2631.1) and cloned into the Lenti–cytomegalovirus (CMV)–puro expression vector. The empty Lenti-CMV-puro vector served as the negative control. Human HEK293 cells (Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, SCSP-5209) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells at 70 to 80% confluence were transiently transfected with either the SATB1 overexpression plasmid or the empty vector using Lipofectamine 8000 (Beyotime, C0533) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were harvested for Hi-C and CUT&Tag library preparation and sequencing.


### SATB1 siRNA knockdown
  Male WT C57BL/6 mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were euthanized, and the spleens were collected to prepare single-cell suspensions by mechanical dissociation. Red blood cells were lysed using red blood cell lysis buffer, after which the remaining cells were washed once with PBS and resuspended in electroporation buffer. FAM-labeled SATB1 siRNAs (Jiman Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) were added immediately before electroporation. The sequences of the two SATB1 siRNAs and control siRNA were as follows: siRNA1, 5′-CAGAUGUUCUUCGCUAAUA(dT)(dT)-3′; siRNA2, 5′-CGCGAUUUCUCGAGUCCAA(dT)(dT)-3′; Control, 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU(dT)(dT)-3′. Cells were electroporated using a MaxCyte STX electroporation system, and FAM-positive cells were immediately isolated by FACS. Naive CD4+ T cells were subsequently purified using the EasySep Mouse Naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, catalog no. 19765) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium. After 48 hours, the cells were harvested for RT-qPCR analysis of Satb1 expression as described above. The primer sequences used for qPCR were as follows:

  Satb1: forward 5′TCACAGGCAGTATTTGCACGCG-3′ and reverse 5′-CGAAGGTTTACCAGCAGAGACTG-3′;

  β-Actin: forward 5′-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′ and reverse 5′-TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG-3′.


### 3C–quantitative PCR
  Naive CD4+ T cells electroporated with SATB1-targeting siRNA1 were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium for 48 hours before being harvested for chromosome conformation capture (3C) assays. The 3C procedure largely followed the Hi-C protocol described above, with one key modification: Following DpnII digestion, the DNA end fill-in step was omitted, and digested chromatin fragments were directly subjected to intramolecular ligation. After ligation, protein-DNA complexes were digested with proteinase K, and cross-linked DNA was purified using magnetic beads. The 3C-qPCR primer pairs were designed to quantify E-P interactions at the Nfatc1, Ccl, and Il1b loci. Primer sequences were as follows:

  Nfatc1, forward 5′-GACAGCATTTTGCTTCTTCCTTC-3′ and reverse 5′-CCTCTGCTGGCACCCAGAG-3′;

  Ccl, forward 5′-CATACAGTCGCCATTGCCCATC-3′ and reverse 5′-CCAGAGTGCAATGAGAAAC-3′;

  Il1b, forward 5′-CTGTGAGTGTGTGGCTCTG-3′ and reverse 5′-CCCATTCTGAGTATAGGG-3′. The genomic positions of these primer pairs are shown in fig. S25. A control primer pair targeting a genomic region spanning multiple DpnII restriction sites was also included (forward, 5′-GGTTCTGCTACTTGCTGC-3′; reverse, 5′-CAGATAACTGCTCTTAAAG-3′). qPCR was then performed to quantify the relative frequencies of E-P chromatin interactions at each locus.


### CoIP for MS
  For the CTCF CoIP-MS experiment, naive CD4+ T cells were isolated from young and aged mice using the EasySepTM Mouse Naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, 19765) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The CoIP procedure was performed as previously described, with minor modifications (84). Specifically, cells were lysed in Dignam buffer C [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 25% glycerol, 420 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), and protease inhibitors] for 10 min at 4°C. The lysate was then diluted with dilution buffer [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors] to the final concentration of 150 mM NaCl. After dilution, 10 μg of anti-CTCF antibody (Abcam, 128873) was added to the diluted lysate and incubated overnight at 4°C with rotation. On the following day, protein G magnetic beads (Invitrogen, 10003D) were added and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. The magnetic beads–antibody-protein complex was then washed twice with washing buffer (PBS, 0.03% Triton X-100). Last, the proteins were eluted from the beads by heating at 99°C for 10 min and subsequently analyzed by MS.


### Mass spectrometry
  CoIP samples were quantified for protein concentration, separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and assessed for quality control. For proteome profiling, peptides were analyzed using a Q Exactive HF-X Hybrid Quadrupole-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with a high-performance liquid chromatography system (EASY nLC 1200, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Dried peptide samples were redissolved in solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and loaded onto a 2-cm self-packed trap column (100 μm inner diameter, packed with 3-μm ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ beads; Dr. Maisch GmbH) using solvent A, followed by separation on a 15-cm analytical column (150 μm inner diameter, packed with 1.9-μm ReproSil-Pur C18-AQ beads; Dr. Maisch GmbH) with a 30-min linear gradient (solvent A: 0.1% formic acid in water; solvent B: 0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile) at a constant flow rate of 600 nl/min. The gradient program was as follows: 0 to 4 min, 8 to 15% solvent B; 4 to 19 min, 15 to 30% solvent B; 19 to 22 min, 30 to 50% solvent B; 22 to 23 min, 50 to 100% solvent B; and 23 to 30 min, holding at 100% solvent B. Eluted peptides were ionized at 2 kV and introduced into the mass spectrometer, which operated in data-dependent acquisition mode. For MS1 full scans, ions with a mass/charge range of 300 to 1400 were acquired using an Orbitrap mass analyzer at a resolution of 120,000, with an automatic gain control (AGC) target of 3 × 106 and a maximum ion injection time of 80 ms. The top 60 precursor ions were selected for fragmentation in a higher-energy collisional dissociation cell with a normalized collision energy of 27%, and the resulting fragment ions were detected by the Orbitrap analyzer at a resolution of 7500, with an AGC target of 5 × 104, a maximum ion injection time of 20 ms, and dynamic exclusion of previously acquired precursor ions enabled for 15 s.

  Protein quantification was based on the integrated peak areas of ions in the mass spectra, reflecting the relative abundances of corresponding peptides. Identified peptides were matched to proteins using the National Center for Biotechnology Information Reference Sequence database. Protein abundance data from young and aged mouse samples were integrated, and differential expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq algorithm. Log2 fold changes (log2FoldChange) and P values were calculated for each protein. Differentially expressed proteins were defined by a threshold of log2FoldChange >1 and P < 0.05.
