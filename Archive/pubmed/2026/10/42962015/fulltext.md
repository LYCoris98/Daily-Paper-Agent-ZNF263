# PDS5A and TOP2B cooperate for chromatin recruitment via CTCF


## Abstract
  PDS5A, a cohesin regulatory subunit, and topoisomerase IIB (TOP2B), an enzyme resolving DNA topological problems, interact with CTCF and regulate transcription and genome organization. We ascertained that PDS5A and TOP2B are recruited to CTCF-bound chromatin cooperatively and that catalytically active TOP2B increases PDS5A occupancy genome wide. Notably, the amino-terminal 95 to 116 amino acids of CTCF interacts with PDS5A, giving rise to CTCF-PDS5A-TOP2B interaction in vitro and to PDS5A chromatin enrichment via active TOP2B in vivo. PDS5A-TOP2B function together in moderating the resultant gene expression and genome organization. Loss of CTCF (95 to 116 amino acids) led to dysregulated gene expression and mildly reduced chromatin loops. In gliomas, PDS5A and TOP2B expression levels vary, but correlate, contributing to distinctive gene expression patterns. Inducible PDS5A knockdown led to reduced TOP2B occupancy and altered gene expression. Furthermore, PDS5A mediated sensitivity to TOP2 cancer drugs in glioma cells. This functional interaction between PDS5A and TOP2B at CTCF boundaries expands upon the mechanisms affecting gene regulation through genome organization, with implications for glioma therapeutics.


## INTRODUCTION
  The organization of the mammalian genome into chromatin loops, topologically associating domains, and genome compartments contributes to appropriate gene expression profiles. The mechanisms fostering gene regulation through three-dimensional genome organization rely on the formation and maintenance of chromatin loops, which enable the relevant interactions of DNA regulatory elements, such as enhancers and promoters. The chromatin anchors of these loops comprise insulation factors, including CTCF (1, 2). These anchors demarcate the chromatin boundaries, which are functionally distinct neighborhoods separated by insulation factors (3, 4), as demonstrated at the Hox clusters (5, 6). Chromatin loops are formed through the action of the cohesin complex, an adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP) motor capable of establishing DNA interactions and extruding chromatin loops in vitro (7) until its inevitable stalling at sites comprising either CTCF (8) and/or other transcription factors with insulating capacity, such as MAZ (6, 9), PATZ1 (10), and other zinc finger proteins (ZNFs) (10). Within this context, the cohesin regulators, PDS5A/B, are not required for loop extrusion in vitro but instead for the resultant length of the chromatin loops in vivo (11–15). Thus, PDS5A-bound cohesin maintains chromatin loops at CTCF binding sites. However, the recruitment of PDS5A at CTCF binding sites is not well understood.

  Changes in the chromatin loops can arise from alterations in the insulation factors, including CTCF (5, 16, 17), MAZ (6, 9), PATZ1 (10), and other ZNFs (10), and also in the subunits of the cohesin complex (18), including PDS5A/B (11, 14). These perturbations can affect the integrity of the chromatin domains. Thus, the processes involving these central proteins affect normal genome organization and the spatial-temporal regulation of gene expression during development, such that their disruption can foster diseases, including cancer (5, 19, 20). Despite the advances in the study of genome organization and its impact on gene expression, our understanding of the basic mechanisms of chromatin loop formation and its maintenance remains limited.

  Mounting evidence supports the loop extrusion model as a viable mechanism that continuously produces chromatin loops in an ATP-dependent fashion by loading cohesin onto chromatin fibers, thereby folding the fibers into loops (7, 21). Notably, cohesin-mediated loop extrusion generates DNA supercoils while the loop is protruding from the cohesin complex in vitro (22). In addition, previous work has associated the supercoiling of the DNA template during transcription with genome organization (23, 24). Supercoiling is accompanied by DNA torsional stress, which is relieved by the actions of topoisomerases II A/B (TOP2A/B) that generate a transient double-strand break (DSB) and rejoin both DNA strands, leading to the interconversion of topological DNA forms (25). While TOP2A is essential for cell division, TOP2B is involved in transcription control (26, 27). Of note, TOP2B colocalizes with CTCF and cohesin at the chromatin loop anchors (28), and its enzymatic activity can generate chromosomal rearrangements frequently found in cancers (29, 30). While the mechanisms of TOP2A/B enzymatic function have been extensively studied, their recruitment to chromatin is not well understood.

  TOP2A/B are overexpressed in many tumors including glioblastomas (GBM) (31), and many available cancer drugs target TOP2A/B enzymatic function (32, 33). However, these therapies generally give rise to unpredictable and variable responses. GBM, for example, are the most malignant among gliomas, do not respond predictably to therapies, and exhibit heterogeneity in gene expression and lethality. Furthermore, TOP2B has been associated with secondary malignancies that result from TOP2 drug–induced translocations (34–37). Thus, understanding the mechanisms underlying the recruitment of TOP2B activity to chromatin will very likely inform the appropriate and efficacious use of TOP2 drugs.

  Here, we investigated the relationship between CTCF, PDS5A, and TOP2B. By trapping the active form of TOP2 on chromatin using a drug called etoposide (ETO), we observed an enrichment of PDS5A at CTCF binding sites in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), cervical motor neurons (MNs), and glioma cells pointing to a functional link between TOP2B activity and PDS5A localization at chromatin borders. The distribution of PDS5A on chromatin coincided with the binding motifs of CTCF and other insulation factors, such as MAZ, PATZ1, ZNFs, and VEZF1. We mapped a region of 95 to 116 amino acids within the N-terminal domain of CTCF as being critical for CTCF interaction with PDS5A and TOP2B, in addition to the previously reported PDS5A-interaction sites on CTCF (12). In gliomas, we found that PDS5A and TOP2B expression levels correlate across specimens and that TOP2B enzymatic activity is linked to PDS5A at CTCF boundaries in glioma cells. Notably, PDS5A knockdown (KD) reduced TOP2B occupancy in glioma cells, indicating that PDS5A occupancy might mediate sensitivity to TOP2 drugs in these cells. Our results support a model in which PDS5A is incorporated into chromatin through the enzymatic activity of TOP2B and that PDS5A-TOP2B interaction is required for proper TOP2B occupancy genome wide. These findings clarify the heterogenous GBM response to TOP2 drugs and inform their use in patients.


## RESULTS

### CTCF, TOP2B, and PDS5A interact in mouse and human cells
  On the basis of previous work, CTCF interacts with TOP2B and the cohesin complex at topological domain borders in HeLa cells (28). Nonetheless, a mechanistic understanding of TOP2B recruitment to chromatin is lacking. Given that TOP2B localizes at topological borders, we investigated the connection between CTCF, TOP2B, the cohesin complex, and its accessory subunits on chromatin. We initially performed immunoprecipitation (IP), followed by Western blot (WB) analysis in HeLa nuclear extracts. Our findings indicated that CTCF coimmunoprecipitated with TOP2B and PDS5A/B in HeLa cells (Fig. 1A). We also observed RAD21 and WAPL, consistent with previous studies showing CTCF-RAD21 (38) and PDS5-WAPL interactions (39) (Fig. 1A). In contrast to TOP2B that interacts with CTCF, TOP2A does not appear to interact with CTCF in HeLa cells (Fig. 1A). Similarly, IP assays performed in nuclear extracts from mESCs indicated that CTCF interacted specifically with TOP2B, compared to TOP2A (Fig. 1B). Both TOP2A and TOP2B interact with each other, yet the expression levels of TOP2A are higher than those of TOP2B in mESCs (see input; Fig. 1B). Together, TOP2B interacts with PDS5A/B and CTCF.

  Given that PDS5A/B regulate chromatin loops at CTCF binding sites (11, 14), we sought to determine whether these proteins work together to recruit TOP2B to chromatin. As we observed that the pharmacological induction of TOP2B enzymatic activity influences TOP2B localization in the genome across human glioma cell lines (31), we investigated TOP2B interaction with PDS5A/B using ETO, a TOP2 poison that traps “active” TOP2A/B on chromatin. This process of TOP2 trapping occurs after the TOP2-elicited DNA DSB and before its DNA rejoining (32). As TOP2A and CTCF interaction was not detectable in mESCs (Fig. 1B), we aimed to discriminate TOP2A from TOP2B enzymatic activity using differentiated MNs derived from mESCs, wherein TOP2A expression levels range from low to undetectable as a result of differentiation toward postmitotic neurons (Fig. 1C). As expected, based on the switch from TOP2A to TOP2B isoforms during neuronal differentiation (40), higher levels of TOP2B were observed in MNs compared to those in mESCs (Fig. 1C). We next isolated the chromatin from MNs, either untreated or treated with ETO to trap active TOP2B on chromatin, and performed IP assays for PDS5A and TOP2B. PDS5A was enriched on chromatin from ETO-treated MNs as compared to nontreated cells [ETO versus dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), respectively; Fig. 1D], as evidenced by WB analysis of TOP2B and PDS5A IPs (see also fig. S1, A and B, for WB analysis and fig. S1, C to E, for additional IPs followed by WB analysis across cellular fractions). In contrast, ETO treatment was ineffectual with respect to the levels of CTCF, PDS5B, and other cohesin-associated components, such as WAPL and RAD21 (Fig. 1D and see fig. S1F for WAPL and RAD21). These data point to a previously unrecognized functional link between TOP2B and PDS5A: The trapped, active TOP2B and/or the product of its activity (i.e., DSB) promotes an increase of PDS5A on chromatin. Notably, we had previously detected PDS5A within the top 20 candidates whose loss further affects the integrity of the Hoxa5|6 boundary in MNs already having a deletion of a CTCF DNA binding site (CTCF ∆5|6:6|7) (6). Together, TOP2B and PDS5A interact with CTCF on chromatin, and PDS5A levels were increased upon trapping active TOP2B. These results prompted us to investigate PDS5A localization as a function of ETO treatment using chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq).


### TOP2B enzymatic activity enriches PDS5A on chromatin in a CTCF-dependent manner
  Previous work in mESCs showed that PDS5A specifically interacts with CTCF (12) and cooperates with PDS5B in regulating chromatin loops (11, 14). Thus, we next probed for the relationship between CTCF and PDS5A/B upon ETO treatment to “trap” TOP2A/B on chromatin using ChIP-seq in mESCs. We used mESCs having a CTCF degron [CTCF–green fluorescent protein (GFP)–AID–Tir1] (16), either untreated [CTCF–wild type (WT)] or treated with auxin to degrade endogenous CTCF (∆CTCF), followed by DMSO versus ETO treatment before ChIP-seq. ETO treatment did not affect CTCF protein levels (fig. S1G) or CTCF occupancy on chromatin (Fig. 2, A to C, and fig. S2A). Moreover, we and others have observed previously that the cohesin subunit, RAD21, relocalizes on chromatin in the absence of CTCF in mESCs (10), and this outcome also occurred irrespectively of ETO treatment (Fig. 2, D to F, and fig. S2B), suggesting that TOP2 activity does not mainly affect cohesin complex localization. Similar to our previous IP results (Fig. 1D), ETO treatment was ineffectual with respect to PDS5B binding on chromatin by ChIP-seq (Fig. 2, G to I). However, the absence of CTCF did lead to the loss of PDS5B compared to the CTCF-WT case (Fig. 2, G to I and fig. S2C). In contrast to PDS5B, PDS5A remained bound on chromatin upon CTCF degradation (∆CTCF; Fig. 2, J to L, and fig. S2D), while a reduction in PDS5A signal was observed (Fig. 2, J and K). This reduction in PDS5A intensity upon CTCF degradation pointed to the importance of CTCF binding to retain levels of PDS5A. ETO-mediated chromatin trapping of TOP2B led to enriched PDS5A occupancy genome wide (Fig. 2, J to L), as also observed in TOP2B or PDS5A IPs from chromatin (Fig. 1D). This enrichment was connected to the presence of CTCF (CTCF-WT) as evidenced by its abolishment upon CTCF degradation (∆CTCF; Fig. 2, J to L).

  While PDS5A was localized mostly at promoters, intronic, and intergenic regions in CTCF-WT cells, its localization at promoters increased from 21% in CTCF-WT cells to 38% in ∆CTCF cells (fig. S2, E to G). In addition to this global analysis, we also observed examples of individual loci bound by PDS5A in the absence of CTCF. For example, PDS5A localizes with CTCF in the HoxA cluster and remains bound on chromatin even in the absence of CTCF (fig. S3). Notably, the binding pattern of PDS5A at the HoxA cluster is consistent with our previous observation that its loss further disrupts the Hoxa5|6 boundary in MNs lacking CTCF binding at the Hoxa5|6 boundary (6).

  To further analyze this enhanced PDS5A chromatin binding upon ETO treatment, we compared PDS5A ChIP-seq peaks under conditions of DMSO versus ETO treatment (fig. S4). TOP2B activity induced by ETO appears to incorporate PDS5A at 7438 de novo binding regions in addition to enriching the vast majority of the 12,512 PDS5A peaks observed under DMSO conditions (fig. S4A). To gain insight into these PDS5A-enriched genomic regions, we ascertained the DNA motifs to which PDS5A localizes upon ETO treatment and assessed the nuclear factors that bind to these motifs (fig. S4B). CTCF and other insulation factors such as MAZ, PATZ1, ZNFs, and VEZF1 matched to the motifs overrepresented at all of these ETO-enriched sites, whether emerging or present with DMSO, thereby linking TOP2B activity and PDS5A localization to chromatin insulators. Similarly, DNA-motif analysis for 2128 relocalized PDS5A binding sites in ∆CTCF under ETO conditions (see Fig. 2L) indicated the motifs corresponding to various ZNFs and transcription factors (fig. S4, C and D). Together, these results show not only that PDS5A enrichment on chromatin is controlled by the presence of active TOP2B or the product of its activity (i.e., DSB) in a CTCF-dependent manner but also that this enrichment is localized to motifs associated with CTCF and other insulation factors.


### PDS5A-CTCF interaction region facilitates PDS5A-TOP2B localization on chromatin
  Given the requirement of CTCF for PDS5A enrichment, we investigated CTCF-PDS5A interaction using AlphaFold protein-protein prediction tools to ascertain the CTCF-PDS5A interaction surfaces. In addition to the previously reported PDS5A-interacting region on the CTCF N-terminal 13 to 33 amino acids (region 1) (12), we found another CTCF-PDS5A–interacting region on the CTCF N-terminal amino acids 95 to 116 (region 2; fig. S5, A and B). To understand the impact of these two CTCF regions on PDS5A interaction on chromatin, we performed rescue experiments in auxin-treated ∆CTCF background mESCs using hemagglutinin (HA)–FLAG–tagged versions of CTCF, either WT or having a deletion in either region 1 [MUT1(∆13–33)] or region 2 [MUT2(∆95–116)] (Fig. 3A). Endogenous CTCF-GFP was degraded under these conditions, as expected, and the different CTCF versions rescued CTCF to similar levels as assessed by WB of nuclear extracts (Fig. 3B). Of note, TOP2B and PDS5A expression seemed to be regulated by CTCF as auxin treatment reduced their levels, as evident in the case of RAD21 as well (Fig. 3B). The expression of PDS5A, TOP2B, and RAD21 was restored to levels similar to those of control cells upon expressing any of the HA-FLAG-CTCF constructs: CTCF-WT or CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) or CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) (Fig. 3B).

  To test whether the deletion mutants affect CTCF interaction with PDS5A or prevent PDS5A enrichment at CTCF boundaries via TOP2B activity, we treated the CTCF rescue cell lines with DMSO or ETO and performed ChIP-seq with HA (fig. S5, C to G) or PDS5A (Fig. 3, C to F; fig. S6, A to D; and fig. S7). In CTCF-WT cells, we observed a gain of PDS5A genome wide both at CTCF binding sites (fig. S6, A and B) and at PDS5A binding sites upon ETO treatment (Fig. 3, C and D), as expected. In the case of CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33), a weak PDS5A enrichment upon ETO treatment was observed genome wide, as compared to CTCF-WT (Fig. 3, C to E, and fig. S6, A to C). In contrast, CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) failed to enrich for PDS5A upon ETO treatment genome wide, as compared to CTCF-WT (Fig. 3, C and F, and fig. S6, A and D). Notably, PDS5A binding across CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) and CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) under DMSO conditions indicated a slight reduction compared to CTCF-WT condition (fig. S7, A to D); however, Pds5a expression was not significantly affected in the mutants (fig. S7, E and F, and see Fig. 3B for protein levels). CTCF-WT, CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33), and CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) exhibited similar CTCF expression levels in untreated cells as a control (Fig. 3B and fig. S7, E and F), yet a mild reduction in DNA binding was observed in CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) and CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) compared to WT (fig. S5, C and G). While we observed a modest decrease in CTCF-WT binding upon ETO treatment, CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) and CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) showed similar levels of CTCF binding irrespective of ETO treatment (fig. S5, C to F).

  To verify the protein interactions on chromatin, we performed CTCF FLAG-IP, followed by WB for FLAG-CTCF, PDS5A, and TOP2B using cells expressing CTCF-WT, CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33), or CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116), either untreated (DMSO) or under ETO-treatment conditions (Fig. 3G). CTCF-WT coimmunoprecipitated with PDS5A and TOP2B under ETO-treated conditions (Fig. 3G, right), pointing to CTCF-PDS5A-TOP2B interaction in the cells. Under similar conditions, CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) coimmunoprecipitated PDS5A, but not TOP2B (Fig. 3G, right), indicating that CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) affects the interaction between TOP2B and CTCF on chromatin upon ETO treatment. On the other hand, CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) exhibited the highest levels of PDS5A coimmunoprecipitation in untreated cells (Fig. 3G, left), while under ETO conditions, CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) affected the interaction between CTCF, PDS5A, and TOP2B, as compared to CTCF-WT (Fig. 3G, right), consistent with our ChIP-seq results. Our results showing increased PDS5A coimmunoprecipitation upon CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) in untreated cells (Fig. 3G, left) is consistent with a published study showing increased PDS5A localization upon CTCF (∆89–178 amino acids) mutation (12). Thus, while CTCF MUT2(∆95–116) accumulated PDS5A on chromatin (DMSO), its interaction with PDS5A was impaired upon ETO treatment, thereby preventing the chromatin-localized gain of PDS5A. Thus, active TOP2B enhances PDS5A occupancy genome wide as a function of the presence of region 2 (95 to 116 amino acids) of the CTCF N terminus.


### The N terminus of CTCF harboring CTCF-PDS5A interaction region is required for association with PDS5A-TOP2B in vitro
  To further confirm the CTCF-PDS5A-TOP2B interaction and the impact of CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) on PDS5A-TOP2B recruitment, we purified recombinant mouse mCTCF-WT and mCTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) (Fig. 3H). In addition, we expressed a recombinant fragment of mouse PDS5A(1 to 750 amino acids) including the conserved module that interacts with RAD21 and SMC3 in vitro (41, 42) and purified this protein from nuclei using FLAG affinity purification under conditions conducive to retaining its interacting partners upon elution (Fig. 3I). WB analysis demonstrated the presence of full-length PDS5A, PDS5B, TOP2A, TOP2B, and RAD21 (Fig. 3I). We next tested whether the PDS5A-TOP2B fraction can interact with CTCF-WT protein bound to a probe containing a CTCF binding site (43) in an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) (Fig. 3, J and K). Increasing amounts of CTCF-WT gave rise to a shift in the probe mobility (Fig. 3J, lanes 2 to 4, shown by black arrow), and the addition of the fraction containing PDS5A-TOP2B gave rise to another shift of slower mobility (Fig. 3J, lane 5, shown by orange arrow). To verify that the PDS5A-TOP2B–associated shift depends on CTCF-WT, we used decreasing amounts of CTCF-WT and observed the gradual loss of both shifts corresponding to CTCF-WT and CTCF-WT-PDS5A-TOP2B (Fig. 3J, lanes 6 to 8). Using the same EMSA conditions, we next tested whether PDS5A-TOP2B can bind to CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) (Fig. 3K). Again, increasing amounts of CTCF-WT gave rise to a shift (lane 2 and 3, shown by black arrow), and the addition of the fraction containing PDS5A-TOP2B gave rise to a further shift (lane 4, shown by orange arrow) that was dependent on the presence of CTCF-WT (lane 5) (Fig. 3K). However, while increasing amounts of CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) gave rise to a shift of the probe, as expected (lanes 7 and 8, shown by black arrow), the addition of the fraction containing PDS5A-TOP2B was ineffectual in further shift (lane 9) (Fig. 3K), pointing to the inability of CTCF-motif–bound CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) protein to recruit PDS5A-TOP2B in vitro. Thus, CTCF amino acids 95 to 116 at the N terminus interact with PDS5A-TOP2B, and an intact CTCF N terminus is required for PDS5A-TOP2B binding to CTCF in vitro.


### CTCF N terminus ∆95 to 116 amino acid mutant mildly affects genome organization in mESCs
  On the basis of our results described above, the recruitment of PDS5A and TOP2B to chromatin is dependent on an intact CTCF N terminus region (Fig. 3) and possibly other insulation factors given the remaining chromatin-bound PDS5A in ∆CTCF (see Fig. 2, J to L, and fig. S3 and S4) and its occupancy at insulator motifs in the presence of active TOP2B (fig. S4B). In addition, PDS5A occupancy on chromatin appears to be dynamic as we observed variable levels depending on TOP2B catalytical activity (Figs. 2 and 3). As the N terminus of CTCF (amino acids 95 to 116) is required for PDS5A-TOP2B binding in vitro (Fig. 3, J and K) and this requirement is also detectable in mESCs under conditions of ETO treatment (Fig. 3G, right), we next examined the impact of CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116 amino acids) on genome organization via Micro-C (Fig. 4 and figs. S8 and S9). While notable changes in active and inactive compartments were not detected, as expected (fig. S8A), differential loop analysis indicated mild alterations in looping interactions (fig. S8B). We observed that CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) led to mildly reduced looping interactions relative to WT by aggregate peak analysis (APA) (fig. S8, C and D). Similarly, the looping interactions were mildly reduced in CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) at loop anchors as categorized by the occupancy of CTCF, PDS5A, and CTCF-PDS5A (Fig. 4A and see fig. S8E for CTCF-PDS5A-RAD21–containing loops). These decreased signals observed at loop anchors containing CTCF, PDS5A, and CTCF-PDS5A (Fig. 4A) could be attributable to the slight reduction in CTCF and PDS5A binding in CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) (figs. S5, C and G, and S7, A and B). In particular, two example loci with significant alterations in gene expression reside in regions wherein a mild reduction in looping interactions was observed in CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) compared to WT (Fig. 4, B and C), as depicted along with underlying ChIP-seq and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) signal intensities (see fig. S9 for individual replicates). Upon CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116), we observed a mild down-regulation of loops at the Adgrv1 and Cnga3 loci accompanied by up-regulation of these genes and a slight alteration in CTCF and PDS5A binding, respectively (Fig. 4, B and C, and fig. S9). Notably, the loops lost or gained in CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) compared to WT appeared to overlap with 17.9 and 18.9% of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116), respectively, while retained loops overlapped with 9.8% of DEGs (fig. S8F).


### CTCF N terminus ∆95 to 116 amino acid mutant affects gene expression in mESCs
  In addition to the mild impact on genome organization, CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) affected global gene expression in mESCs (Fig. 4, D to F, and fig. S10). Comparison of gene expression from CTCF-WT versus CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) by RNA-seq gave evidence of 1718 DEGs [adjusted P (Padj) < 0.05] in the latter case (Fig. 4D). These DEGs corresponded to biological processes involved in development, cell signaling, and metabolism (Fig. 4E), and ∼64% of them were found in proximity to PDS5A ChIP-seq peaks in CTCF-MUT2 (Fig. 4F and see fig. S10F).

  As we observed that CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) also affected TOP2B interaction in the context of CTCF-PDS5A-TOP2B (Fig. 3G, right), we analyzed gene expression in CTCF-WT versus CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) using RNA-seq (fig. S10A); 3143 genes were differentially expressed in the latter case (Padj < 0.05; see fig. S10A). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that mainly ribosome biogenesis pathways were affected (fig. S10B), and ∼63% of DEGs were found in proximity to PDS5A ChIP-seq peaks in CTCF-MUT1 (fig. S10, D and E). Thus, a different set of genes was affected in CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) as compared to CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116), confirming that these mutants are not redundant (fig. S10C).

  When DEGs in CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) and CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) were compared with DEGs upon CTCF degradation in mESCs (16), 11 and 14% of them overlapped, respectively (fig. S10, G and H). Notably, when DEGs in CTCF-MUT1(∆13–33) and CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) were compared with DEGs in PDS5A knockout mESCs (13), 33 and 35% of them overlapped, respectively (fig. S10, I and J). In addition, the analysis of PDS5A ChIP-seq signal across DEGs in CTCF-MUT1 and CTCF-MUT2 indicated a significant reduction in CTCF-MUT1 (fig. S10K) or CTCF-MUT2 (fig. S10L) compared to WT, yet similar PDS5A ChIP-seq reduction was also observed in non-DEGs, as expected based on the genome-wide analysis of PDS5A (fig. S7B). Together, our results show that CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116), which cannot bind to PDS5A-TOP2B in vitro, led to mildly reduced loop formation and altered gene expression in mESCs in vivo.


### In gliomas, PDS5A and TOP2B protein levels are correlated across tumors
  GBM are the most lethal among brain tumors and exhibit heterogeneity in gene expression. We previously showed that in a subset of gliomas, TOP2B is overexpressed and thereby modulates oncogene expression and tumor growth (31). Given the functional link described herein between TOP2B and PDS5A in mESCs and their association in gene expression and genome organization, we next tested whether TOP2B and PDS5A expression levels are correlated in gliomas. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) of a brain tumor tissue array including normal samples and all tumor grade specimens in adults indicated correlative levels of TOP2B and PDS5A (Fig. 5, A to C, and see fig. S11 for controls), which support their interaction in gliomas and suggest a possible functional association in tumors. Moreover, we used GlioVis data portal for visualization and analysis of brain tumor expression datasets (44). Using the CGGA dataset with 1019 RNA-seq samples from all glioma tumor grades in adults, we found that TOP2B and PDS5A expression correlated in gliomas [Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) = 0.72, P < 0.001] (Fig. 5, D to F). In addition to their correlative RNA expression levels, we used cBioPortal (45–47) to analyze a GBM dataset with 99 adult patient samples with protein quantification data determined by mass spectrometry (48). Consistent with the RNA expression levels, we found that TOP2B and PDS5A protein abundance is correlated in GBM [Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) = 0.66, P = 6.91 × 10−14] (Fig. 5G). We next assessed the protein levels of TOP2B and PDS5A in the glioma cell lines via WB and found that both are higher in U87MG and BT142 cells, as compared with controls (Fig. 5H). As BT142 glioma cells expressed the highest levels of TOP2B and PDS5A and, additionally, RAD21 appears to be elevated in these cells, relative to U87MG and A172 (Fig. 5H), we next investigated the potential functional association between TOP2B and PDS5A in glioma using BT142 cells.


### PDS5A recruitment is enhanced by TOP2B activity at CTCF binding sites in BT142 glioma cells
  We first examined TOP2B and PDS5A colocalization on chromatin in BT142 glioma cells after DMSO or ETO treatment, followed by ChIP-seq for CTCF, PDS5A, and TOP2B (Fig. 6). As previously observed in mESCs, CTCF binding is not affected by ETO treatment (Fig. 6A, left). We observed a gain in PDS5A under conditions of ETO, but not DMSO treatment (Fig. 6, A to D). Similar to our results shown above (Fig. 2, J to L), the binding sites of PDS5A overlapped those of CTCF (Fig. 6B). Thus, the enrichment in PDS5A binding following TOP2 trapping on chromatin (ETO) was localized mainly to CTCF binding sites (Fig. 6, A to D), and TOP2B colocalized with both CTCF and PDS5A across these regions (Fig. 6, A to D). TOP2B was also observed at other locations without CTCF (Fig. 6E and fig. S12). In other words, CTCF, PDS5A, and TOP2B colocalization was evident in ∼27% of TOP2B-bound sites (see Fig. 6B).

  In addition to their genome-wide cooccupancy, TOP2B and PDS5A localization was observed at highly expressed oncogenes, such as PDGFRA and MYC (Fig. 6, F and G), for which the localization of TOP2B activity and transcriptional modulation were described earlier (31). To search for transcription factors that may be associated with the TOP2B binding sites in glioma cells, we used the TOP2B ChIP-seq data attained under DMSO conditions. We found that those TOP2B signals corresponded not only to CTCF binding motifs but also to the binding motifs associated with other insulation factors including MAZ, PATZ1, VEZF1, and other ZNFs (Fig. 6H and fig. S12F), reflecting the results obtained above for PDS5A (fig. S4B). Overall, our results in gliomas highlight the existence of a previously unrecognized functional link between the active form of TOP2B and the enrichment of PDS5A on chromatin, mainly located at CTCF cobound regions.


### PDS5A KD results in reduced occupancy of TOP2B on chromatin in BT142 glioma cells
  Given the link between TOP2B catalytical activity and PDS5A enrichment on chromatin, we tested whether PDS5A functionally cooperates in localizing TOP2B on chromatin. To do so, we used three different short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) driven by an inducible Tet-On promoter in BT142 cells to KD PDS5A. TOP2B, PDS5A/B, and CTCF protein levels were not affected by induction of PDS5A KD with doxycycline (Dox), with histone H3 as loading control (Fig. 7A). Using this system, we assessed whether PDS5A KD affected TOP2B localization either at CTCF binding sites or at PDS5A binding sites genome wide. We performed ChIP-seq in WT (untreated) and under KD (Dox) conditions and observed that PDS5A KD resulted in the reduction of TOP2B signal at both CTCF-PDS5A-TOP2B overlapping sites (Fig. 7, B, C, and E, and see Fig. 7, D and F, as controls) and overall at PDS5A-bound sites genome wide (fig. S13, A and C, and see fig. S13, B and D, as controls). Global alterations in PDS5A, CTCF, and TOP2B binding were also noted upon PDS5A KD (see fig. S13, E to K). As expected in the controls, PDS5A KD led to reduced PDS5A levels (Fig. 7, C and D, and fig. S13, A, B, J, and K) and did not affect CTCF localization (Fig. 7, C and F, and fig. S13, A and D). Specifically, we observed that genes coregulated by CTCF, PDS5A, and TOP2B, such as IDH2, exhibited the loss of TOP2B upon PDS5A KD (Fig. 7G). On the basis of these results, we reasoned that TOP2B was either lost or relocalized on the genome upon PDS5A KD, compared to WT. Further analysis indicated that PDS5A KD appeared to lead to TOP2B relocalization in the genome (figs. S13G and S14).

  To gain insight into those genes that may be coregulated by PDS5A, TOP2B, and CTCF, we evaluated global gene expression using RNA-seq after the pharmacological inhibition of TOP2 for 6 hours with ICRF-193, an inhibitor of TOP2A/B enzymatic activity. The pharmacological inhibition of TOP2 resulted in ∼4000 genes (Padj < 0.05) being differentially expressed as compared to the untreated case (Fig. 7H; see fig. S15A for GO analysis). About 70% of DEGs upon ICRF-193 treatment were found in proximity of TOP2B ChIP-seq peaks in BT142 cells (fig. S15B). Given that TOP2B activity enriched PDS5A on chromatin in gliomas, we tested whether PDS5A KD affects gene expression similar to TOP2 enzymatic inhibition. Upon inducing PDS5A KD in BT142 glioma cells, ∼2600 genes (Padj < 0.05) were differentially expressed (Fig. 7I). We found that PDS5A KD affected biological pathways (fig. S15C), some of which resembled those affected by TOP2 enzymatic inhibition (fig. S15A). In the case of PDS5A KD, 80% of DEGs were found in proximity to PDS5A ChIP-seq peaks in BT142 cells (fig. S15D). On the basis of the association of TOP2B with a tumor growth phenotype described earlier (31), we verified that about 34 and 23% of cell-cycle–associated genes were differentially expressed upon TOP2 inhibition or PDS5A KD, respectively (fig. S15E). A total of 820 genes of 4066 DEGs upon TOP2 pharmacological inhibition overlapped with DEGs upon PDS5A KD (Fig. 7J; see fig. S15F for GO analysis). These 820 overlapping DEGs derived from TOP2 inhibition and PDS5A KD were mainly localized (74%) at both PDS5A and TOP2B ChIP-seq binding sites (fig. S15G). Together, these findings showed that catalytical inhibition of TOP2B and the reduction in PDS5A levels have an overlapping influence on gene expression and on the cellular processes affected in glioma cells.


### PDS5A genome occupancy mediates sensitivity to TOP2 cancer drugs in gliomas
  We next examined whether the observed TOP2B relocalization on the genome upon PDS5A KD can mediate the sensitivity/resistance capacity of BT142 glioma cells to TOP2 drugs. First, we confirmed that the cell growth of BT142 cells was similar whether the Tet-On shPDS5A was present under WT (–Dox) or PDS5A KD (+Dox) conditions (Fig. 7K). We then analyzed whether PDS5A levels modulate resistance/sensitivity to TOP2 drugs by treating PDS5A-WT versus PDS5A KD BT142 cells with TOP2-targeting drugs (i.e., ETO or doxorubicin). Upon treatment with ETO (0 and 0.25 μM) (Fig. 7L) or doxorubicin (0, 2.5, 5, and 7.5 μM) (Fig. 7M), a fraction of PDS5A KD BT142 glioma cells survived as compared to the control, PDS5A-intact BT142 cells. Thus, modulation of PDS5A levels within glioma cells mediates their sensitivity to treatment with TOP2 drugs.


## DISCUSSION
  The three-dimensional chromatin architecture facilitates transcription, DNA replication, recombination, and chromatin remodeling. While these cellular processes alter the topological state of the genome, the topoisomerases actively keep the DNA in its proper state (49). Specifically, TOP2B interacts with cohesin and CTCF on chromatin to release DNA tortional stress (28, 29). Although TOP2B-induced DSB resolves DNA topological problems during transcription (26, 27), DSB generates recurrent hotspots for chromosomal rearrangements that drive cancer (50, 51). Furthermore, TOP2B colocalization with CTCF and/or the cohesin complex (i.e., CTCF-RAD21-TOP2B and/or RAD21-TOP2B binding sites) promotes somatic mutations observed in many cancers (30). However, the molecular mechanism of TOP2B recruitment has remained elusive. Here, we show that PDS5A, a regulatory subunit of the cohesin complex, cooperates with TOP2B for their recruitment on chromatin in the context of CTCF and the cohesin complex in mouse cells, as well as in human glioma models.

  We observed that PDS5A/B and TOP2B colocalized with CTCF and that TOP2B activity or the product of its activity (i.e., DSB) enriched PDS5A, but not PDS5B, genome wide. Furthermore, motif analyses of de novo PDS5A binding regions indicated motifs that match not only those of CTCF but also those of other insulation factors (i.e., MAZ, PATZ1, and ZNFs), suggesting that the cooperative recruitment of PDS5A-TOP2B occurs on chromatin and possibly at chromatin boundaries demarcated by insulation factors (Fig. 8A). PDS5A remains bound on chromatin upon CTCF degradation. This outcome is consistent with a model in which other insulation factors identified in our motif analyses cooperate to recruit or maintain PDS5A at CTCF binding sites and other chromatin boundaries. Supporting this model, we previously observed that the loss of PDS5A leads to a further disruption of the Hoxa5|6 boundary in cervical MNs that had been derived from mESCs bearing CTCF motif deletions at the HoxA cluster. In this differentiation system, the Hoxa7 gene that had already been derepressed upon the loss of CTCF binding was now aberrantly up-regulated upon the additional absence of PDS5A; accordingly, PDS5A was detected as a top-ranking candidate in CRISPR loss-of-function screens (6). Overall, our results point to the unique enrichment of PDS5A genome wide, including at chromatin boundaries as delimited by CTCF or other insulation factors, through TOP2B enzymatic activity that is critical for the resolution of topological stress in cells.

  In addition to a previously reported CTCF-PDS5A–interacting region (12), we identified another CTCF-PDS5A–interacting region that facilitates TOP2B localization at CTCF binding sites on chromatin. Notably, deletions in these CTCF-PDS5A–interacting regions affected different gene sets, validating that these regions are not redundant. In particular, the deletion of CTCF-PDS5A interaction region, CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116), led to DEGs that overlap with PDS5A binding sites and mildly reduced looping interactions in mESCs. Underscoring the cooperation between PDS5A-TOP2B, the loss of either of the CTCF-PDS5A–interacting regions reduced TOP2B interaction upon ETO treatment, known to trap active TOP2B. In accordance, PDS5A KD resulted in decreased TOP2B occupancy at CTCF-PDS5A-TOP2B–occupied regions in glioma cells. Thus, we propose that proper gene expression and genome organization require an intact N-terminal domain of CTCF that is critical for its interaction with PDS5A-TOP2B and that this interaction is modulated upon activating TOP2B.

  Cell-type–specific distal gene regulation is dependent on the cohesin loading cycle, including its accessory loading and unloading activities (15, 52, 53). Previous work showed that PDS5A/B (11, 14), WAPL (11), and cohesin acetylation control chromatin loop length at CTCF binding sites (52, 54–57), consistent with the mild reduction of loops in CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) cells. Similarly, CTCF(∆13–33) was reported to have a mild effect on genome organization (12). Nonetheless, we highlight that loss of either CTCF or the CTCF N-terminal domain (1 to 265), which interacts not only with PDS5A but also with the cohesin complex, shows notable impacts on genome folding (12, 16, 38). The unloading of the cohesin complex from chromatin is facilitated by the PDS5-WAPL interaction (39, 58, 59), yet our study did not detect WAPL interaction with PDS5A-TOP2B nor WAPL enrichment on chromatin by TOP2B activity. In relation to cohesin loading, whereas the interaction of NIPBL-cohesin favors cohesin adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activity (60), the interaction of PDS5A-cohesin results in a reduction of this cohesin ATPase activity (15, 54, 60). However, the molecular mechanism of the exchange between NIPBL and PDS5A on cohesin at chromatin boundaries is not understood. Further biochemical experiments could address whether TOP2B activity favors PDS5A exchange for NIPBL on cohesin at CTCF binding sites or other insulation factor binding sites. Pds5 is involved in the inhibition of loop enlargement despite the lack of CTCF in yeast (56, 61), and overexpression of Top2 specifically suppressed temperature sensitivity of Pds5 mutants in yeast (62). Given that the activity of TOP2B regulates PDS5A chromatin occupancy and that KD of PDS5A alters TOP2B occupancy reciprocally, our study points to an interplay between PDS5A-TOP2B and CTCF in cohesin dynamics and the regulation of loops.

  In GBM, some genetic alterations have been associated with epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylator phenotype (CIMP) in IDH1/2 mutant cells (63, 64), insulator dysfunction (20), histone mutations of H3K27 (65–67), and three-dimensional genome organization (68–71). On the basis of our results indicating the interaction of PDS5A-TOP2B-CTCF, the dependence of PDS5A occupancy on TOP2B enzymatic activity in mESCs and gliomas, and the previously shown link between TOP2B enzymatic activity and oncogene expression in gliomas (31), we further investigated the correlation of TOP2B and PDS5A expression in gliomas. Given that PDS5A and TOP2B cooperate for their recruitment on chromatin via CTCF, perturbing the interaction between CTCF-PDS5A affecting the number of loops in mESCs and the localization of TOP2B in both mESCs and glioma cells. Reduced TOP2B occupancy is observed upon PDS5A KD, while the enrichment of PDS5A on chromatin required TOP2B enzymatic activity. Consistent with the model proposed (Fig. 8B), PDS5A KD in glioma cells leads to a relocation of TOP2B on chromatin that further contributes to the heterogeneity in gene expression reported and, thus, might mediate variable responses to TOP2 drugs.

  Many cancer drugs targeting TOP2A/B, such as ETO and doxorubicin, are frequently used in cancer therapies. However, secondary malignancies have been associated with TOP2A/B at CTCF binding sites (50). In addition, CTCF became a hotspot frequently associated with some translocations in therapy-related myeloid leukemias and cancer drug resistance (37, 50). As TOP2B contributes to the disentanglement of CTCF/other insulator–mediated chromatin loops to release torsional stress affecting vital cellular processes, TOP2B enzymatic function is directly implicated in TOP2-drug resistance in cancer (72, 73). Notably, TOP2B relocation upon PDS5A KD facilitates resistance to ETO and doxorubicin in glioma cells. Thus, we envision that the dynamics of PDS5A-TOP2B occupancy in the genome might clarify GBM-associated heterogeneity in expression and inform about sensitivity to TOP2-drug response. Together, our study highlights the cooperativity of PDS5A-TOP2B for their genomic localization and demonstrates that PDS5A mediates sensitivity to TOP2 drugs in glioma cells.


### Limitations of the study
  The TOP2 inhibitors used in this study induce both TOP2A and TOP2B cleavage complexes. However, we present complementary gene silencing experiments supporting that PDS5A interacts specifically with TOP2B in glioma cells. We also studied PDS5A and TOP2B interaction in mESCs as a representative normal phenotype to support our conclusions. We further addressed this limitation by presenting experiments in MNs differentiated from mESCs. The TOP2B protein levels are higher in MNs relative to the undifferentiated cells, and TOP2A protein levels decreased upon differentiation. We relied on glioma cell lines in culture, in which characterization of gene expression and ChIP-seq were performed in vitro. These in vitro experiments could be influenced by artifacts and changes in tumor cell phenotype that relate to culture conditions. These latter limitations were addressed by our complementary analysis of human glioma and brain tissues.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Cell culture
  E14 mESC (ES-E14TG2a) cell line from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; CRL-1821) was cultured in standard medium supplemented with leukemia inhibitory factor under 2i conditions [1 mM mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 1/2 inhibitor (PD0325901, Stemgent) and 3 mM GSK3 inhibitor (CHIR99021, Stemgent)]. In the case of CTCF degradation, CTCF-degron mESCs were treated with auxin for 48 hours, as described earlier (16). Differentiation into cervical MNs was performed using a protocol described previously (6, 10). Briefly, mESCs were differentiated into embryoid bodies in 2 days, and further patterning was induced with the addition of 1 μM all–trans–retinoic acid (MilliporeSigma) and 0.5 μM smoothened agonist (Calbiochem). 293FT cells were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (R70007) and cultured in standard medium as described in the manufacturer’s protocol. The identity of the BT142 IDH1 R132H cell line from ATCC (ACS-1018) was confirmed by short tandem repeat, and the cells were cultured with NeuroCult NS-A Proliferation kit (catalog no. 5751, STEMCELL Technologies), recombinant human epidermal growth factor (20 ng/ml; catalog no. 100-15, PeproTech), recombinant human platelet-derived growth factor–AA (PDGF-AA; 100 ng/ml; catalog no. 100-13A, PeproTech), recombinant human fibroblast growth factor (20 ng/ml; catalog no. 233-FB, R&D Systems), heparan sulfate (2 μg/ml; catalog no. H3149, Sigma-Aldrich), and antibiotics (1×) (31).


### ChIP-seq and native ChIP-seq under ETO/DMSO conditions
  ChIP-seq-ETO and native ChIP-seq were performed with 5 × 107 cells that were harvested after growth in cell suspension. For ChIP-seq, the cells were treated with 500 μM ETO or DMSO for 10 min in culture medium, washed 1× with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then resuspended in cross-linking medium using 1% formaldehyde (1% FA) or non-FA, as indicated. Nuclear extracts were obtained, and chromatin was fragmented to ∼500 base pairs (bp) using a Covaris sonicator. Ten micrograms of the antibodies listed in table S1 was incubated overnight. In addition, chromatin from Drosophila (1:100 ratio to mESC-derived or BT142-derived chromatin) and Drosophila-specific H2Av antibody were used as a spike-in control in each sample. Then, washes were performed four times with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, followed by a final wash of TE containing NaCl (50 mmol/liter). The cross-link was reversed, and DNA was purified using phenol:chloroform:isoamyl alcohol for library preparation and sequencing.


### Immunoprecipitation
  Nuclear extracts were prepared as described previously (74, 75). Briefly, buffer A [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 10 mM KC1, and 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)] was used to remove the cytoplasmic fraction, and buffer C [20 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 25% (v/v) glycerol, 420 mM NaCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.2 mM EDTA] was used to obtain the soluble nuclear extracts. The remaining insoluble nuclear pellet containing chromatin-bound fraction was treated with 250 U of benzonase (#E1014, MilliporeSigma) and incubated with rotation in the cold room to digest nucleic acids before IP. When indicated as benzonase untreated, the chromatin-bound fraction was sonicated for solubilization (Diagenode Bioruptor; five pulses of 20 s, low intensity). Anti-FLAG M2 Magnetic Beads (MilliporeSigma, #M8823) were used for IP, and proteins bound to the beads were released by competitive elution using 3X FLAG peptide. For other IP experiments, the specific antibodies used are listed in table S1.


### Expression of recombinant proteins
  cDNA of the protein of interest was cloned into CβF plasmid and transfected into 5 × 108 293FT cells using polyethylenimine (PEI). After 60 hours, cells were harvested by washing with cold 1× PBS, scraping and incubating with TMSD buffer [20 mM Hepes, 5 mM MgCl2, sucrose (85.5 g/liter), 25 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors] for 10 min. After centrifugation to pellet the cells, the supernatant containing the cytoplasmic fraction was removed. The nuclei fraction was further incubated in BA450 buffer (20 mM Hepes, 450 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and protease inhibitors) for 30 min, the insoluble material was pelleted, and the supernatant containing the nuclear extract was used for FLAG affinity purification for 12 hours. FLAG beads were washed 3× with BA450 and then 3× with BA50 (20 mM Hepes, 50 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol). Ultimately, the proteins were eluted using 3X FLAG peptide with rotation at 4°C overnight.


### IP of FLAG-mPDS5A(1–750 amino acids)
  CβF-mPDS5A(1-750 amino acids) construct was transfected into 293FT cells using PEI, and nuclei were fractionated using TMSD buffer [20 mM Hepes, 5 mM MgCl2, sucrose (85.5 g/liter), 25 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and protease inhibitors]. After cytosol removal, nuclear extraction was processed as described above for recombinant proteins using BA450 buffer (20 mM Hepes, 450 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, and protease inhibitors). FLAG affinity purification and 3X FLAG peptide elution were performed in BA50 buffer with rotation at 4°C overnight.


### RNA sequencing
  Total RNA from mESCs or BT142 cells was purified with RNAeasy Plus Mini kit (QIAGEN) using the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 1 μg of RNA was used to prepare standard RNA-seq libraries according to the manufacturer’s instructions by the Onco-Genomics Shared Facilities at the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine.


### WB analysis
  Cells were washed in 1× PBS, resuspended in RIPA buffer [150 mM NaCl, 10 mM Hepes (pH 7.9), 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and 0.1% sodium deoxycholate], and incubated for 20 min, and proteins were spun down and quantified. WB analysis was used to evaluate protein abundance using the antibodies. Antibodies used are listed in table S1.


### Electrophoretic mobility shift assays
  Single-stranded 5′Cy3-oligonucleotides containing the cHS4 FII CTCF binding site were resuspended in a 100 mM stock solution using 10 mM Hepes (pH 7.5) and annealed. A total of 1 mM probe was resuspended in binding buffer [10 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 50 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM ZnSO4, and 0.1 μg of salmon sperm DNA]. The reactions were then incubated with increasing amounts (0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 μg) of mouse recombinant CTCF, either WT or CTCFMut2(∆95–116) for 1 hour at 25°C and/or PDS5A (0.4 μg) for 1 hour at 25°C. The reactions were then run on 5.2% acrylamide gels for 30 min at room temperature with 150 V and 0.25× tris-borate EDTA buffer. Last, the acrylamide gel was exposed using the imager settings for UV-Cy3.


### Immunohistochemistry
  Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded human glioma tissue microarray (TissueArray.com GL2082a) was deparaffinized with xylene, and antigen retrieval was performed with sodium citrate buffer (10 mmol/liter, pH 6). IHC staining was performed on a Leica BOND-MAX automatic immunostainer. IHC was performed for PDS5A (Novogen), PDGFRA (5241, Cell Signaling Technology), and TOP2B (ab72334, Abcam) antibodies. Counterstaining was done with hematoxylin and eosin. Visiopharm software was used to quantify the PDS5A-, PDGFRA-, or TOP2B-positive cells per total cell number as indicated in the figure legend. Parameters were adjusted to detect nuclei first by hematoxylin staining. We applied a threshold to the mean intensity of DAB (3,3′-diaminobenzidine) chromogen for all the cells to define positive staining nuclei for PDS5A and TOP2B. Cytoplasmic PDGFRA staining was quantified after applying a threshold to the mean intensity of the DAB chromogen for all cells. The same thresholds and parameters were used for all tumor samples (see tables S2 to S5 for IHC quantifications).


### CTCF WT and mutant rescues
  CTCF-degron (CTCF-GFP-AID-Tir1) cells (1 × 105) were transfected using Lipofectamine reagent (11668019, Invitrogen) with pPB-CAG-FLAG-HA-CTCF WT, pPB-CAG-FLAG-HA-CTCF(∆13–33 amino acids), or PB-CAG-FLAG-HA-CTCF(∆95–116 amino acids). After 72 hours, cells were selected with hygromycin (0.2 mg/ml), and protein expression levels were assessed by WB.


### shRNA-PDS5A Tet-On system in BT142 cells
  BT142 cells were infected with shRNA-PDS5A-Tet-On system (TRIPZ, Horizon) viral particles for 12 hours. Cells were selected with puromycin (1 μg/ml) after 72 hours postinfection, and protein expression levels were tested. PDS5A KD following Dox (1 μg/ml) for 72 hours and the shRNA-based PDS5A silencing in a Tet-On system was determined by WB.


### Micro-C preparation
  Dovetail Micro-C Kit was used for Micro-C library preparation according to the manufacturer’s protocol. One million cells were used for Micro-C experiments in each replicate in WT versus CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) conditions across duplicates. Briefly, disuccinimidyl glutarate and FA were used for cross-linking chromatin in the nucleus. Then, the cross-linked chromatin was digested with micrococcal nuclease in situ. After digestion, chromatin fragments were extracted by lysing with SDS and binding to chromatin capture beads. Next, chromatin ends were repaired and ligated to a biotinylated bridge adapter through proximity ligation. Last, the cross-links were reversed, the associated proteins were degraded, and the DNA was purified and used for library preparation using Illumina-compatible adaptors. After library preparation, biotin-containing fragments were isolated using streptavidin beads before polymerase chain reaction amplification. Micro-C libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq X platform to generate >800 million 2 × 150 bp read pairs to ensure the required data depth for loop analysis (see table S6 for the number of reads across Micro-C samples).


### Cell growth curve
  BT142 cells expressing Tet-On shPDS5A under WT (–Dox) versus PDS5A KD (+Dox) conditions were seeded onto 96-well plates at a concentration of 2000 cells per well. Dox was added at day 0.5, and the cells were followed for 10 days. CellTiter-Glo 2.0 (G9242) luminescent reagent was used for lysis and detection of viable cells. Three technical replicates per condition in two independent experiments were performed. A Student’s t test showing P < 0.05 was considered significant.


### Cell survival assay
  BT142 cells expressing Tet-On shPDS5A under WT (–Dox) versus PDS5A KD (+Dox) conditions were seeded onto 96-well plates at a concentration of 5000 cells per well. TOP2 drugs, ETO (1226, Tocris) (0 and 0.25 μM), and doxorubicin (D1515, Sigma-Aldrich) (0, 2.5, 5, and 7.5 μM) were added to the medium. After 0, 24, 48, and 72 hours of treatment, CellTiter-Glo 2.0 (G9242, Promega) luminescent reagent was used for lysis and detection of viable cells. Four technical replicates per condition in two independent experiments were performed. A Student’s t test showing P < 0.05 was considered significant.


### Data analysis

#### ChIP-seq analysis
  ChIP-seq data were analyzed as described previously (6, 10). Briefly, Bowtie 2 (version 2.3.4.1) was used for mapping the sequence reads to mm10, hg38, or dm6 reference genomes using default parameters. Quality filtering and duplicate removal were performed using SAMtools (version 1.9). MACS (version 1.4.2) was used for narrow peak calling using default parameters of “macs2.” After normalization with spike-in read counts or total read counts as indicated, ChIP-seq heatmaps, density plots, venn diagrams, and tracks were generated. deepTools in R was used for generating heatmaps. Venn diagrams were generated using “ChIPpeakAnno” package (version 3.36.1) from Bioconductor. The Venn diagram sizes were drawn using the online tools: “http://barc.wi.mit.edu/tools/venn/” and “www.meta-chart.com/venn.” Normalized ChIP-seq tracks were visualized in Integrative Genomics Viewer (version 2.16.2). ChIP-seq replicates were evaluated by visualizing at Integrative Genomics Viewer and generating heatmaps. ChIPseeker was used for ChIP-seq data annotation to the genes within ±3-kb distance from transcription start site. For motif analysis, ChIP-seq “bed” file coordinates were converted into “fasta” using fetch sequences tool within Regulatory Sequence Analysis Tools, MEME (version 5.5.8) was used for motif analysis of PDS5A and TOP2B in mESCs and/or BT142 cells, and Tomtom (version 5.5.8) was used as a motif comparison tool. Motif search in MEME was performed de novo until 1000 sites were reached and the E values corresponding to the motifs were depicted.


#### Micro-C analysis
  The Micro-C analysis was performed by Dovetail using the tools shown in https://micro-c.readthedocs.io/en/latest/index.html. Briefly, HiC contact matrices were generated in each sample by aligning FASTQ files and generating valid pairs bam files. After assessment of each replicate and merging the replicates, the loops were identified using HiCCUPs from the Juicer package (76) and Mustache (77), with default parameters. Micro-C comparative analysis between CTCF-WT versus CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) samples was performed using HiCcompare algorithm by Dovetail. As HiCcompare method applies a joint normalization for CTCF-WT versus CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) conditions before the differential analysis, this process ensures the statistical robustness of the loops called (see table S7 for differential loops). In this method, an MD normalization plot was generated to visualize the effect of normalization between the conditions to ensure that the data were appropriate for the downstream difference detection. Moreover, the standard outputs generated here include a table of differentially interacting genomic coordinates, the frequency of interactions, the difference, and the permutation P values (for pipeline, see https://micro-c.readthedocs.io/en/latest/microc_compare.html). FAN-C toolkit was further used for generating AB compartments by Dovetail, and the resulting tables were presented as bar plots in GraphPad Prism. APA analysis and visualization of loops: Normalized APA analysis was performed for all the loops identified by Mustache (16-kb loops) in WT versus CTCF-MUT2(∆95–116) ESCs using 10-kb resolution using Juicer APA tools (76). All loop anchors identified by Mustache were annotated to the genes (table S8 and S9) using BEDTools suite (version 2.27.1) (78). Visualization of the zoomed-in coordinates/loci in Micro-C was performed using Juicebox (79). HiCcompare differential loop outcomes were plotted using RStudio. Analysis of PDS5A, CTCF, and RAD21 ChIP-seq occupancy at loop anchors: Chromatin loops called by Mustache were used for overlapping with PDS5A, CTCF, and RAD21 ChIP-seq peaks using pairToBed from the BEDTools suite (version 2.27.1) (78) and pgltools (version 1.2.1) (80). Afterward, APA plots were generated at the overlapping regions using Juicer APA tools.


#### RNA-seq analysis
  RNA-seq data were analyzed as described for BT142 cells (31) and for mESCs (6). Briefly, the sequence reads were mapped to mm10 and hg38 reference genome using Bowtie 2 (version 2.3.4.1), and normalized differential gene expression was obtained with DEseq2 (version 1.26.0) using the Wald test built into DEseq2 with a false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of 0.05. Differential gene expression values and corresponding P values are listed in tables S10, S12, S14, and S16. For GO analysis, the PANTHER database was used, and the PANTHER overrepresentation test tool was used for statistical analysis. The relevant GO terms are listed in tables S11, S13, S15, S17, and S18. Venn diagrams were drawn using online tools: Venny (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/), Venn Diagram Generator (http://barc.wi.mit.edu/tools/venn/), and Venn Diagram Maker (www.meta-chart.com/venn#/display). In the case of reanalysis of RNA-seq upon TOP2 inhibition in BT142 cells (31), the deposited gene counts in GEO (GSE133561) dataset were used for further analysis, as described above. For comparisons of RNA-seq from CTCF degradation (16) or PDS5A knockout (13), the reported DEGs were used.


#### Quantification and statistical analysis
  Statistical analysis related to experiments has been described above in each section. In addition, the two-sided Student’s t test was generally used in the cases where two populations were compared.
