# Allele-specific chromatin architecture shapes imprinted domains and coordinates a distal enhancer and antisense transcription at the mouse Mest-Copg2 domain


## Abstract
  Genomic imprinting results in parent-of-origin-dependent gene expression, but how three-dimensional genome organization contributes to imprinted gene regulation remains unclear. Using Capture Hi-C in mouse cortex and primary cortical neurons, we identified parental allele-specific chromatin architectures across multiple imprinted domains. These architectures largely originate from imprinting control regions and correlate with DNA methylation-sensitive CTCF binding. Active and inactive alleles of imprinted genes show distinct promoter interaction profiles and differential engagement with distal regulatory elements in both contact frequency and the epigenetic state of distal regions. A CRISPR interference screen identified a distal enhancer that regulates Mest-Copg2 imprinted expression through allele-specific chromatin interactions. In neurons, this enhancer activates Copg2 on the maternal allele, whereas on the paternal allele it drives Mest isoforms transcribed antisense to Copg2 and contributes to Copg2 repression. In summary, we show that allele-specific chromatin architecture coordinates maternal enhancer activity and paternal antisense transcription to control imprinted expression in neurons.


## Introduction
  Three-dimensional (3D) genome organization in the nucleus spans multiple hierarchical scales and has been widely implicated in gene regulation1,2. At the compartment level, chromatin is partitioned into A and B compartments, which largely correspond to transcriptionally active and inactive regions, respectively3. At a finer scale, chromatin is organized into topologically associating domains (TADs)4,5, within which gene regulatory mechanisms, such as chromatin loop-mediated enhancer-promoter communication, take place6–8. TADs also serve to insulate regulatory effects between neighboring domains9,10, although recent evidence suggests that some regulatory crosstalk can occur across TAD boundaries11. Allele-specific chromatin architectures have been identified in both genome-wide Hi-C studies in mouse and human cells7,12,13 and in locus-specific studies at imprinted domains14–18, revealing parent-of-origin-specific chromatin organization. Since genomic imprinting is an epigenetic phenomenon where gene expression is restricted to or biased toward a single parental allele, these observations provide a strong rationale for further dissecting the contribution of chromatin architecture to the regulation of imprinted expression.

  Imprinting control regions (ICRs), which correspond to germline-established differentially methylated regions (gDMRs), are key regulatory elements that orchestrate imprinted gene expression across long genomic distances and are defined by parent-of-origin-specific CpG methylation that is maintained throughout development19–22. These primary imprinting marks direct the establishment of secondary, somatic differentially methylated regions (sDMRs) at a subset of gene promoters during development20,23. Promoter-associated DMRs can directly regulate transcription by modulating access to regulatory sequences, thereby restricting expression to the unmethylated parental allele. However, some imprinted genes lack promoter-associated DMRs, indicating that ICRs can also exert control through long-range regulatory mechanisms.

  Two major mechanisms have been described for long-range regulation of imprinted genes: cis-acting long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and higher-order chromatin architecture. At many loci, unmethylated ICRs drive transcription of lncRNAs that act in cis to repress neighboring imprinted genes. For instance, Snhg14 lncRNA transcriptionally silences paternal Ube3a expression in neurons24,25, whereas Kcnq1ot1 lncRNA recruits and spreads repressive chromatin modifiers across neighboring genes26–28. At other loci, ICRs can regulate distal genes by organizing higher-order chromatin architecture through methylation-sensitive binding of the CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), a key mediator of chromatin looping29. This mechanism was first characterized at the H19-Igf2 locus, where CTCF binding at the unmethylated ICR insulates the maternal Igf2 promoter from its distal enhancer, restricting expression to the paternal allele14,30,31. More recently, additional locus-specific studies have shown that allele-specific chromatin interactions between enhancers and promoters are a central mechanism of imprinted expression14–18. Together, these studies suggest that imprinted expression can be regulated through both transcriptional and architectural mechanisms.

  However, a systematic examination of allele-specific chromatin architecture across imprinted domains is still lacking. Although recent technical advances have revealed allelic biases at sub-TAD scales, few studies have functionally linked these structures to imprinted expression. Moreover, how transcriptional repression by lncRNAs and chromatin insulation mechanisms act together to establish or maintain imprinted expression remains unclear.

  In this work, we generated high-resolution, allele-specific chromatin contact maps across multiple imprinted domains in the mouse brain, enabling systematic analysis of how DNA methylation at DMRs influences chromatin insulation and providing evidence for long-distance regulation. To identify functional cis-regulatory elements, we performed CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screens and identified a distal enhancer at the Mest-Copg2 domain whose activity is correlated with allele-specific chromatin interactions. We further show that Copg2 imprinted expression in neurons is regulated by both enhancer activity and antisense transcription, with the enhancer directly promoting maternal Copg2 through allele-specific chromatin interactions, while the nuclear-enriched antisense transcript MestXL selectively represses paternal Copg2. Together, these findings underscore the critical role of 3D genome folding in coordinating enhancer function and transcriptional repression at imprinted loci.


## Results

### Imprinted domains exhibit allele-specific chromatin architectures
  Given the characteristic monoallelic expression pattern of imprinted genes, we asked whether imprinted domains exhibit allele-specific chromatin organization. A recent study suggested the presence of such structures at a subset of imprinted domains in the mouse brain13. To systematically examine the relationship between allelic chromatin architecture and imprinted expression at higher resolution, we performed region Capture Hi-C on cortical tissue from reciprocal F1 hybrid mice (BL6 x CAST and CAST x BL6; two biological replicates per cross). Capture probes targeted ~23 Mb spanning eight imprinted domains, and libraries were sequenced to high depth (Supplementary Fig. 1a). After applying HICUP quality control for mapping, ligation, and PCR duplication, 10 to 17 million valid paired reads were obtained per sample (5–9% of total sequenced reads). Consistent with the spatial segregation of chromosomes, interchromosomal (trans) interactions occur at a lower frequency than intrachromosomal (cis) interactions (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Sequencing reads were phased by parental origin using strain-specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). Approximately 25% of valid reads were allelically informative, reflecting the fraction of both ends of reads overlapping strain-specific SNPs (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

  Using the cis-allelic read pairs, we generated allele-specific Hi-C contact matrices at up to 5 kb resolution (Supplementary Fig. 1c). To control for differences in allelic coverage and strain-of-origin effects, reads from reciprocal crosses were downsampled to equal depth and combined by parent-of-origin prior to analysis. Across the eight imprinted domains examined (Peg10-Sgce, Mest-Copg2, Peg3-Usp29, Snrpn-Peg12, H19-Igf2, Kcnq1-Kcnq1ot1, Meg3-Dio3, and Peg13-Kcnk9), most domains exhibited clear allele-specific chromatin architectures, although the magnitude of these differences varied between loci (Fig. 1a–p, and Supplementary Figs. 2–9). Importantly, the interaction patterns were highly consistent between reciprocal crosses, indicating that the observed differences reflect parent-of-origin effects rather than strain-specific differences (Supplementary Fig. 10).Fig. 1Imprinted domains exhibit allele-specific chromatin architectures.a–h Capture Hi-C contact matrices generated from allele-specific read pairs in hybrid mouse cortex at 5 kb resolution.   Allele-specific chromatin features observed by Capture Hi-C included distinct TAD-like structures, prominent chromatin loops between distal loci, and architectural stripes consistent with slow loop extrusion (Fig. 1a–h). Differences in chromatin architectures between the two parental alleles can be further visualized as allelic subtraction maps and loops specific to each parental allele (Fig. 1i–p). Collectively, these results revealed widespread allele-specific chromatin organization across imprinted domains in the mouse brain.


### Methylation-sensitive CTCF at ICRs shapes allelic architecture
  Given the central role of ICRs in regulating imprinted gene expression, we asked whether allele-specific chromatin architectures are anchored at ICRs and associated DMRs. Allele-specific DMRs, including both gDMRs and sDMRs, have been previously identified in the mouse brain32. To assess their relationship with chromatin organization, we performed pile-up contact analysis using our Capture Hi-C data, quantifying chromatin interactions within a 150 kb window centered on each DMR (Supplementary Fig. 11a).

  Pile-up analysis across all gDMRs (n = 8) revealed pronounced allele-specific differences in chromatin architecture. Methylated alleles at gDMRs displayed relatively uniform interaction patterns (insulation score = 0.90), whereas the unmethylated alleles exhibited strong local insulation (insulation score = 1.87), resulting in reduced interaction frequencies across the DMR (Fig. 2a). In contrast, pile-up analysis across sDMRs (n = 21) showed more modest allelic differences, with similar insulation scores between the methylated and unmethylated alleles at sDMRs (1.21 and 1.42, respectively; Fig. 2b).   To assess whether these insulation features are associated with CTCF binding, we performed CTCF CUT&Tag in hybrid primary cortical neurons. We observed strong allele-specific CTCF binding at imprinted domains, including 17 paternally biased and 4 maternally biased CTCF peaks (Fig. 2c). These peaks were detected within our Hi-C capture regions (Peg10-Sgce, Mest-Copg2, Peg3-Usp29, Snrpn-Peg12, H19-Igf2, Kcnq1-Kcnq1ot1, Meg3-Dio3, and Peg13-Kcnk9) and additional imprinted loci (H13, Gnas, Grb10, Commd1, and Impact), largely consistent with previous reports in mouse brain29.

  Notably, allele-specific CTCF binding was enriched at the unmethylated allele relative to the methylated allele at DMRs and coincided with regions of strong chromatin insulation (Fig. 2a,b). This enrichment was strongest at gDMRs but also observed at sDMRs, including the Meg3 promoter (Supplementary Fig. 11b). These results provide systematic support for a model in which DNA methylation-sensitive CTCF binding at ICRs contributes to allele-specific chromatin insulation across imprinted loci.

  We next asked whether loss of DNA methylation disrupts allele-specific CTCF binding and chromatin architecture at imprinted domains. To test this, we pharmacologically depleted DNA methylation in hybrid mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) using the DNA methyltransferase 1 inhibitor GSK-3484862 (DNMTi)33,34. Four days of DNMTi treatment strongly reduced DNA methylation at ICRs, decreasing methylation on the normally methylated allele from ~70% to ~5% (Supplementary Fig. 11c,d), and led to loss of allele-specific chromatin architectures. At the H19-Igf2 locus, a representative imprinted domain, DNMTi treatment resulted in maternalization of the paternal chromatin architecture (Supplementary Fig. 11e). More broadly, demethylation increased insulation at normally methylated alleles (0.98 in DMSO and 1.22 in DNMTi), accompanied by the emergence of biallelic CTCF binding at ICRs (Fig. 2d,e). In contrast, insulation at normally unmethylated alleles was largely unchanged (1.36 in DMSO and 1.32 in DNMTi; Fig. 2d,e). These results indicate that DNA methylation restricts CTCF binding and chromatin insulation to a single allele at imprinted domains. Because DNMTi induces genome-wide demethylation, these effects may reflect both direct loss of methylation at ICRs and indirect consequences of broader epigenetic changes.

  We then assessed how loss of DNA methylation and associated changes in chromatin architecture impact gene expression using allele-specific RNA-seq. Under control conditions, many imprinted genes maintained moderate-to-strong allelic bias (≥60% expression from one allele), likely reflecting partial loss of DNA methylation at DMRs in cultured mESCs. Disruption of DNA methylation and chromatin architecture was broadly associated with shifts in allelic expression toward more balanced (biallelic) expression. Paternally expressed genes (e.g., Snrpn, Kcnq1ot1, Peg3, Snhg14, and Peg10) showed reduced paternal bias, whereas maternally expressed genes (e.g., Mirg, Rian, Meg3, Cdkn1c, Grb10, and H19) showed increased paternal expression, consistent with convergence toward equal expression from both alleles (Fig. 2f,g). These shifts in allelic expression are consistent with reactivation of the normally silent allele.

  We aimed to validate these observations in a cell system that more strongly preserves allele-specific methylation at DMRs. However, the effects of DNMTi appeared to be cell-type specific. In primary neural precursor cells (NPCs), treatment only partially reduced DNA methylation (from ~90% to ~45% on the normally methylated allele) and did not significantly alter allelic expression bias (data not shown). Although DNMTi treatment was more toxic in NPCs, the limited demethylation and preserved imprinted expression likely reflect slower cell division rates and/or additional mechanisms that maintain imprinting in more differentiated cells.

  To identify additional epigenetic features associated with the allele-specific chromatin organization, we next analyzed histone modification profiles (Fig. 2g). Active histone marks, including H3K27ac, H3K4me3, and H3K36me3, were preferentially enriched at the unmethylated allele at gDMRs, whereas the repressive histone mark H3K9me3 was associated with the methylated allele. H3K27me3 was also enriched at the unmethylated allele at gDMRs, consistent with its known mutual exclusivity with DNA methylation35. These patterns indicate that allele-specific chromatin architecture is accompanied by distinct local chromatin states at DMRs, consistent with previous observations36,37. Together with the observed CTCF-dependent insulation, these features support an established model in which DNA methylation and chromatin state jointly shape allele-specific regulatory environments, which may influence interactions with distal regulatory elements.


### Neural-specific chromatin organization at the Meg3 locus
  During our analysis of chromatin architecture in the cortex, we identified a region in which allele-specific chromatin organization was not directly associated with CTCF binding. At the Meg3-Dio3 imprinted domain, a 210 kb region spanning the Meg3 lncRNA gene body is largely devoid of long-range chromatin contacts on the maternal allele (Fig. 3a, grey highlight).   To investigate the epigenetic features underlying this organization, we examined histone modifications and DNA methylation across the locus. Although several imprinted lncRNAs, such as Airn and Kcnq1ot1, lead to deposition of H3K27me338, the Meg3 gene body did not show enrichment of this mark. Instead, we observed maternal-specific enrichment of H3K36me3 together with an extended, heterogenous DMR characterized by high DNA methylation on the inactive paternal allele and partial methylation on the active maternal allele (Fig. 3a). Although MethylC data cannot distinguish 5-methylcytosine from 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, the observed allele-specific cytosine modification pattern is nonetheless consistent with transcription-associated chromatin features across the Meg3 gene body.

  A similar maternal-specific depletion of long-range contacts was observed in primary cortical neurons (Fig. 3b), whereas a more defined TAD-like organization was present in mESCs (Fig. 3c) and mESC-derived cardiomyocytes15,39. These findings suggest that allele-specific chromatin organization can also arise from transcription-associated mechanisms, in addition to classical CTCF-mediated insulation.


### Active and inactive alleles engage distinct regulatory landscapes
  Having established that imprinted domains exhibit distinct allele-specific chromatin architectures, we next investigated how these structural differences relate to imprinted gene expression. Specifically, we asked whether imprinted genes show allelic differences in their interactions with distal regulatory elements, focusing on both the frequency of transcription start site (TSS)-anchored contacts and the chromatin states of distal interacting regions (schematic illustrated in Fig. 4a).Fig. 4Imprinted gene promoters exhibit allelic differences in distal chromatin contacts.a Schematic illustration of the analytic framework used to assess allele-specific chromatin interactions at imprinted genes and non-imprinted genes.   To quantify allele-specific interactions, we measured the frequency of distal chromatin contacts anchored at the TSS of imprinted genes using the cortex Capture Hi-C data (Fig. 4b–e). Active and inactive alleles were defined based on allele-specific RNA-seq from hybrid primary cortical neurons (≥10 normalized reads across samples; Supplementary Fig. 12a). As a control, we also examined the frequency of distal contacts with all non-imprinted genes (i.e., biallelically expressed genes) within the capture regions.

  Non-imprinted genes exhibited similar TSS-anchored contact frequencies between parental alleles. In contrast, imprinted genes showed a marked imbalance, with the active allele engaging in more distal contacts than the inactive allele (Fig. 4b). This difference was most pronounced for genes whose TSS overlapped gDMRs or sDMRs (Fig. 4c), whereas imprinted genes lacking promoter-associated DMRs (non-DMR imprinted genes) showed similar contact frequencies between alleles. Similar patterns were observed in primary cortical neurons (Supplementary Fig. 12b,c).

  To further characterize the regulatory potential of allele-specific chromatin interactions, we asked whether distal regions contacted by imprinted gene promoters differ in their chromatin states between alleles, particularly for non-DMR imprinted genes that show similar contact frequencies. To address this, we classified chromatin states across imprinted domains using ChromHMM40, integrating publicly available ChIP-seq data with our allelic CUT&Tag datasets from neuronal cells and tissue. The input features included allele-specific CTCF, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K27ac and H3K4me3 profiles, as well as non-allelic H3K4me1 data. Chromatin states were defined based on combinations of active and repressive histone marks and grouped into functional categories, including active enhancers, transcribed regions, repressive chromatin, and quiescent states (Fig. 4d).

  We then quantified the frequency of TSS-anchored contacts with ChromHMM-defined chromatin states. Non-imprinted genes exhibited minimal allelic differences in contacts with regions annotated as active enhancers or repressive chromatin. In contrast, for non-DMR imprinted genes, the active allele preferentially contacted regions annotated as active enhancers compared to the inactive allele, in both cortex and primary cortical neurons (Fig. 4e, and Supplementary Fig. 12d). Together, these results indicate that allele-specific gene expression is associated with increased interaction frequency (or hubness) and, in some cases, preferential engagement with active regulatory elements.


### CRISPRi screening reveals a distal enhancer for the Mest-Copg2 domain
  Our Capture Hi-C analysis provided chromatin interaction maps at 5 kb resolution; however, regulatory elements are often smaller than this scale. We therefore refined candidate cis-regulatory regions using chromatin accessibility profiling. ATAC-seq on hybrid mouse cortex identified ~78,000 accessible regions genome-wide. Using allele-specific SNPs, we identified ~100 peaks with allele-specific accessibility, most of which overlapped known imprinted promoters or DMRs (Supplementary Fig. 13a). Only a small number of allele-specific peaks were detected outside these regions, suggesting that allele-specific accessibility of regulatory elements is relatively rare at this resolution.

  To identify functional cis-regulatory elements involved in imprinting, we performed a CRISPR interference (CRISPRi) screen using a nuclease-dead Cas9 (dCas9) fused to the KRAB repressor domain (Fig. 5a). To facilitate the assessment of imprinted expression in neurons, we established hybrid NPCs from dCas9-KRAB (BL6) x CAST F1 embryos, delivered guide RNAs (gRNAs) by lentiviral transduction, and differentiated the cells into neurons.   We focused on the Mest-Copg2 domain, which exhibits distinct patterns of allele-specific expression. Mest is exclusively expressed from the paternal allele across tissues. In contrast, Copg2 is biallelically expressed in most tissues but shows a maternal expression bias specifically in neurons, despite lacking a promoter-associated DMR, suggesting regulation beyond promoter-proximal methylation. We designed gRNAs targeting 25 accessible chromatin peaks within this domain (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Table 1). Each region was targeted individually in NPCs, and expression of Mest and Copg2 expression was measured following neuronal differentiation (Fig. 5c). Among these candidates, inhibition of a single element (E105) significantly reduced Mest expression, while total Copg2 levels were unchanged (Fig. 5c).

  Given the maternal bias of Copg2 expression in neurons, we next assessed allele-specific expression using RT-digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), which provides a quantitative readout of allelic expression (Supplementary Fig. 13c). E105 inhibition significantly reduced the maternal-to-paternal expression ratio of Copg2 (Fig. 5d), corresponding to a decrease in the maternal fraction from 67% to 57% (Supplementary Fig. 13d). Relative to a biallelic baseline of 50% maternal expression, this represents a loss of ~60% of the neuron-specific maternal bias. Notably, E105 is not the closest element to either promoter, indicating that its regulatory effects are not explained by linear proximity.

  The E105 region exhibits histone modifications characteristic of active enhancers, including H3K4me1 and H3K27ac, across mouse brain and other tissues (Fig. 5e, and Supplementary Fig. 13e). CRISPRi-mediated repression of E105 resulted in a significant, though incomplete, reduction of H3K27ac at the enhancer site in NPC-derived neurons (padj = 1.7 × 10−13; Fig. 5e), without affecting H3K27ac at the Mest or Copg2 promoters (padj > 0.95; Supplementary Fig. 13f–h). These results indicate that partial repression of E105 is sufficient to alter imprinted expression at this locus.

  Transcriptome-wide analysis confirmed that E105 functions as a selective regulatory element. Total RNA-seq showed that E105 inhibition specifically reduced Mest expression with minimal effects on other genes, including total Copg2 levels (Fig. 5f). However, allele-specific analysis revealed a reduction in the Copg2 maternal-to-paternal expression ratio, while monoallelic Mest expression remained unchanged (Fig. 5f).

  To validate the role of E105 using an independent approach, we deleted E105 using dual gRNAs and catalytically active Cas9 in hybrid NPCs (Supplementary Fig. 13i, j). Despite heterogeneous editing outcomes, including ~33% of alleles with E105 deletion (Supplementary Fig. 13i,j), NPC-derived neurons recapitulated reduced Mest expression and reduced Copg2 allelic bias by RT-qPCR and RT-ddPCR analyzes (Fig. 5g).

  We further assessed in vivo relevance using AAV-mediated delivery of E105-targeting gRNAs into adult dCas9-KRAB mouse brain via retro-orbital injection. Approximately 45% of nuclei in the brain were transduced (Supplementary Fig. 13k), and cortical RT-qPCR analysis revealed a ~20% reduction in Mest expression relative to controls (Supplementary Fig. 13l).

  To assess CRISPRi efficiency in the NPC differentiation system used for the screen, we performed H3K9me3 CUT&Tag as a readout of dCas9-KRAB activity following gRNA transduction. Targeting of E104 and E105 successfully induced H3K9me3 at their respective loci, whereas E111 did not (Supplementary Fig. 13m), indicating variable gRNA efficiency. Thus, we cannot exclude the possibility that additional regulatory elements contribute to Mest-Copg2 regulation. Together, these results identify E105 as a distal enhancer that selectively regulates Mest expression and modulates allelic bias at Copg2 within this imprinted domain.


### Copg2 is regulated by both a distal enhancer and MestXL transcription

Complementary virtual 4C profiles anchored at the Mest and Copg2 TSSs confirmed these allele-specific contact patterns (Supplementary Fig. 14a).   In addition to chromatin architecture, antisense transcription has been proposed to regulate Copg2 imprinting. MestXL, a 3’ UTR-extended isoform of Mest, is transcribed from the paternal allele antisense to Copg2 in neural tissue41 (Fig. 6c). To assess its potential regulatory role, we assessed subcellular localization of MestXL in NPC-derived neurons. In nuclear fractionation experiments, MestXL was strongly enriched in the nuclear fraction, whereas total Mest (representing both Mest and MestXL isoforms) showed only modest nuclear enrichment (Fig. 6d, and Supplementary Fig. 14c). Fractionation purity was confirmed by enrichment of nuclear lncRNAs (Neat1, Malat1) and depletion of cytosolic housekeeping genes (Sdha, Psmd4). Consistent with these results, fluorescence in situ hybridization using probes targeting the MestXL extension together with a Copg2 intron probe showed that MestXL localizes at the Copg2 transcription site (Supplementary Fig. 14d). These results indicate that MestXL is a nuclear-enriched transcript consistent with a potential role in allele-specific regulation.

  To distinguish the contributions of enhancer-mediated activation and MestXL-mediated repression to Copg2 imprinting, we first asked whether the reduced maternal-to-paternal ratio following E105 inhibition reflected decreased maternal expression, increased paternal expression, or both. Absolute allele-specific quantification by RT-ddPCR showed that E105 inhibition reduced maternal Copg2 transcripts by 18% and increased paternal Copg2 transcripts by 40% (Supplementary Fig. 14e). This effect was independently confirmed by allele-specific RNA-seq, which showed a 9% decrease in maternal Copg2 expression and a 35% increase in paternal Copg2 expression (Supplementary Fig. 14f). Together, these results indicate that E105 promotes maternal Copg2 expression while also indirectly influencing paternal expression.

  To directly test the role of MestXL, we selectively inhibited its transcription using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), which interfere with nascent transcription by inducing premature termination42,43. An ASO targeting the MestXL extended 3′ UTR strongly reduced MestXL (>93%) and partially reduced total Mest RNA (~50%), consistent with targeting of the extended Mest transcript (Fig. 6e). However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that ASO treatment indirectly affects transcription or stability of other Mest isoforms; therefore, we refer to this perturbation as Mest/MestXL knockdown. In the absence of enhancer inhibition, Mest/MestXL knockdown increased paternal Copg2 expression by 66% without detectably altering maternal Copg2 expression (Fig. 6f; CRISPRi Scrb), consistent with a role for paternal Mest/MestXL in repressing paternal Copg2.

  When E105 and Mest/MestXL were simultaneously inhibited by CRISPRi and ASO treatment, respectively, paternal Copg2 expression remained elevated relative to the control condition, while maternal Copg2 expression was reduced (Fig. 6f; ASO-Ctrl + CRISPRi-Scrb vs. ASO-XL + CRISPRi-E105). Using NPC-derived neurons from the reciprocal cross (CAST x dCas9-KRAB (BL6)), we confirmed that this regulatory mechanism acts in a consistent, parent-of-original manner (Supplementary Fig. 14g). In contrast, E105 inhibition had minimal effects on Mest or Copg2 expression in undifferentiated NPCs (Supplementary Fig. 14h). These results support a model in which E105 promotes maternal Copg2 expression, whereas Mest/MestXL contributes to repression of paternal Copg2.

  Together, these findings define two complementary regulatory mechanisms at the Mest-Copg2 domain. E105 acts as a long-range enhancer that promotes maternal Copg2 expression through allele-specific chromatin interactions, while on the paternal allele it engages the Mest/MestXL promoter to drive transcription. Mest/MestXL then contributes to repression of paternal Copg2, likely through transcriptional interference, although a direct repressive role of the RNA cannot be excluded. Thus, maternal activation by E105 and paternal repression by Mest/MestXL operate on opposite alleles and together shape neuron-specific Copg2 allelic bias (Fig. 6g).


## Discussion
  Our study reveals that allele-specific chromatin architecture is a common feature of imprinted domains in the mouse brain and is closely linked to the regulation of imprinted gene expression. Consistent with established models, we find that DNA methylation-sensitive CTCF binding at ICRs is associated with allele-specific chromatin insulation. Loss of DNA methylation leads to biallelic CTCF binding, increased insulation at normally methylated alleles, and disruption of allele-specific chromatin organization, accompanied by reduced allelic expression bias. These findings provide systematic support for a model in which DNA methylation restricts CTCF binding and chromatin insulation to a single parental allele across imprinted domains.

  Beyond insulation at ICRs, our results indicate that allele-specific chromatin organization also modulates distal regulatory interactions. Active alleles of imprinted genes exhibit increased interaction frequency with distal regions, consistent with promoter hubness, whereas inactive alleles show reduced connectivity. For genes lacking promoter-associated DMRs, differences in contact frequency are less pronounced; instead, allele-specific expression is associated with preferential engagement with active enhancer states. Together, these findings suggest that allele-specific gene regulation can arise from both quantitative differences in chromatin contacts and qualitative differences in the regulatory potential of interacting regions.

  Through CRISPRi screening, we identify a distal enhancer (E105) within the Mest–Copg2 domain that selectively regulates Mest expression and modulates allelic bias of Copg2. Chromatin interaction analyzes show that E105 engages different promoters on the two parental alleles, contacting Mest on the paternal allele and Copg2 on the maternal allele, thereby enabling allele-specific enhancer-promoter communication.

  The architectural logic is similar to the well-established paradigm at the H19–Igf2 locus, where a methylation-sensitive ICR establishes a boundary that governs allele-specific enhancer access. However, our data also reveal a key distinction: whereas the H19–Igf2 ICR primarily functions as an insulator that blocks enhancer access on one allele, the Mest–Copg2 domain exhibits a more complex regulatory architecture. Here, the ICR not only contributes to insulation but also participates in allele-specific chromatin interactions that direct enhancer engagement with different target genes. This suggests that ICRs can play multiple roles in organizing allele-specific regulatory interactions beyond simple enhancer blocking.

  Our results further integrate enhancer activity with antisense transcription in controlling imprinted gene expression. We show that E105 promotes maternal Copg2 expression, whereas transcription of the paternal antisense transcript MestXL represses Copg2, likely through transcription interference. These two mechanisms act on opposite parental alleles and together shape the allelic expression pattern of Copg2. This dual regulatory logic provides a mechanistic framework for understanding how imprinted genes lacking promoter-associated DMRs can achieve allele-specific expression through coordinated activation and repression.

  Around the time of this work, a preprint reported that Copg2 maternal expression bias is established independently of MestXL and instead reflects stage-specific regulation during neural differentiation, with MestXL required for maintenance rather than initiation44. That study further proposed the involvement of an upstream enhancer acting in an allele-specific manner to promote maternal Copg2 expression. Our findings are consistent with this framework and extend it by providing direct functional and structural evidence for such a mechanism. Specifically, we identify a distal enhancer (E105) and demonstrate that it regulates Mest and Copg2 through allele-specific chromatin interactions, thereby defining how enhancer activity is partitioned between parental alleles and integrated with antisense transcription.

  In addition to CTCF-mediated insulation, we identify transcription-associated chromatin features as an alternative mechanism contributing to allele-specific chromatin organization. At the Meg3 locus, allele-specific chromatin organization is associated with transcription-linked chromatin signatures rather than canonical CTCF binding at the ICR, indicating that multiple mechanisms can generate allele-specific chromatin architectures across imprinted domains.

  Several limitations in our study should be considered. DNMTi treatment induces genome-wide DNA demethylation, making it difficult to distinguish direct effects at ICRs from indirect consequences of broader epigenetic changes. In addition, incomplete demethylation in certain cell types and variable efficiency of CRISPRi targeting may limit the identification of additional regulatory elements. Future studies using locus-specific perturbations and higher-resolution approaches will be important to further define the causal relationships between DNA methylation, chromatin architecture, and gene regulation.

  Together, our findings support a model in which allele-specific chromatin architecture integrates DNA methylation, chromatin insulation, enhancer activity, and antisense transcription to regulate imprinted gene expression. More broadly, these results highlight how multiple layers of chromatin organization cooperate to generate parent-of-origin gene regulation in the mammalian genome.

Mice (Mus musculus domesticus and castaneus) were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory, including C57BL/6J (#000664), dCas9-KRAB on a C57BL/6J background (#030000), Rosa26-Cas9 on a C57BL/6 background (#028555), and CAST/EiJ (#000928). ### Primary cell culture
  For NPC culture, dorsal cortices from E14 embryos (pooled irrespective of sex) were dissected into HEPES-buffered saline solution (Lonza #CC-5024). Tissues were dissociated with 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific #15090046; diluted in HEPES-buffered saline supplemented with 1 mM EDTA) at 37 °C for 5 min, assisted by mechanical trituration by pipetting in the presence of trypsin inhibitor (thermo Fisher Scientific #R007100). Cell suspensions were filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and plated onto sterile bacterial Petri dishes in NPC medium consisting of DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #11320033), 2% B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #17504044), 20 ng/mL basic FGF (PeproTech #450-33), and 20 ng/mL EGF (PeproTech #315-09). Cultured were maintained for 4–5 days to allow neurosphere formation, with daily medium changes. Neurospheres were dissociated with 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA at 37 °C for 5 min, with mechanical trituration in the presence of trypsin inhibitor. Cells were plated onto Geltrex-coated tissue culture dishes (Thermo Fisher Scientific #A1413202) and passages daily for 3–4 days. NPCs were then cryopreserved until use. For routine adherent NPC culture, cells were passaged on Geltrex-coated dishes in NPC medium and used within 9 passages.

  For NPC differentiation into excitatory neurons, NPCs were plated onto Poly-D-lysine-coated plates (PDL; Sigma Aldrich #P7886) at a density of 100,000–150,000 cells/mL and maintained in cortical neuron maintenance medium consisting of Neurobasal (Thermo Fisher Scientific #21103049) supplemented with 2% B27 (Thermo Fisher Scientific #17504044) and 1X glutamine (Corning #25-055-Cl) for 3–5 days.

  For primary cortical neuron culture, cortices from E16 embryos were dissected into plating medium consisting of Neurobasal, 2% B27, 2% FBS (Avantor #97068-091), and 1X glutamine. Tissues were mechanically dissociated by pipetting, filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer, and plated onto PDL-coated plates at a density of 200,000 cells/mL in plating medium. The next day, medium was replaced with cortical neuron maintenance medium, and cultures were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 10 days.


### mESC culture
  The mESC line used in this study was derived from an F1 129/Sv x CAST cross (F1.2-3)45. For routine maintenance, mESCs were grown on 0.2% gelatin-coated plates in ESC medium consisting of DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific #11995073), 10 mM HEPES (Thermo Fisher Scientific #15630080), 0.11 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma Aldrich #M6250), 1X non-essential amino acids (Corning #25-025-Cl), 2 mM L-glutamine (Corning #25-055-Cl), 15% FBS (Avantor #97068-091), and 1000 μ/mL LIF (ESGRO #ESG1107). For routine passaging, cells were washed with HEPES-buffered saline, dissociated with 0.25% Trypsin/EDTA, and replated. For drug treatment, 5 μM GSK-3484862 (MedChemExpress #HY-135146) was added for 4 days, with volume-matched DMSO used as the control. Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2.


### Capture Hi-C library preparation
  Dissected mouse cortices from reciprocal F1 hybrids (two BL6 x CAST and two CAST x BL6; 7–8 weeks old) were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Frozen tissues were pulverized in liquid nitrogen using a Cellcrusher device and resuspended in 5 mL PBS by gentle inversion. Samples were crosslinked in 2% formaldehyde by adding 500 μL TC buffer (50 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, and 22% formaldehyde; Sigma-Aldrich #252549) and incubated at room temperature for 20 min.

  For adherent cells (mESCs, n = 3 per condition; primary cortical neurons from BL6 x CAST, n = 4), cells were washed twice with PBS, scraped into 5 mL PBS, and directly crosslinked to a final concentration of 2% formaldehyde.

  Crosslinking was quenched by addition of 289 μL stop solution I for 5 min at room temperature. Samples were centrifuged at 2000 × g for 15 min at room temperature, and pellets were stored at −80 °C until use.

  Capture Hi-C libraries were prepared using the Arima Hi-C kit (Arima Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell pellets were resuspended in 20 μL lysis buffer and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. Chromatin was conditioned using SDS solution at 62 °C for 10 min and quenched with 20 μL stop solution II at 37 °C for 15 min. Digestion was performed with Arima restriction enzymes by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min, 65 °C for 20 min, and 25 °C for 10 min. Biotin end repair was carried out at room temperature for 45 min, followed by proximity ligation at room temperature for 15 min. Crosslinks were reversed with proteinase K by incubation at 55 °C for 30 min, 68 °C overnight, and 25 °C for 10 min. Proximity-ligated DNA was purified using AMPure XP beads.

  Proximity-ligated DNA was fragmented in 100 μL elution buffer using a Covaris S220 (140 W, 10% duty factor, 200 cycles per burst, 55 s) to a target fragment size of ~400 bp. Double-sided size selection was performed using 0.6X and 0.25X AMPure XP beads. Biotin enrichment was performed using 300-400 ng size-selected DNA and 12.5 μL T1 Streptavidin beads in 200 μL binding buffer. Washes were performed according to the Arima protocol, in 200 μL wash buffer at 55 °C for 2 min each and then 100 μL elution buffer. Washed beads were resuspended in 50 μL of water. End repair was then performed on bead-bound DNA by incubating in end repair mix at 20 °C for 15 min, 72 °C for 15 min, followed by adaptor ligation at 20 °C for 30 min. Samples were cleaned up by magnetizing beads and washing in 200 μL wash buffer at 55 °C for 2 min and in 100 μL elution buffer. Washed beads were resuspended in 34 μL water and immediately used for library amplification using Herculase II Fusion polymerase (98 °C for 2 min followed by 10 cycles of 98 °C for 30 sec, 60 °C for 30 sec, 72 °C for 1 min, and then 72 °C for 5 min). Different index primer combinations were used for each sample. The amplified library was purified using 1X AMPure XP beads.

  For target capture, 1.5 μg amplified Hi-C library was pre-cleared of residual biotin using 50 μL T1 Streptavidin beads in a 230 μL reaction. Approximately 1 μg of pre-cleared Hi-C library was resuspended in 14 μL water and was hybridized to custom capture probes designed to mm10 genome (Agilent Technologies; Supplementary Figs. 2–9 coordinates of capture probes track were lifted over to mm39) in a thermocycler (95 °C for 5 min, 65 °C for 10 min followed by 60 cycles of 65 °C for 1 min, 37 °C for 3 s) and purified using 50 μL T1 Streptavidin beads. After stringent washes (70 °C for 5 min, 6 times), captured libraries were amplified using Herculase II polymerase (98 °C for 2 min followed by 13 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, 72 °C for 1 min, and then 72 °C for 5 min). Final library purification was performed using 1X AMPure XP beads, pooled equimolarly, and subjected to Illumina PE150 sequencing (Novogene).


### Capture Hi-C analysis
  The mouse genome (GRCm39) was prepared for allele-specific analysis using the SNPsplit_genome_preparation script to mask all the CAST_EiJ SNPs from the Mouse Genomes Project (mgp_REL2021_snps.vcf.gz)46,47. The SNP-masked genome was digested at Arima restriction enzyme sites using HiCUP_digester48. Capture Hi-C FASTQ files were mapped using HiCUP (v0.8.0), and allele phasing was performed with SNPsplit (v0.6.0) using “hicup.bam” files as input. Reads assigned to the same parental genome (“hicup.G1_G1.bam” or “hicup.G2_G2.bam”) were converted to pre-Juicer format using hicup2juicer (v0.9.0) with --usemid and --digest options.

  Using a custom script, read pairs were filtered to capture regions and required to have MAPQ > 30 at both ends. For each sample, maternal and paternal read counts were normalized by subsampling the deeper allele. To control for strain-of-origin effects, reads from reciprocal crosses were merged by parent-of-origin; specifically, G1_G1.medium files from BL6 × CAST samples were merged with G2_G2.medium files from CAST × BL6 samples, and vice versa. For comparisons across tissues or cell types, capture-region reads were further subsampled to equal depth. Medium files were converted to.hic format using Juicer pre tool (v1.22.01). Contact matrices were visualized in R (v4.4.1) using plotgardener (v1.10.2)49 or Juicebox (v1.11.08)50. Chromatin loops were identified with Juicer HICCUPS using VC_SQRT normalization7. Pile-up analysis was performed using Coolpuppy51.

  Virtual 4 C plots were generated by extracting one-dimensional contact profiles from Capture Hi-C data, using a 10 kb window centered on the genomic feature of interest (the anchor). Contacts between the anchor and all other loci were binned at 5 kb resolution and plotted across the region.

  Insulation scores were calculated across each imprinted domain at 2.5 kb resolution using a sliding window scaled to domain size (50–200 kb). At each bin boundary, the number of contacts spanning that position within the defined window was quantified. Regions with higher numbers of cross-boundary contacts were considered less insulated (higher insulation score), whereas regions with fewer cross-boundary contacts were considered more insulated (lower insulation score). Custom analysis scripts are available at github.com/Whipple-Lab/capture-hic-imprinting52.


### Chromatin state annotation and contact frequency analysis
  For chromatin contact frequency analysis, a 5 kb window centered on the TSS of each gene was defined as the anchor, and all Capture Hi-C contacts originating from this anchor were included in the analysis. To focus on distal interactions, contacts within ±50 kb of the anchor were excluded. Based on the imprinted expression pattern of each gene, TSS-anchored contacts were classified as originating from the active (predominantly expressed) or inactive allele. For non-imprinted genes, contacts from each parental allele were analyzed. Contact frequencies are reported as the fraction of total contacts across both alleles.

  For analysis of chromatin states associated with distal Capture Hi-C interactions, chromatin states were first defined using ChromHMM52 by integrating allele-specific and non-allelic epigenome datasets from neuronal cells and tissues. The input features included allele-specific CTCF, H3K36me3, H3K27me3, H3K27ac, and H3K4me3 profiles, together with non-allelic H3K4me1 data. Public datasets were obtained from GEO and ENCODE, including allele-specific H3K27me3 from primary cortical neurons (GSE118399), allele-specific H3K27ac and H3K4me3 from mouse frontal cortex (GSE30199), and non-allelic H3K4me1 from E15.5 mouse forebrain (ENCODE). Allele-specific CTCF and H3K36me3 datasets were generated in this study from primary cortical neurons.

  ChromHMM models were trained on binarized input tracks to segment the genome into twelve chromatin states (java – jar ChromHMM.jar LearnModel --numstates 12 --assembly mm39). Each distal Capture Hi-C interaction was assigned to a chromatin state based on the state at its distal interacting locus. For each allele category (non-imprinted paternal, non-imprinted maternal, imprinted active, and imprinted inactive), the average number of interactions per TSS for each chromatin state was calculated and normalized to the genomic coverage of that state. The twelve states were grouped into functional categories by manual annotation as active enhancers (strong H3K4me1 and/or H3K27ac outside RefSeq exons), transcribed regions (strong H3K27ac or H3K36me3 overlapping RefSeq exons), repressive chromatin (strong H3K27me3), and quiescent states (no detectable histone modification signal).


### CUT&Tag library preparation
  CUT&Tag libraries were prepared using CUT&Tag-IT kit (Active Motif). Cells were gently washed with PBS, scraped into 1 mL PBS, and 600,000 cells were used per sample (n = 2 per condition). Cells were pelleted at 300 × g for 4 min, resuspended in lysis buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.25% IGEPAL CA-630), and incubated on ice for 5 min with gentle trituration. Nuclei were collected by centrifugation at 800 × g for 4 min, washed, and processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

  Nuclei were bound to conditioned concanavalin A beads and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CTCF (Cell Signaling #2899), H3K36me3 (Active Motif #61902 Lot 23065105), H3K27ac (Active Motif #39136 Lot 28720002) or H3K9me3 (Active Motif #39062 Lot 24306101). After incubation with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temp, beads were resuspended in diluted CUT&Tag transposomes in CUT&Tag Dig-300 buffer and incubated on an orbital rotator for 1 h at room temperature. After transposome binding, beads were washed three times with Dig-300 buffer. Tagmentation was induced by incubating beads in 125 μL of CUT&Tag tagmentation buffer for 1 h at 37 °C. DNA was purified, PCR-amplified with indexed primers, purified using SPRI beads, pooled equimolarly, and subjected to Illumina PE150 sequencing (Novogene).


### CUT&Tag analysis
  Adapters sequences were trimmed using TrimGalore (v0.6.10). Reads were mapped to the CAST SNP-masked GRCm39 genome using Bowtie2 (--local -X 2000; v2.5.1). PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates, and allele-specific reads were parsed using SNPsplit. Peaks were called on unsplit BAM files using MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) or SICER2, depending on the dataset. Peak coordinates from all samples were merged and converted to SAF format for read counting. Reads from the SNPsplit BAM files were counted with FeatureCounts (Subread v2.0.6) using -p –countReadPairs. Allelic enrichment was assessed in R using DEseq2. For visualization, BAM files were converted to CPM-normalized bigWig tracks using deepTools (v3.5.2). Differential analyzes were performed in R using DEseq2 (v1.40.2).


### ATAC-seq library preparation
  ATAC-seq libraries were prepared using the Active Motif ATAC-seq kit. Frozen mouse cortices (three BL6 x CAST and three CAST x BL6; 7–8 weeks old) were minced on ice and transferred to cold PBS. Tissue was pelleted at 500 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, resuspended in 1 mL ATAC lysis buffer, and homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer. Samples were filtered through 40 μm strainer, and nuclei were counted by trypan blue staining. A total of 100,000 nuclei were used per sample. Following pelleting, nuclei were subjected to tagmentation at 37 °C for 30 min with shaking at 800 rpm. DNA was purified, PCR-amplified with indexed primers, purified using SPRI beads, pooled equimolarly, and subjected to Illumina PE150 sequencing (Novogene).


### ATAC-seq analysis
  Adapters sequences were trimmed using TrimGalore. Reads were aligned to the CAST SNP-masked GRCm39 genome using Bowtie2 (--very-sensitive -X 2000). PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates, and allele-specific reads were parsed using SNPsplit. Peaks were called on unsplit BAM files using MACS2. Peaks coordinates from all samples were merged and converted to SAF format for read counting. Reads from the SNPsplit BAM files were counted with FeatureCounts using -p –countReadPairs. Parental bias in accessibility was assessed in R using DEseq2.


### RNA-seq analysis
  Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific #15596018) and treated with TURBO DNA-free kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #AM1907). Library preparation and sequencing were performed by Novogene. RNA-seq from mESCs treated with DMSO or GSK-3484862 (n = 3 per condition) used poly(A)-selected libraries, whereas RNA-seq from primary cortical neurons (n = 3 per reciprocal cross) and NPC-derived neurons following CRISPRi (n = 3 per condition) used rRNA-depleted libraries.

  Adapters sequences were trimmed using TrimGalore. Reads were aligned to the CAST SNP-masked GRCm39 genome using STAR (--sjdbGTFfile Mus_musculus.GRCm39.110.gtf --sjdbOverhang 149 --alignEndsType EndToEnd; v2.7.0e). PCR duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates, and allele-specific reads were parsed using SNPsplit. Read counts were generated on SNPsplit BAM files using FeatureCounts with exon-level annotation (-p --countReadPairs -t exon -g gene_id -a Mus_musculus.GRCm39.110.gtf). Allelic bias and differential expression were assessed in R using DEseq2.


### gRNA cloning
  Two to four gRNAs (Supplementary Table 1) were designed for selected ATAC-seq peaks using CRISPick (Broad Institute). To maximize target coverage, gRNAs were chosen with minimal overlap. gRNAs were cloned into a 4X gRNA system53 with some adaptations. Specifically, (i) the scaffold sequence in each gRNA plasmid (ph7SK, phH1, phU6, and pmU6) was replaced with the scaffold from the CROP-seq-opti plasmid (Addgene #106280) to increase dCas9-KRAB residence time54, and (ii) gRNA flanking overhangs were modified to CACC to increase interchangeability of gRNAs. Individual gRNA cassettes were cloned by digestion and ligation, amplified using Q5 High-Fidelity polymerase (New England Biolabs #M0492), and assembled into a single lentiviral transfer plasmid using HiFi assembly (New England Biolabs #E2621). The lentiviral vector pLV-GG-hUbC-dsRed (Addgene #84034) was modified to replace the fluorescent marker with a blasticidin resistance gene. Final constructs were verified by colony PCR and whole-plasmid or Sanger sequencing.


### Lentivirus production
  293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) were maintained at 50-80% confluency in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. For virus production, cells were plated in 6-well plates at ~80% confluency and transfected after 3–4 h with 500 ng pMD2.G (Addgene #12259), 1000 ng psPAX2 (Addgene #12260), and 1500 ng transfer plasmid using PEI (Polysciences #23966) in OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific #31985062). The next day, medium was replaced with cortical neuron culture medium. Viral supernatant was collected 48 h later, clarified by centrifugation at 800 × g for 4 min, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. Lentiviral titers were determined using Lenti-X qRT-PCR Titration Kit (Takara Bio # 631235).


### Lentivirus transduction for CRISPRi and CRISPR/Cas9 approaches
  NPCs (generated from dCas9-KRAB(BL6) × CAST for CRISPRi and Rosa26-Cas9(BL6) × CAST for CRISPR-mediated enhancer deletion) were plated on Geltrex-coated plates at 50,000 cells/mL and allowed to adhere for 3–4 h. Lentiviral transduction was performed at 6500–7500 genomic particles per cell, a range determined by functional titration in NPCs that yielded ~100% transduction. The next day, NPC medium was replenished. After 48 h of transduction, cells were replated onto fresh Geltrex-coated plates in blasticidin-supplemented medium. The next day, cells were maintained on Geltrex-coated plates or differentiated into neurons as needed. Samples were collected into DNA/RNA Shield (Zymo Research #R1100) and RNA was extracted using TRIzol method.


### AAV injection
  AAV vectors were generated using pAAV-U6-sgRNA-CMV-GFP (Addgene #85451) as the backbone. The CMV promoter was replaced with the UbC promoter, and three SV40 nuclear localization sequences were added to the N-terminus of GFP. For the control vector, a scrambled gRNA was cloned downstream of the hU6 promoter. For the E105-targeting vector, two gRNAs flanking E105 were cloned downstream of the hU6 and hH1 promoters (Supplementary Table 1, E105*). AAV.PHP.eB particles were packaged by the Boston Children’s Hospital Viral Core. 11–15 weeks-old adult dCas9-KRAB mice were injected retro-orbitally with 5 × 1011 viral genomes per animal (n = 5 mice). Mice were euthanized 3−3.5 weeks later by isoflurane overdose and processed for downstream analyzes. Both sexes were used indistinguishably (4 females and 1 male per group).


### Fluorescence in situ hybridization
  RNA FISH was performed using the HCR v3.0 kit (Molecular Instruments) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, NPC-derived neurons grown on coverslips were fixed in ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences #15710) in PBS for 5 min, washed with PBS, and dehydrated sequentially in 50%, 70%, and 100% ethanol. Fixed samples were stored at −20 °C until use. Before hybridization, samples were rehydrated in PBS and pre-hybridized for 10 min in probe hybridization buffer at 37 °C. Custom probes targeting MestXL (chr6:30749023-30753868) and Copg2 intron 3 (chr6:30867643-30871718) were prepared at 16 nM in hybridization buffer and incubated with samples overnight at 37 °C in a humidified chamber.

  The next day, samples were washed sequentially in 100%, 75%, 50%, and 25% wash buffer in 5X SSCT for 15 min each, followed by a final wash in 100% 5X SSCT for 15 min. Samples were then equilibrated in amplification buffer for 30 min at room temperature. Hairpin mixtures were prepared by snap-cooling in amplification buffer and incubated with samples for 4.5 h at room temperature in a dark humidified chamber. After amplification, excess amplifiers were removed by two 30 min washes in 5X SSCT followed by a 5 min wash. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:2000 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific #62249). Coverslips were mounted using ProLong Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific #P36984). Images were taken using Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope with a 40X objective.


### Brain cryosection imaging
  Following isoflurane overdose, mice were perfused with PBS and then 4% PFA. Whole brains were dissected, fixed overnight in 1% PFA at 4 °C, washed in PBS, and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C. Brains were then incubated in a 1:1 mixture of OCT (Fisher Healthcare #23730571) and 30% sucrose for 30 min to 6 h, embedded in OCT, and stored at –80 °C. Cryosections were cut at 12–14 μm onto SuperFrost Plus slides (Fisher #1255015S24). For AAV-derived GFP imaging, nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 and mounted with ProLong Glass Antifade Mountant. Images were taken using a Zeiss Axio Scan Z1 with a 10X objective. Total and GFP-positive nuclei were quantified using ImageJ (particle analysis tool). Briefly, Hoechst 33342 images were thresholded to identify nuclei, and touching nuclei were separated using the watershed function. Particle analysis was then performed to estimate the total number of nuclei per image. The same procedure was applied to corresponding GFP images, and the proportion of GFP-positive nuclei relative to the total number of nuclei was calculated.


### Antisense oligonucleotide treatment
  Affinity plus gapmer ASOs targeting the 3′ extended region unique to MestXL (+T*+A*+C*A*T*T*T*G*A*G*T*C*T*+C*+C*+T) or a scrambled control ASO (+T*+C*+C*C*T*G* A*A* G*G*T*T*C*+C*+T*+C) were synthesized and purified by IDT. ASO was directly added to NPC medium at a final concentration of 10–12.5 μM. The next day, cultures were supplemented with half the original medium volume. After 3 days of ASO treatment, NPCs were differentiated. The next day, the medium was fully replaced and supplemented with ASO. Samples were collected on day 3–5 of differentiation.


### Nuclear fractionation
  For nuclear-to-cytosolic fractionation, NPC-derived neurons were washed with PBS and lysed in 1 mL lysis buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.25% IGEPAL CA-630) for 5 min on ice with gentle trituration. Successful lysis was confirmed by trypan blue staining and inspection under a light microscope. Samples were centrifuged for at 800 × g for 4 min. The supernatant was collected for cytosolic RNA extraction. Nuclei were washed once in nuclei wash buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 1.5 mM MgCl2, and 0.1 mM EDTA), pelleted again, and used for nuclear RNA extraction.

  For nuclear-to-total fractionation, lysates were split into two aliquots. One aliquot was used directly for total RNA extraction. The other was washed in nuclei wash buffer, resuspended in PBS, stained with Hoechst 33342, and sorted on a Beckman Coulter MoFlo Astrios EQ Cell Sorter with a 70 μm nozzle. Debris was excluded using a forward- versus side scatter plot (FSC-H vs SSC-H), doublets were removed using a forward scatter area versus height plot (FSC-A vs FSC-H), nuclei were gated based on Hoechst (excitation at 405 nm; 448/59 bandpass) versus the open 488-513/26 channel, and events within the R4 were collected. Sorted nuclei were used for nuclear RNA extraction.


### SYBR RT-qPCR
  RNA was extracted using TRIzol and treated with TURBO DNA-free kit. RNA was diluted to 5 ng/μL, and 2 μL was used per reaction in one-step RT-qPCR with either iTaq Universal One-Step RT-qPCR kit (BioRad #1725151) or Luna Universal One-Step RT-qPCR kit (NEB #E3005). Relative expression was calculated using the ddCt method. Psmd4 was used as the normalization control. Primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST and sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 2.


### Allele-specific RT-digital droplet PCR
  RNA was extracted using TRIzol and treated with Turbo DNase. A total of 300-350 ng RNA was reverse transcribed using Maxima reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific #EP0742) and random hexamers (Thermo Fisher Scientific #N8080127). cDNA was diluted 1:5 in water, and 5 μL of diluted cDNA was used per ddPCR reaction. For Ubc expression quantification, an additional 1:10 dilution of cDNA was performed. Reactions contained 1X ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP) (BioRad #186-3023) and 1X TaqMan SNP genotyping probes for Copg2 (probe 1, VIC-TTGAATATGCG[T]GCCTCC; probe 2, FAM-AATATGCG[C]GCCTCC; forward primer, GCATTTGGAGAAGAGTGCTGTCT; and reverse primer CTTGGATTGATGGGCGTTTCG) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A separate reaction containing 1X Taqman probe for Ubc (TaqMan Gene Expression Assay Mm02525934_g1; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for normalization. Droplets were generated on a QX200 droplet generator (Bio-Rad). Droplets were carefully transferred to thermocycler and PCR was performed at 95 °C for 10 min followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, ramp to 60 °C (at a rate of 2 °C/s) for 1 min, and then 98 °C for 10 min. Droplet count was performed within 1 h of PCR on a QX200 droplet reader (Bio-Rad) with the absolute quantification program. Probe specificity was validated using mixtures of synthetic oligonucleotides containing each SNP at different proportions.


### gDNA extraction
  Cells were resuspended in 300 μL cell lysis buffer (0.1 M Tris pH 8, 0.2 M NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 0.1% SDS, and 10 μ/mL Proteinase K) and incubated at 50 °C for 1 h to overnight. Genomic DNA was precipitated with 600 μL 100% isopropanol at −20 °C for 2 h, pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min, washed with 70% ethanol, and resuspended in 50 μL water.


### Bisulfite treatment and nanopore methylation analysis
  Bisulfite conversion was performed using EpiTect Fast DNA Bisulfite kit (Qiagen #59824) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Bisulfite-converted DNA was eluted in 15 μL water. Target regions were amplified using EpiMark Hot Start Taq (NEB #M0490) with bisulfite conversion-aware primers (Supplementary Table 2). PCR products were gel-purified using NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel #740609) and sequenced by Plasmidsaurus using the Premium PCR sequencing service. FASTQ files were aligned to CAST SNP-masked and CT-converted GRCm39 genome using minimap2 (v2.24). Reads were phased by SNPs to assign parental origin, and CpG methylation percentages were calculated for each allele.


### Sanger sequencing
  PCR was performed using Q5 High-Fidelity 2X Master Mix (NEB M0492). Products were resolved on 1% agarose gels, purified using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up kit (Macherey-Nagel #740609), and sequenced by Eton Biosciences.


### Statistics and reproducibility
  Statistical analyzes, including Wilcoxon tests, Dunnett’s tests, Tukey’s HSD tests, and unpaired t-tests, were performed in R (v4.4.1). Significance was defined as p < 0.05 unless otherwise indicated. The number of biological replicates for each experiment is provided in the Fig. legends.
