# CTCF aligns single-cell TAD-like domain boundaries and stabilizes long-range active chromatin clusters


## Abstract
  CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF) is a key architectural protein in the three-dimensional (3D) genome, yet how its loss reshapes chromatin structure and transcription at single-cell resolution remains unclear. Using HiRES, which jointly profiles chromatin contacts and RNA from the same nucleus, we examined genome-wide effects of CTCF depletion. Topologically associating domain (TAD)–like domains (TLDs) across single cells remained largely unchanged in number and size after CTCF loss, but their boundaries became more variably positioned, and pseudobulk analyses revealed reduced interactions within A compartments. We also developed SALTAFinder to identify Spatially Aggregated Long-distance TLD Assemblies (SALTAs), clusters of TLDs occupying shared 3D space within single cells. A subset of SALTAs is enriched for highly expressed genes and super-enhancers and declines upon CTCF depletion. This structural reorganization coincided with a global reduction in per-cell RNA output, as indicated by HiRES and orthogonal measurements. Together, these findings suggest that CTCF contributes to the coordinated regulation of chromatin organization and transcriptional capacity and is associated with stabilization of long-range active chromatin clusters.


## INTRODUCTION
  The three-dimensional (3D) genome provides an essential regulatory framework that coordinates gene expression and chromatin topology. The genome is partitioned into A/B compartments, topologically associating domains (TADs), and chromatin loops, which constrain regulatory communication at multiple levels (1–3). A key architectural factor, CCCTC-binding factor (CTCF), anchors cohesin-mediated loops and demarcates TAD boundaries through sequence-specific binding and orientation-dependent blocking of loop extrusion (4, 5). By insulating adjacent chromatin domains, CTCF safeguards enhancer-promoter specificity and helps preserve cell type–specific transcriptional programs (6–10).

  Bulk Hi-C experiments have shown that depletion of CTCF in mammalian cells leads to marked architectural rearrangements, such as weakening or even loss of TAD boundaries, disappearance of CTCF-anchored loops, and increased inter-TAD contacts while leaving large-scale A/B compartmentalization largely intact (11–18). However, bulk assays obscure the extensive cell-to-cell heterogeneity of chromatin folding. Single-cell Hi-C and chromatin tracing imaging have revealed that individual nucleus harbor highly variable domain structures at the single-cell level, with boundaries that fluctuate in position and extent across cells. These structures share similarities in scale with TADs identified from bulk Hi-C and are therefore referred to as TAD-like domains (TLDs) (19–24). These findings indicate that bulk TADs represent an aggregate of heterogeneous single-cell configurations, with CTCF-enriched sites exhibiting the highest boundary probability. Yet, how CTCF depletion affects TLD boundary positioning and stability at the single-cell level remains unclear.

  CTCF is indispensable for mammalian development. Complete knockout of CTCF in mice causes early embryonic lethality, and tissue-specific deletions result in diverse developmental defects, underscoring its essential role in maintaining proper gene regulation in vivo (25). Nevertheless, in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), CTCF degradation affects only a limited number of genes and does not compromise pluripotency, although it markedly reduces differentiation potential (12, 15, 17).

  Together, these observations raise two key questions central to understanding CTCF’s architectural role. First, does CTCF loss eliminate domain boundaries, or does it instead alter where boundaries form across single cells? Second, can such structural disorganization influence a cell’s global transcriptional capacity, even if most individual genes change little in expression?

  To address these questions, we applied HiRES [Hi-C and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) measured simultaneously] (26), a paired single-cell approach that profiles chromatin contacts and RNA from the same nucleus, to control and CTCF-depleted mESCs. This joint measurement of genome architecture and transcription revealed that CTCF loss increases the variability of TLD boundary positions across single cells and weakens interactions within A compartments, coinciding with lower per-cell RNA abundance. By generating allele-resolved single-cell contact maps with full genomic coverage—and obtaining over a thousand such maps with sufficient contact depth—our dataset enabled us to quantify how boundary usage varies across individual cells relative to canonical TAD boundaries and to examine how single-cell TLD boundary frequencies relate to bulk TAD boundary strength. The high contact number per cell further allowed us to apply SALTAFinder, a framework we developed to identify spatially aggregated long-distance TLD assemblies (SALTAs), defined as clusters of distantly located TLDs that occupy shared 3D space within individual nuclei. Among these assemblies, a subset enriched for super-enhancers and highly expressed genes forms transcriptionally permissive clusters whose prevalence decreases after CTCF depletion. Together, our combined use of HiRES and SALTAFinder establishes a framework for characterizing alterations in single-cell boundary positioning and for evaluating changes in long-range active chromatin clusters, thereby providing the structural context for interpreting the associated reduction in global transcriptional capacity.


## RESULTS

### TLD boundary redistribution in single cells after CTCF loss
  To study how CTCF shapes 3D genome folding and transcription within the same nucleus, we performed HiRES (26) on control (untreated) CTCF-AID mESCs and auxin-treated CTCF-AID mESCs (hereafter CTCF-depleted) (Fig. 1A and fig. S1A). After stringent quality control (QC), we retained 668 high-quality single cells (344 CTCF-depleted and 324 control), with a median of 375,424 contacts and 10,536 RNA unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) per nucleus (Fig. 1, B to D; fig. S1, B to D; and table S1). UMAP embeddings based on either DNA contact profiles or RNA expression profiles clearly separated the two groups (Fig. 1, E and F), demonstrating coordinated structural and transcriptional changes. These paired single-cell datasets provided a high-resolution basis for quantifying how CTCF loss perturbs genome organization and transcription within the same nucleus.

  Aggregating contacts across single cells recapitulated the canonical effects of CTCF depletion, including enlarged TADs (fig. S1E and table S2), weakened insulation (fig. S1, F and G), and reduced loop strength (fig. S1H). Furthermore, using Higashi (27), we identified TLDs at the single-cell level at a 50-kb resolution in each individual nuclei. Haplotype phasing enabled allele-specific TLD calling (see Materials and Methods). The identified TLDs were further validated by visual inspection and aggregated domain analysis (fig. S2, A and B).

  In control cells, each nucleus contained an average of 6640 TLDs (mean size: 666 kb). TLD boundaries showed broad cell-to-cell dispersion but were most frequently located near CTCF/cohesin sites and around bulk TAD boundaries (Fig. 1G). To quantify how often any genomic locus becomes a TLD boundary, we calculated the probability that each 50-kb bin serves as a boundary across all alleles—hereafter referred to as the TLD boundary probability (PTLD-B) (fig. S3A). Notably, even bins corresponding to bulk TAD boundaries—appearing sharply demarcated in pseudobulk maps—showed only modest boundary frequencies at the single-cell level, averaging ∼13%. By comparison, loci located >200 kb away from any bulk boundary still exhibited PTLD-B values around ∼6% (fig. S3B). Thus, bulk TAD boundaries do not reflect universally used boundaries but rather represent local probability maxima within a broad distribution of possible single-cell boundary positions. Consistently, PTLD-B correlated strongly and negatively with insulation scores (Fig. 1H and fig. S3C), indicating that bulk TAD boundaries correspond to genomic regions with the highest relative, not absolute, likelihood of acting as boundaries across cells.

  Following CTCF depletion, pseudobulk TAD maps exhibited an apparent increase in TAD size (Fig. 1G and fig. S1E), yet single-cell TLD characteristics, including domain number (6681 per cell) and size (661 kb), remained unchanged (Fig. 1, I and J). Consistent results were observed after controlling for contact number per cell across control and CTCF-depleted cells, with TLD number and size remaining largely unchanged (fig. S4A). Insulation at TLD boundaries was similarly preserved (fig. S4, B to D), in contrast to the weakened insulation at population-averaged TAD boundaries (fig. S1G). Instead of eliminating boundaries, CTCF loss primarily altered where boundaries form: PTLD-B decreased sharply at former TAD boundary centers (Fig. 1K, gray), and these decreases mirrored reductions in bulk insulation strength (fig. S4, E and F). Conversely, PTLD-B increased in nearby regions within ∼±100 to 400 kb (Fig. 1K, yellow). This overall shift in boundary positioning was also reproduced using an alternative TLD-calling method (DeDoc2) (28) (fig. S4, G to I). In total, 1947 bins showed significantly reduced PTLD-B and 1833 showed increases (fig. S4J and table S3). Their spatial distribution revealed that decreases were concentrated at former TAD boundaries, whereas increases occurred in nearby flanking regions (fig. S4K), indicating a local shift in boundary usage rather than a loss of TLD boundaries.

  Together, these results clarify the quantitative relationship between bulk TAD boundaries and single-cell TLD organization. As illustrated in Fig. 1L (left), bulk boundaries correspond to genomic regions with the highest local likelihood of serving as TLD boundaries across cells—information that becomes accessible only with sufficiently large single-cell datasets. CTCF depletion reduces this positional preference and increases boundary usage in nearby regions, producing a broader and flatter positional distribution (Fig. 1L, right). Consequently, bulk TAD boundaries blur although domains persist in individual nuclei. Thus, CTCF preserves domain integrity primarily by constraining boundary positions across cells.


### Reduced A compartment interactions and per-cell transcription after CTCF loss
  We next examined how CTCF loss affects A/B compartmentalization. Using Higashi (27), we computed 100-kb compartment scores for each nucleus. Single-cell compartment scores broadly matched pseudobulk profiles and aligned with local transcriptional activity across genomic regions (Fig. 2A). Genome-wide comparisons confirmed that compartment identity remained largely stable (∼78%) between control and CTCF-depleted cells, with 2600 and 2673 bins exhibiting significantly decreased or increased scores, respectively (Fig. 2B). Pseudobulk analysis similarly identified only a limited number of bins with significant score changes (1036; fig. S5A). Bins identified as significantly changed in the single-cell analysis showed strong concordance with those detected in the pseudobulk comparison, as highlighted in Fig. 2B. To interpret these single-cell score shifts in a transcriptional context, we integrated the 2600/2673 bins with differential gene expression profiles (fig. S5B). Bins with reduced compartment scores were enriched for down-regulated genes (Fig. 2C), consistent with the fact that most of these loci (∼63.8%) reside in A compartment regions. In contrast, bins with increased scores did not consistently show gene up-regulation (Fig. 2D), likely because over half (56.8%) map to B compartment regions, where modest score increases are insufficient to activate transcription.

  Although CTCF depletion had only a limited effect on linear A/B compartment assignments, it noticeably altered compartment interaction patterns. Pseudobulk observed/expected (O/E) matrices showed a pronounced reduction in intra-A compartment contact frequencies in CTCF-depleted cells, accompanied by a modest increase in intra-B contacts (Fig. 2E and fig. S6A). Consistent with these observations, published bulk Hi-C datasets from CTCF-AID depletion experiments (12, 15, 17) also showed reduced intra-A compartment interactions, whereas changes in intra-B contacts were not consistently observed across datasets (fig. S6, B to D). Together, these results, along with the changes in compartment scores described above, indicate that CTCF depletion is associated with alterations in both compartment strength and internal interaction patterns. The combined reduction in A compartment scores and intra-A interaction strength suggests a relaxation of A compartments and a modest compaction of B compartments, which may be associated with a more dispersed organization of active chromatin.

  These structural changes prompted us to test whether CTCF depletion reduces the transcriptional capacity of individual cells. Supporting this possibility, HiRES RNA measurements showed reductions in both the number of expressed genes and total RNA UMIs per nucleus in CTCF-depleted cells (Fig. 1, B and C). To directly quantify per-cell transcriptional output, we performed in situ reverse transcription followed by flow cytometry (Fig. 2F). Nuclear RNA was reverse transcribed with FAM-labeled primers, and fluorescence intensities were measured for individual nuclei. This single-nucleus assay revealed that CTCF-depleted nuclei exhibited ∼75% of the RNA-derived fluorescence signal of control cells (P < 2.2 × 10−16, Wilcoxon rank sum test) (Fig. 2, G and H), indicating a global reduction in transcriptional activity. To exclude potential off-target effects of auxin treatment, we performed control experiments in wild-type (WT) mESCs lacking the AID tag. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) treatment alone did not affect global transcriptional output in WT mESCs (fig. S6, E and F). Because standard RNA-seq normalization assumes constant total RNA levels and would obscure such changes, we performed cell number–normalized RNA-seq on both control and CTCF-depleted cells using Drosophila RNA spike-ins (29–31). After normalization, CTCF-depleted samples retained ∼91% of the transcriptional output of control cells (Fig. 2I), matching the flow cytometry measurements. Together, these findings demonstrate that per-cell transcriptional capacity is reduced following CTCF depletion and is accompanied by weakened A compartment coherence.


### Identification of higher-order TLD assemblies by SALTAFinder in single cells
  Previous studies have suggested that TADs can assemble into large-scale chromatin hubs through long-range interactions over megabase distances (32, 33). To systematically identify such higher-order structures at single-cell resolution, we developed SALTAFinder, a computational framework designed to detect spatially aggregated long-distance TLD assemblies (SALTAs) from single-cell Hi-C data (Fig. 3A). SALTAs represent clusters of distantly located TLDs that occupy shared 3D space, reflecting how linearly remote chromatin regions are positioned in the same spatial neighborhoods at the single-cell level. In brief, each chromosome in individual cells was represented as a weighted contact network in which each 50-kb bin served as a node and edge weights reflected contact frequency. A modularity-based algorithm then partitioned nodes into modules maximizing intramodule connectivity while minimizing intermodule links. All bins belonging to the same TLD were constrained to fall within the same module during SALTA detection, and modules containing at least three TLDs were designated as SALTAs (see Materials and Methods).

  Applying SALTAFinder to control mESCs identified an average of 458 SALTAs per cell, each consisting of ∼12 TLDs (Fig. 3B). SALTAs encompassed ∼8.2 Mb of the cumulative genomic sequence (“SALTA size”) yet spanned ∼35 Mb along chromosomes (“SALTA span”), indicating that discontinuous genomic regions are frequently brought into shared spatial neighborhoods (Fig. 3, C and D). A representative SALTA on paternal chromosome 8 illustrates this: Eight TLDs spanning 114 Mb in linear distance form a compact spatial cluster covering only 6.45 Mb of the cumulative sequence (Fig. 3, E and F). Additional examples from other cells showed similar spatial clustering of distantly located TLDs within individual SALTAs (fig. S7A). Comparing with their distance-matched control, intra-SALTA TLD pairs displayed significantly higher contact frequencies (fig. S7B) and shorter 3D distances (fig. S7C). The spatial compactness of each SALTA, quantified by its radius of gyration (Rg) in 3D models, increased with SALTA size but plateaued beyond ∼25 Mb, suggesting a biophysical upper limit of compaction for large assemblies (fig. S7D). Overall, observed SALTAs showed substantially smaller Rg values than shuffled or shifted controls (P < 2.2 × 10−16; Fig. 3G and fig. S7E), confirming that they represent genuine spatial aggregates, not artifacts of sparse single-cell Hi-C sampling. Comparison with bulk multiway interaction datasets such as GAM and SPRITE (33, 34) provided additional support for the long-range clustering patterns captured by SALTAFinder (fig. S7, F and G). We further assessed the robustness of SALTA identification across different contact numbers per cell and observed that SALTA features remain stable across a broad range of contact numbers (fig. S7, H to L).

  To further characterize SALTA composition and its relationship to transcription, we examined their correspondence with A/B compartments. TLDs within a given SALTA were strongly biased toward the same compartment identity (Fig. 3H), indicating that chromatin regions from the same compartment tend to cluster in shared spatial neighborhoods in single cells. Using an A-score measuring the proportion of compartment A sequence within each SALTA, we observed strong enrichment of both A-SALTAs (A-score > 0.92; 19.1%) and B-SALTAs (A-score < 0.05; 4.1%), relative to the randomized controls (P < 2.2 × 10−16 from the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; Fig. 3I). SALTAs with intermediate A-scores were classified as compartment-neutral SALTAs. As expected, A-SALTAs were significantly enriched for highly expressed genes and super-enhancers (fig. S7, M and N), suggesting that SALTAs can serve as large-scale spatial hubs that cluster active regulatory domains. Collectively, these analyses show that SALTAFinder identifies higher-order spatial groupings of TLDs within single cells, reflecting the tendency of chromatin domains with similar compartment identity to assemble into shared 3D neighborhoods that may relate to transcriptional state.


### Stabilization of highly active SALTAs by CTCF
  Building on the identification of these higher-order TLD groupings (SALTAs), we next examined how CTCF depletion affects their structural properties. We identified SALTAs in CTCF-depleted cells using the same criteria as in untreated controls and compared their global architectural features. CTCF loss led to an overall expansion of SALTAs, reflected by significant increases in their genomic size, chromosomal span, and number of constituent TLDs (fig. S8). The mean SALTA size increased from 8.2 to 10.0 Mb [fold change (FC) = 1.22; P < 2.2 × 10−16; fig. S8A], the mean span increased from 35.0 to 40.4 Mb (FC = 1.15; P < 2.2 × 10−16; fig. S8B), and the number of TLDs per SALTA rose from 12 to 15 (FC = 1.25; P = 2.7 × 10−16; fig. S8C). In parallel, the fraction of A-SALTAs decreased slightly (19.1% → 17.6%) whereas compartment-neutral SALTAs became more prevalent (76.8% → 78.2%) (Fig. 3J). This observation is consistent with the weakened A compartment interactions in earlier analyses (Fig. 2E). Together, these changes indicate that CTCF may contribute to maintain the compactness and compartmental segregation of these higher-order chromatin assemblies.

  To further dissect the chromatin composition of different SALTA types, we clustered all SALTAs from both control and CTCF-depleted cells based on their chromatin states defined by ChromHMM (35) (see Materials and Methods). This classification revealed three major categories (Fig. 4A): (i) active SALTAs (C1 to C3) enriched for promoters, enhancers, super-enhancers, and active histone marks, with C1 showing the strongest transcriptional signatures; (ii) mixed SALTAs (C4 and C5) marked by repressive or mixed chromatin states; and (iii) inactive SALTAs (C6 and C7) enriched for H3K9me3 heterochromatin or quiescent, DNA-methylated regions. Among these, C1 SALTAs exhibited the highest enrichment for super-enhancers and highly expressed genes (FCs of 1.35 and 1.34; Fig. 4, B and C, and fig. S9A) and were smaller and more compact than the other groups (fig. S9, B to D), suggesting that C1 SALTAs may serve as transcriptionally permissive chromatin neighborhoods that facilitate coordinated activation of multiple active genes. C2 and C3 SALTAs displayed similar active chromatin features, with progressively reduced activity relative to C1.

  CTCF depletion caused a marked shift in the overall distribution of SALTA types: The proportion of the most active C1 and C2 SALTAs decreased, whereas C3 to C5 SALTAs—associated with less active or partially repressed chromatin—showed a modest but reproducible increase (Fig. 4D). A representative locus further illustrates this trend: The fraction of SALTAs containing super-enhancer SE#1 that fell into C1/C2 clusters decreased from ∼69.3% in control cells to ∼49.7% after CTCF depletion, accompanied by a corresponding increase in assignments to C3 to C5 clusters (Fig. 4E). This indicates that CTCF helps maintain super-enhancer–containing regions within highly active SALTAs, and its loss promotes the redistribution of these loci into less active chromatin environments.

  We next assessed whether the structural reorganization of SALTAs relates to transcriptional changes. Because the most active SALTAs (C1/C2) are enriched for strongly expressed genes (Fig. 4, A to C), we selected a set of highly expressed genes to examine how their SALTA environments change upon CTCF depletion. Across these genes, reductions in the most active-SALTA occupancy were modestly but significantly correlated with the magnitude of expression change between the control and CTCF-depleted cells (P = 0.017, Pearson correlation; Fig. 4F). These results indicate that genes losing preferential access to active SALTA environments tend to undergo stronger down-regulation, suggesting that the disassembly of active SALTAs contributes to reduced expression of genes that previously resided in these highly active chromatin contexts.

  To determine whether this gene-level trend also manifests at single-cell resolution, we examine a subset of representative genes selected from the genome-wide analysis in Fig. 4F, taking advantage of the paired DNA-RNA measurements provided by HiRES. For each of these genes, we stratified cells according to whether its transcription start site (TSS) resided inside or outside the most active SALTAs (C1/C2) in that nucleus, enabling a direct comparison of the same gene’s expression across distinct 3D chromatin contexts. Across these representative genes, expression was consistently higher in cells where their TSSs were located within C1/C2 SALTAs than in cells where the same TSSs lay outside such assemblies (Fig. 4G). An example is Polr2a, a gene encoding the largest subunit of RNA Polymerase II: In control cells, its TSS frequently localized within C1/C2 SALTAs, whereas upon CTCF depletion it more often shifted to C2/C3 SALTAs (fig. S9E). This redistribution was accompanied by a marked decrease in Polr2a expression across single cells (Fig. 4G), further illustrating at the individual-gene level how relocation out of the most active SALTAs coincides with diminished transcription.

  Together, these findings suggest that CTCF loss weaken the structural environments that support high transcriptional activity. Upon CTCF depletion, the most active SALTAs (C1/C2) expand, lose compactness, and shift toward less active chromatin neighborhoods. Such redistribution of active loci is consistent with the reduced transcriptional capacity observed across single cells, highlighting active SALTAs as the subset of higher-order chromatin assemblies most dependent on CTCF for their integrity.


## DISCUSSION
  CTCF has long been recognized as a central organizer of chromatin topology (36), yet how its loss perturbs genome architecture and transcription within the same cell has remained unclear. By leveraging HiRES (26) to jointly profile chromatin contacts and RNA from individual mESCs after CTCF depletion, we characterized how the absence of CTCF affects genome organization and transcriptional output at the single-cell level. This multiomic, paired design enables a direct, within-cell correlation between 3D genome reorganization and transcriptional capacity.

  At the bulk level, CTCF loss produces the expected signatures of TAD boundary weakening and loss of CTCF-anchored loops (11). Our single-cell analyses refine this view by quantifying, genome-wide, how bulk TAD features arise from probabilistic TLDs. Using over a thousand alleles with full genomic coverage, we calculated absolute TLD boundary frequencies and showed that genomic bins forming bulk TAD boundaries have the highest local boundary probabilities, despite modest absolute usage (Fig. 1K and fig. S3B). We note that, because of the limited contact numbers inherent to single-cell data, the frequency of TLD boundary occurrence at bulk TAD boundaries is likely underestimated and should therefore be interpreted as a conservative estimate. We further demonstrated a quantitative relationship between TAD insulation strength and TLD frequency, linking single-cell variability and aggregated bulk patterns. Upon CTCF depletion, TLD boundaries persist but their positional probabilities redistribute—decreasing at former CTCF sites and increasing in neighboring regions (Fig. 1K)—indicating that weakened bulk TADs largely reflects reduced TLD boundary alignment across cells, rather than domain loss within individual nuclei.

  Despite largely preserved A/B compartment identity, CTCF loss altered their internal interaction patterns. We observed reduced interactions within A compartments and a slight increase within B compartments (Fig. 2E), indicating a relaxation of active chromatin neighborhoods. These structural changes are accompanied by changes in transcriptional capacity, consistent with the enrichment of active genes and regulatory elements in A compartments. HiRES single-cell RNA measurements indicated a reduction in per-cell RNA abundance, and this trend was confirmed by two orthogonal assays capable of capturing global RNA shifts (Fig. 2, F to I). Together, these results indicate that although gene-level differential expression remains limited, CTCF depletion is associated with a genome-wide reduction in per-cell transcriptional capacity that coincides with weakened A compartment connectivity. The causal relationship between these changes remains unresolved, and it is possible that alterations in chromatin organization and transcriptional activity influence each other.

  To identify which higher-order chromatin structures are most affected by CTCF loss, we developed SALTAFinder, a framework for detecting SALTAs from single-cell Hi-C data. SALTAs represent higher-order spatial groupings of distantly located TLDs that share 3D neighborhoods and often exhibit coherent compartment identity, capturing a finer-scale and more dynamic layer of genome organization beyond classical compartmentalization (Fig. 3, H and I). In control cells, a subset of SALTAs—corresponding to active SALTAs (C1 to C3)—is enriched for A compartment sequence, super-enhancers, and highly expressed genes, forming compact chromatin clusters that appear permissive for transcriptional activity (Fig. 4, A to C). Consistent with multicontact datasets such as GAM and SPRITE (33, 34), together with polymer modeling, supports SALTAs as bona fide spatial aggregates (Fig. 3G and fig. S7, F and G).

  CTCF depletion disrupted these assemblies in multiple ways: SALTAs increased in size, spanned greater genomic distances, and shifted toward mixed or less active chromatin states (fig. S8). The most active SALTAs (C1 and C2) decreased in prevalence, whereas mixed SALTAs (C4 and C5) increased (Fig. 4D). Because HiRES provides paired DNA-RNA information from the same cell, we could directly link transcriptional output to SALTA environments: Genes whose TSSs lost access to active SALTAs tended to exhibit greater expression decreases (Fig. 4F), and representative loci further showed higher transcription in cells where their TSS resided within active SALTAs (Fig. 4G). These analyses indicate that destabilization and redistribution of high-activity SALTAs are associated with the genome-wide reduction in per-cell transcriptional capacity observed after CTCF loss.

  The conceptual model in Fig. 5 integrates these findings. In control cells, subsets of A compartment domains frequently assemble into compact, active SALTAs enriched for super-enhancers and highly expressed genes, forming spatial hubs that support robust transcription. Upon CTCF depletion, boundary probabilities redistribute around former CTCF sites, A compartment interactions weaken, and active SALTAs lose both compactness and prevalence as their constituent loci shift into mixed or less active chromatin environments. This coordinated reorganization disperses regulatory hubs and reduces opportunities for multilocus colocalization, which is consistent with the observed global decline in per-cell transcriptional capacity.

  Beyond local domain effects, these results suggest that CTCF contributes to maintaining the integrity of active SALTAs, the higher-order assemblies of active chromatin identified in this study. Earlier studies proposed long-range CTCF-mediated interactions through phase separation–related mechanisms (37–39), and our single-cell multiomic data provide genome-wide evidence that CTCF loss is associated with destabilization of spatial assemblies of active domains, in parallel with reduced transcriptional capacity. The mechanisms by which CTCF influences higher-order chromatin organization and transcriptional capacity remain unclear. One possibility is that CTCF directly contributes to the maintenance of long-range active chromatin clusters through its role in organizing local chromatin architecture, such as stabilizing TLD boundaries and local chromatin insulation. Disruption of this structural organization may, in turn, impair the assembly of active SALTAs and subsequently affect transcriptional capacity. Alternatively, CTCF may influence transcriptional output through mechanisms that are not solely dependent on its role in 3D genome organization, and reduced transcriptional activity may, in turn, contribute to the destabilization of these assemblies, indicating that SALTA organization could also be influenced by transcription-dependent mechanisms. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive and may act in combination. This raises the possibility that similar mechanisms may influence differentiation phenotypes reported in CTCF-deficient mESCs (15–17, 40) and embryos (25).

  Future studies will be needed to elucidate the causal relationship between transcriptional decline and A compartment disassembly and determine whether similar phenomenon occurs in differentiated cells or during development. Perturbations of cohesin regulators (e.g., NIPBL and WAPL) may help separate boundary alignment effects from extrusion-independent mechanisms. Last, integrating additional single-cell modalities, such as chromatin modifications (41–45), accessibility (46–49), or nascent transcription (50–52), will refine SALTA state definitions and help distinguish structural from kinetic contributions to RNA reduction. In addition, as current SALTA analysis is restricted to intrachromosomal TLDs, extending this framework to investigate interchromosomal TLD clustering in 3D space represents an interesting future direction, although this will likely require higher-coverage single-cell datasets.


## MATERIALS AND METHODS

### Cell lines
  The F1 Mus musculus castaneus × S129/SvJae CTCF-AID knock-in mESC clones (XY; F123 cells) were gifts from B. Ren (University of California San Diego School of Medicine). ESCs were cultured in KnockOut Serum Replacement (KSR) containing an mESC medium: 85% Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Corning, 10013-CV), 15% KSR (Gibco, 10828028), penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122), 1× nonessential amino acids (Gibco, 11140-050), 1× GlutaMAX (Gibco, 35050), LIF (1000 U/ml; Millipore, ESG1107), and 0.4 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M3148). The WT R1 mESCs were kindly provided by Y. Zhang (Fudan University, China.). R1 cells were cultured in same media as F123 cells, except that fetal bovine serum (FBS; SE200-ES, ES Cells Qualified) was used instead of KSR, and the medium was additionally supplemented with 1× nucleosides (Millipore, ES-008-D). Cells were grown on 0.2% gelatin-coated plates. Cells were maintained by passaging using Accutase (Gibco, 00-4555-56) on 0.2% gelatin-coated dishes at 37°C with 5% CO2. The medium was changed daily when cells were not passaged. Cells were checked for mycoplasma infection and tested negative.


### Auxin treatment of mESCs
  The CTCF-AID knock-in mESCs expressing TIR1-9myc and WT R1 mESCs were passaged on 0.2% gelatin-coated plates without MEFs. We added 1 μl of 500 mM auxin (Abcam, ab146403) per 1 ml of medium to deplete CTCF and changed the medium with auxin every 24 hours. Cells were collected 48 hours after auxin treatment began.


### Antibodies
  Antibodies used in this study were mouse anti-CTCF (Santa Cruz, sc-271474), rabbit anti-histone H3 (Proteintech, 17168-1-AP), for Western blotting. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H+L) (Proteintech, SA00001-2) and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG(H+L) (Proteintech, SA00001-1) were used as secondary antibodies for Western blotting.


### Western blotting
  Cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and scraped in cold PBS and pelleted to be stored at −80°C. One million cells were resuspended in 100 μl of 1× SDS loading buffer. Each sample was incubated for 5 min at 95°C and run on 4 to 20% gradient SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels for 70 min at 130 V and then transferred onto preactivated PVDF (polyvinylidene difluoride) membranes at 15 V for 20 min by Semi-Dry Transfer Cell (Bio-Rad). The membranes were rinsed with 1× tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) and blocked with 5% dry milk at room temperature for 60 min. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with diluted antibody in the blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. After overnight incubating, membranes were washed three times 5 min in 1× TBST at room temperature and incubated with secondary antibody in blocking buffer at room temperature for 60 min. After washing three times with TBST, the substrates were detected using the Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).


### HiRES library preparation and sequencing

#### Cell fixation, reverse transcription, digestion, and single-cell sorting
  CTCF-AID mESCs treated with auxin for 48 hours were collected for the HiRES experiment as described with minor modification. In brief, after dissociation with Accutase, cells were centrifuged at 300g for 5 min and resuspended in 300 μl of Nuclei Storage Buffer (HiRES), counted by an Automated Cell Counter (Bio-Rad, TC20). Approximately 300k cells were transferred to a new tube and resuspended in 500 μl of Nuclei Storage Buffer, 61.4 μl of 16% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 28908) was added, and the tubes were rotated at room temperature for 10 min. The fixation was quenched by adding 50 μl of 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA). All centrifugation after fixation were performed at 1100g for 5 min at 4°C. Fixed cells were washed with 200 μl of Wash Buffer (HiRES), incubated in 200 μl of Hi-C Lysis Buffer (HiRES) on ice for 30 min, further permeabilized with 100 μl of 0.2% SDS at 65°C for 10 min, immediately cooled on ice for 3 min, and quenched by 100 μl of 5% Triton X-100. Nuclei pellets after centrifugation were resuspended with 100 μl of Reverse Transcription Buffer (HiRES) with 10 μl of SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 18090010) thoroughly. Digestion and ligation were performed as described except that 25 μl of T4 ligase (NEB, M0202L) was used for proximal ligation. After ligation, cells were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1 μg/ml; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 62248) for 15 min, and single nuclei were fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) sorted into 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AB1400L) with a Sony Cell Sorter MA900 in single-cell mode. We sorted 2n nuclei based on the DAPI signal.


#### Single-cell amplification, library construction, and sequencing
  Single nuclei in 96-well plates were lysed and amplified as the HiRES paper (26) described. The amplification product was diluted, tagmentated, and index amplified as the HiRES paper (26) described. The barcoded single-cell libraries from same plates were then pooled and purified with 0.6× and 0.15× DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411) for two rounds. The final libraries were sequenced with paired-end 150–base pair (bp) reads on a DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI) platform. We sequenced 384 cells per lane, generated 3-6 Gb of data for each single cell.


### In situ fluorescent reverse transcription with flow cell recording
  CTCF-AID mESCs or R1 mESCs treated with or without auxin for 48 hours were collected for transcription quantification. After dissociation by Accutase and supernatant removal, cells were resuspended at 1M cells per 300 μl of Nuclei Isolation Buffer [5% BSA, 0.1% CA-630, 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, SUPERase·In (0.5 U/μl), and RNaseOUT (0.5 U/μl)] and incubated on ice for 5 min and then washed twice with ice-cold 5% BSA/PBS. A total of 300k nuclei were resuspended in 100 μl of Reverse Transcription Buffer [20 μl of 5x SSIV buffer, 20 μl of 5 M betaine, 5 μl of 10 mM deoxynucleoside triphosphate, 5 μl of 50 μM 5′FAM-GAT5-RT primer (same sequence as the RT primer in HiRES, with FAM labeled on the 5′ end), 5 μl of 100 mM dithiothreitol, 1 μl of 100 mM MgSO4, 2 μl of SUPERase·In, 2 μl of RNaseOUT, and 10 μl of SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase]. The reverse transcription was performed in a thermocycler with the following program: Step 1: 50°C for 10 min; Step 2: 32°C for 1 min, 42°C for 2 min, and 50°C for 2 min; repeat step 2 for additional four cycles. After the reaction, the solution was mixed with 1 ml of 5% BSA/PBS, nuclei were spun down at 500g for 5 min at 4°C, then resuspended with 500 μl of ice-cold 5% BSA/PBS with DAPI (1 μg/ml), and filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer and analyzed by a Sony Cell Sorter MA900.


### RNA-seq with spike-in

#### Sample preparation, spike-in addition, and RNA extraction
  CTCF-AID mESCs were seeded in a 6-cm dish and treated with or without auxin for 48 hours. After dissociation by Accutase and supernatant removal, cell pellets were resuspended in 0.5 ml of 5% BSA/PBS with DAPI (1 μg/ml). Single cells were gated, and live (DAPI−) cells were sorted in purity mode by a Sony Cell Sorter MA900. A total of nine independent replicates (n = 9), each derived from separately cultured wells and processed independently, were analyzed. For each sample replication, we precisely obtained 300,000 live cells from an individual 6-well dish. After sorting, cells were spun down at 500g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded until ∼50 μl remained to ensure that the pellets were not aspirated. One milliliter of TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596026), 10 ng of total RNA from S2 cells, and 200 μl of chloroform were added to each tube and thoroughly mixed by vortexing for 5 min. Tubes were centrifuged at 12,000g for 15 min at 4°C, and the aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube with 800 μl of isopropanol and mixed by shaking. Samples were incubated at −20°C for 3 hours and centrifuged at 20,000g for 15 min at 4°C, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was washed with ice-cold 75% ethanol once. The supernatant was discarded carefully, and RNA pellets were air dried for 5 min. RNA was dissolved in 20 μl of nuclease-free water. RNA concentration was measured by the Equalbit RNA HS Assay Kit (Vazyme, EQ211).


#### Library preparation and sequencing
  One microgram of total RNA from each sample was used for RNA library construction following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, mRNAs were captured by mRNA capture beads (Vazyme, N403) and fragmented by incubation at 94°C for 8 min, and first and second strands were synthesized following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA adapters (3.5 μl; Vazyme, N803) were used for ligation, and 12 rounds of indexed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) were performed. The RNA libraries were quantified by the Equalbit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Vazyme, EQ121). Each RNA library was sequenced for 6 Gb in paired-end 150-bp mode on a NovaSeq 6000 platform.


### HiRES data processing
  HiRES pipeline (https://github.com/skelviper/HiRES) was performed as previously described (26) using the mouse mm10 reference genome as well as the vM24 Gencode annotation file downloaded from GENCODE (https://gencodegenes.org/mouse/). The contacts after three rounds of cleaning (i.e., the c123 contacts) were used as the final clean contact lists for downstream analysis. Contacts from maternal chromosome and contacts from paternal chromosome were further distinguished before single-cell analysis (domain, compartment, and SALTA), by providing single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) information of CAST_EiJ and 129S1_SvImJ to the pipeline (23). The SNP information was obtained from the Sanger Institute Mouse Genomes Project (ftp://ftpmouse.sanger.ac.uk/REL-1505-SNPs_Indels/mgp.v5.merged.snps_all.dbSNP142.vcf.gz).

  To estimate the quality of each single cell, we calculated two RNA metrics and three DNA metrics:

  1) nFeature RNA: number of unique genes detected in the cell.

  2) % mitochondria RNA: percent of reads originating from the mitochondria genes to those from all genes.

  3) Clean contacts: total number of clean contacts.

  4) Long-range ratio: the ratio of long-range (≥20k) cis-contacts to all clean contacts.

  5) Clean contacts per G: the number of clean contacts per billion sequencing bases.

  Quality information about each single cell is available in table S1.

  For QC, the following filters were applied to select high-quality cells: (i) nFeature RNA > 500, (ii) % mitochondrial RNA ≤ 5, (iii) clean contacts ≥ 100,000, (iv) long-range ratio > 0.4, and (v) clean contacts per G ≥ 20,000. Pre-M and post-M cells identified using previously published methods (53) were further removed.


### Unsupervised cell clustering
  For RNA data, Seurat (v5.2.1) was used for dimensionality reduction and clustering of RNA data (54). Briefly, the raw RNA count matrix was normalized using the NormalizeData function. The top 2000 variable features were selected and scaled by the ScaleData function using vars.to.regress = “batch” for batch-effect removal. The first 30 principal components (PC) were calculated using RunPCA, based on which the UMAP reduction was applied with default parameters.

  For DNA contact data, the clean contact files of the 668 pass-QC cells were imputed using schicluster (1.3.5) (55) under a 50-kb resolution with default parameters. UMAP was applied on the top 15 embedding vectors from schicluster using n.neighbors = 50.


### Pseudobulk RNA analysis
  The pseudobulk RNA count matrix was created by pooling the read counts of cells from each plate, which means each plate was used as per replicate.

  The raw read count was normalized by DESeq2 (55), and the variance-stabilizing transformed normalized counts calculated by DESeq2 were used for principal components analysis (PCA) visualization. The batch effect was adjusted during differentially expressed gene identification using DESeq2 by setting design = ∼treatment + batch. Up-regulated (or down-regulated) genes were defined as those with FC > 1.5 (or FC < 2/3) and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was applied to the up-regulated and down-regulated genes using the enrichGO function from the R clusterProfiler (v4.10.0) (56) package using the org.Mm.eg.db (v3.18.0) database.


### ChIP-seq analysis
  All ChIP-seq data (CTCF, Rad21, H3K4me3, H3K4me1, and H3K27ac) used in this study were obtained from a previous study (15) in which the cells were of the same genetic background and cultured under the same conditions as the cells used in this study. The Raw FASTQs were all downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with accession number GSE94452 and reanalyzed using the ENCODE ChIP-seq pipeline (https://github.com/ENCODE-DCC/chip-seq-pipeline2) (v2.2.1) with default parameters for histone modification ChIP-seq using the mouse mm10 reference genome.


### Pseudobulk Hi-C analysis

#### Ensembled contact frequency matrix
  To calculate the pseudobulk contact frequency map, clean contacts from cells of each treatment were pooled, obtaining 128,354,123 and 115,506,794 contacts for the control and CTCF-depleted cells, respectively. Then, 115,506,794 contacts were randomly selected for the control group to remove the sequencing depth effect between comparisons.


#### Insulation score and TAD
  For TAD identification, the insulation scores were calculated by cooltools (v0.7.0) (57) under a 10-kb resolution and a 100-kb window size. Boundaries were identified as local minima and further filtered by a boundary strength of >0.5. Regions between adjacent boundaries larger than 150 kb and less than 3 Mb were assigned as TADs. The ADA plot was created by fanc aggregate using -e -r 1.0 --rescale. The heatmap and enrichment profiles of the insulation scores, chromatin protein binding (for example, CTCF and Rad21), and histone modification around the pseudobulk TAD boundaries were provided by the computeMatrix followed by plotHeatmap or plotProfile from deepTools (v3.5.1) (58).

  For boundary comparison across the treatment, boundaries identified from the two treatments were pooled together, and boundaries within 50 kb were merged into one so that an up to 50-kb shift during boundary identification was tolerated. The merged boundaries detected in both treatments, in control only, and in CTCF-depleted cells only were named shared, loss, and emerged boundaries, respectively.


#### A/B compartment
  Compartment scores were calculated using eigenvector from juicer-tools (v1.22.01) (https://github.com/aidenlab/JuicerTools) under a 100-kb resolution. The sign of the original PC1 value was corrected on the basis of the GC content to ensure that a positive compartment score always referred to the A compartment and, otherwise, a negative score always referred to the B compartment.

  Loci with significant compartment switch were identified by cLoops2 using compareCom.py under Mahalanobis distance P value < 0.01. For integrative analysis of compartment switch and gene expression, expressed genes (defined as genes with more than 10 read counts in at least one treatment) were located into the 100-kb bin according to their promoter coordination annotated by GENCODE mm10 vM21.

  To compare compartment strength between the treatments, saddle plots were provided by the saddle function followed by the saddle plot function from cooltools (v.0.7.0) (57) according to the official guidance. To evaluate the compartment strength of published dataset, the mcool (12, 17) (Nora et al. and Hsieh et al.)/HiC files were downloaded from the GEO with accession numbers GSE98671 (12) (Nora et al.)/GSE94452 (15) (Kubo et al.)/GSE178982 (17) (Hsieh et al.), based on which the compartment scores and compartment strength were calculated as described above.

  To correlate compartment switching with changes in RNA expression, genes were assigned into 100-kb bins according to the location of their TSS. To more accurately define the TSS used by mESCs, only those located inside H3K4me3 peaks were considered. For genes with multiple H3K4me3-marked TSS, only the one with the highest H3K27ac signal within ±1-kb region were considered.


### Single-cell DNA analysis

#### Single-cell domain analysis by Higashi
  Higashi was applied to haplotype-phase imputed contacts on the autosomes of 668 pass-QC cells under a 50-kb resolution with default parameters. Then, scTAD.py was used to identify single-cell TLD using --window_ins 500000 --window_tad 500000. TADs of less than 150 kb or larger than 3 Mb were discarded. The ADA plot was created by fanc aggregate using -e -r 1.0 --rescale. The relative TLD boundary frequency (fb) for each 50-kb bin was calculated as the fraction of alleles in which the bin was a TLD boundary.

  To control for the differences in sequencing depth, sequencing depth was normalized by ranking and pairing WT and CTCF-depleted cells according to their total contact number, followed by subsampling contacts to the lower value within each pair. Because of the unequal group sizes (324 and 344 cells), the top 324 cells from each group were selected to generated 324 depth-matched pairs for subsequent analysis. Single-cell TLDs were reidentified from the 648 depth-matched cells by Higashi following the same parameters and procedures described above.


#### Single-cell domain analysis by DeDoc2
  Schicluster (59) was applied to haplotype-phase imputed contacts on the autosomes of 668 pass-QC cells under a 50-kb resolution with default parameters. Then, the DeDoc2 (28) java software was used to identify single-cell TLD using --windowsize 8mb --binsize 50 kb. TADs of less than 150 kb were discarded. The relative TLD boundary frequency (fb) for each 50-kb bin was calculated as the fraction of alleles in which the bin was a TLD boundary.


#### Single-cell compartment
  Compartment scores were calculated from the Higashi-imputed contact heatmap by scCompartmenty.py from Higashi under a 100-kb resolution. Bins with significantly differential compartment scores between treatments were identified using the Wilcoxon test with FDR < 0.05. Delta scCompartment scores were calculated as the difference between the median of the scCompartment scores of the two treatments.


#### Single-cell loop
  The SnapHiC (60) pipeline (https://github.com/HuMingLab/SnapHiC) was used to identify loops from HiRES DNA contacts in a 10-kb resolution with default parameters for the control and CTCF-depleted cells, respectively. Aggregated peak analysis (APA) was then applied on the identified loops by the apa-analysis command provided by HiCPeaks (https://github.com/XiaoTaoWang/HiCPeaks).


### Spiked-in RNA-seq analysis
  Both genome sequence files and the gene annotation files for mouse (mm10) and Drosophila (dm6) were concatenated to create a combined reference genome. Raw sequencing reads with adapter trimmed by cutadapt (61) (v4.1) were first aligned to the combined reference genome by STAR (62) (v2.7.10a). The uniquely mapped reads were then quantified by RSEM (63) (v1.3.3).

  The number of reads successfully assigned to mouse (endogenous genome) or Drosophila (spiked-in genome) gene exons was counted respectively to calculate the number of endogenous reads per million spiked-in, defined asReads assigned to mouse genesReads assigned to Drosophila genes×1,000,000


### SALTA identification
  The contact map on each haplotype-phased chromosome was binned with 50-kb bin and can be then interpreted as a weighted network G=(V,E), where V={vi} represents the set of vertices (the 50-kb bins), E={ei=(vi,vj)} represents the set of edges (the contacts between two 50-kb bins), and the contact frequency matrix M under the same resolution was directly used as the adjacent matrix W. By given a contact network G of a chromosome and the corresponding list of TLDs on the chromosome, the purpose is to seek an optimal division of the graph to maximize the modularity while keeping the integrity of the TLD, which means that the resolved division must satisfy that all vertices from a same TLD must belong to a same group.

  According to Newman et al. (64), given a network G and its division, the modularity was calculated asQ=∑i(eii−ai2)in which for a given group i and a group j, let eij+eji be the fraction of edges that connect between a vertex in group i and a vertex in group j among all edges. By analogy, eii is the fraction of edges that connect two vertices within the group i and ∑ieii is thus the total fraction within group i. As a penalty, ai=∑ieij is the fraction of the ends of edges connected to the vertices in group i and thus ai2 corresponds to estimated fraction of edges within group i at random. Therefore, Q = 0 means that the division gives no more within-community edges than would be expected by random and the maximum value that Q can achieve is 1, which means that all the edges in the graph are within-community edges.

  To optimize the objective function Q, we used a modified version of the fast-greedy algorithm (also known as the Clauset-Newman-Moore or CNM algorithm) (64). Initially, we started with a state that there are m groups in the graph, where m is the number of TLDs on the chromosome. Then, a pair of groups was selected to be joined together for a greatest increase (or smallest decrease) in Q. The joining step was repeated until all the vertices belong to the same group, and a hierarchical dendrogram tree was formed during this process. Last, the tree was cut at the level when the Q is at its maximum value across the tree.

  The output of the modified fast-greedy algorithm is to divide the vertices (or the 50-kb bin) of the chromosome into several groups. A SALTA is such a group that contain at least two TLDs. To further remove low-quality SALTAs, we removed SALTAs whose clustering coefficient is 0, in which, according to the graph theory, the clustering coefficient of a SALTA is the average of the local clustering coefficients of the vertices in the SALTA, calculated asCavg=1n∑i=1nCiwhereas the local clustering coefficient of a vertex i is to measure the tendency of nodes to form triangles defined asCi=Number of triangles involving node iMaximum possible triangles=2Tiki(ki−1)in which Ti is the number of edges between the neighbors of i and ki is the degree of the vertex i.

  Only SALTA containing at least three interacting TLDs are retained for further analysis, ensuring that the identified structures represent multidomain interaction clusters rather than isolated domain-domain contacts.


### Spatial analysis of SALTAs
  The 3D genome structures for each single cell were generated under a 20- and 50-kb resolution by hickit as previously described (26). For each single cell, five replicated structures with different random seed were constructed and the median root mean square deviation (RMSD) for each combination of three replicates was calculated. Only cells with at least one combination of RMSD < 1.5 were included. For these cells, the first replicate in the combination with the minimum median RMSD value was used as the representative for further analysis.

  The spatial distance of two loci in a single cell was measured as the Euclidean distanced=(x2−x1)2+(y2−y1)2+(z2−z1)2in which (x1,y1,z1) and (x2,y2,z2) correspond to the coordinate of locus1 and locus2 in the constructed structure of the cell.

  The Rg of a given SATLA composed of a single cell was defined as the average distance for each particle (i.e., 50-kb bin) to their centroidRg=1n∑i=1n(xi−x¯)2+(yi−y¯)2+(zi−z¯)2in which n is the number of the particle, and (x¯,y¯,z¯) is the centroid of the SALTA calculated asx¯=1n∑i=1nxiy¯=1n∑i=1nyiz¯=1n∑i=1nzi

  The constructed 3D models were visualized by pymol (v3.1; https://pymol.org/) using the CIF file generated by dip-c color (https://github.com/tanlongzhi/dip-c).


### Downsampling analysis for evaluating the robustness of SALTAFinder
  To assess the robustness of SALTAFinder under various contact sparsity, downsampling analyses were performed on 20 high-QC cells. The contacts of each selected cell were subsampled to 10,000, 50,000, 100,000, 150,000, 200,000, 300,000, or 400,000 contacts per cell. SALTAs were then reidentified from the downsampled dataset using the same parameters described above.

  To evaluate the consistency of SALTA identification, two clustering similarity metrics were computed between SALTAs derived from the original and downsampled datasets:

  (i) Adjusted Rand index (65)

  The adjusted Rand index (ARI) can be used to measure the consistency between partition assignments, which ranges from −1 to 1, where 0 indicates random labeling, any positive value denotes better-than-random agreement, and values exceeding 0.6 typically represent good clustering similarity. The ARI is calculated using the following formulaARI=RI−E[RI]max(RI)−E[RI]where RI is the Rand index, which reflects the proportion of agreeing and disagreeing pairs between two groupings, E[RI] is the expected Rand index under random assignment, and max(RI) is the theoretical maximum value of the Rand index. By correcting for randomness, the ARI provides a more robust measure of true agreement between two groupings.

  Specifically, for each individual cell, the group of a bin is denoted by the SALTA it belongs to, and only bins that are successfully assigned to a SALTA in both the original and downsampled dataset were considered. Then, the ARI was calculated between the SALTA assignment in the original and the downsampled dataset by the adjustedRandIndex function from the R mclust package.

  (ii) Weighted similarity (66)

  The weighted similarity (WS) scores between two SALTA partition assignments were calculated asWS=∑iwisi∑iwiwhere i is the ith SALTA in the original dataset, wi is the size (i.e., total base pair) of the ith SALTA, and si is the Jaccard index between the ith SALTA from the original dataset and the jth SALTA from the downsampled dataset that share the most similarity. The Jaccard index was calculated as the number of the overlapped bases divided by the number of union bases between the two SALTAssi=max(∣SALTA(O,i)∩SALTA(D,j)∣∣SALTA(O,i)∪SALTA(D,j)∣)

  The WS ranges from 0 to 1. Normally, a WS score above 0.75 represents high similarity and values between 0.5 and 0.75 indicate moderate-to-good overlap.


### Unsupervised clustering of SALTAs
  The ChromHMM 18-state model of the mESC (35, 67) was downloaded from the ENCODE Data Portal (https://encodeproject.org/) with accession number ENCFF611GSQ. The 18 states were first combined into 12 states by grouping similar states into one. To be specific, the “Tx” and “Tx weak” regions were combined as “Tx region,” the “ReprPC” and “ReprPC weak” regions were combined as “ReprPC,” and the “QuiesG,” “Quies,” “Quies2,” “Quies3,” and “Quies4” regions were combined as “Quies.” For each SALTA, the fraction of chromatin bases belonging to each combined state was calculated. Then, all the SALTAs including those from either control cells or CTCF-depleted cells were pooled and clustered by K-means based on this fraction. The optimal number of clusters was determined using indices such as the within-cluster sum of squares (WSS).


### A-score of a SALTA
  The A-score of a SALTA is calculated as the fraction of bases that overlap with the A compartment identified from the pseudobulk compartment analysis to the total bases that overlap with either A or B compartment. Regions failed in compartment division were ignored in this analysis. On the basis of the A-score, SALTAs were further categorized into A-SALTAs (A-score > 0.92), B-SALTAs (A-score < 0.01), and mixed SALTAs (the remaining).


### Comparing SALTA with published datasets
  The list of interacting domain triplets in mESCs identified by GAM (33) were downloaded from the SPRITE paper (34) (table S1). The list of interaction triplet identified from SPRITE was downloaded from the SPRITE paper (34) (table S1). Only 11,097 interacting domain triplets identified by GAM that are also identified as interaction triplet or enriched k-mers by SRPITE (34) were considered. These domain triplets were further divided into five groups based on their genomic span, which was calculated as the distance between the leftmost and rightmost domains. For each SALTA, a corresponding control was permuted by maintaining the original spacing between single-cell TLD boundaries while randomly shifting their start positions on the same chromosome. For each published domain triplet, we counted fraction of the haplotype-phased alleles in which all of the three domains in the triplet overlap with TLDs within a same SALTA (or its corresponding control).


### Feature enrichment analysis of SALTAs

#### Enrichment of super-enhancers per SALTA
  Super-enhancers were identified from the published H3K27ac ChIP-seq described above using the ROSE software (v0.1) (68) with –tss 1500. For each SALTA, a corresponding control was generated by randomly selecting regions of the same size located outside the SALTA. For each of the observed SALTA and its control, the number of super-enhancers overlapping with the SALTA (at least 1 bp) or the control regions was counted. The fold enrichment of overlapping super-enhancers within each SALTA relative to its matched control regions was then calculated.


#### Enrichment of highly expressed genes per SALTA
  For the control cells, the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of exon per million mapped fragments) of each gene was calculated from the pseudobulk gene expression profiles from HiRES RNA data. Expressed genes were defined as those whose FPKM > 1 in control cells and the top 10% of the expressed genes were considered as highly expressed genes. Genes were assigned into 50-kb bins according to their TSS coordination using the method as described in the above section. For each SALTA, a corresponding control was generated by randomly selecting regions of the same size located outside the SALTA. For each of the SALTA and its control, the number of highly expressed genes TSS overlapping was counted. The fold enrichment of overlapping highly expressed genes within each SALTA relative to its matched control regions was then calculated.
